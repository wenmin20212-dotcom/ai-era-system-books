# 8.1 Maxwell's Equations and Radio Propagation: How Antenna Slots Excite Electromagnetic Waves

Turn your phone over and look at the thin plastic slits running across its metal frame. As Chapter 4 explained, these are "windows" filled in by nano-molding so that electromagnetic waves can escape the metal body. One question was left unanswered, though. The two sides of each slit simply carry an alternating current. Why does that send invisible energy out of the phone and across hundreds of meters to a base station? This section starts from that slit to explain the physics behind wireless communication. It then explains why base stations have become ever denser in the 5G era.

## 8.1.1 From Current in a Wire to a Wave in the Air

### 8.1.1.1 A Familiar Phenomenon

High school physics includes two classic experiments. A compass needle placed beside a current-carrying wire is deflected, so electricity produces magnetism. A magnet pushed into a coil induces a current in the coil, so magnetism produces electricity. In the 19th century, the British physicist James Clerk Maxwell organized the laws of electricity and magnetism into a set of equations, later known as **Maxwell's Equations**. Two of them matter most for radio:

- **A changing electric field induces a magnetic field**;
- **A changing magnetic field induces an electric field**.

Neither field needs a wire to carry it. Both can exist in air, and even in a vacuum. Their field lines form closed loops that join end to end, so they are called "curl fields."

### 8.1.1.2 A Relay Between Two Fields

Taken together, these two laws lead to a striking result. A changing electric field produces a magnetic field, and the changing magnetic field produces an electric field. That new electric field then produces a new magnetic field, and so on. Once the process starts, it sustains itself and keeps pushing outward.

Think of the "wave" that crowds do in a stadium. The spectators never leave their seats. Still, the motion of standing up and sitting down passes from row to row, and a wave travels around the stadium. Electromagnetic waves behave the same way. At each point, the electric and magnetic fields only oscillate in place, yet their mutual relay carries energy far away. From his equations, Maxwell calculated that this wave travels at exactly the speed of light, and he concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. Phone signals, Wi-Fi, visible light, and X-rays are all electromagnetic waves. The only difference among them is frequency.

## 8.1.2 Inside the Phone, in an Instant

Apply these principles to a phone, and a single signal transmission goes roughly through the following steps:

1. **The power amplifier (PA) outputs an alternating current.** As Chapter 5 explained, a data packet is modulated and sent to the RF front end, where the power amplifier boosts it into a strong high-frequency current. In the 3.5 GHz band commonly used for 5G, this current reverses direction about 3.5 billion times per second.
2. **The current surges into the antenna radiator.** The current passes through the feed point into an antenna segment marked out on the metal frame. Charges on both sides of the slit surge back and forth with the current, and the electric field across the slit changes rapidly.
3. **The electric and magnetic fields excite each other.** The rapidly changing electric field induces a curl magnetic field around it. The changing magnetic field in turn induces a curl electric field, and the two fields keep generating each other.
4. **An energy wave packet leaves the phone.** When the oscillation frequency is high enough, the field changes cannot be "pulled back" toward the antenna in time. Instead, they travel outward at the speed of light as a self-sustaining packet of electromagnetic energy, which leaves the phone and spreads toward the base station.

| Stage | Physical entity | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Power amplifier | Outputs a 3.5 GHz alternating current |
| Excitation | Antenna slit and metal radiator | Charges move back and forth, and the electric field changes sharply |
| Relay | Space around the phone body | Electric and magnetic fields excite each other |
| Radiation | Air and free space | A self-sustaining wave packet travels at the speed of light |

The slit sizes and the lengths of the metal segments are carefully designed. An antenna's resonant length is usually proportional to the wavelength. That is why engineers must precisely "cut" antenna segments of different lengths into the frame. Each frequency band has its own "pitch," and each metal segment handles part of the range.

## 8.1.3 Why Higher Frequencies Make Signals "Weaker"

### 8.1.3.1 What the Friis Transmission Formula Says

After leaving the phone, the electromagnetic wave has a long way to travel. The classic formula for power loss along this path is the **Friis Transmission Formula**. One of its key conclusions is this: with the antenna gains at both ends and the distance held constant, **the power received is inversely proportional to the square of the carrier frequency**.

This is not intuitive, so here is one way to think about it. The higher the frequency, the shorter the wavelength. Electromagnetic waves always travel at the speed of light, so a 3.5 GHz signal has a wavelength of only about 8 to 9 centimeters. The bands commonly used in the 2G era had much longer wavelengths. At the same gain, a receiving antenna's effective "capture area" is proportional to the square of the wavelength. As the wavelength shrinks, the antenna becomes like a net with a smaller opening. It catches less of the energy spreading through the air.

Think of catching rain. In the same downpour, a washbasin collects far more water than a teacup. The receiving antenna for a high-frequency signal is the teacup.

### 8.1.3.2 The Physical Price of 5G

Apply this law to the evolution of mobile communications, and the conclusion is clear. The main 5G bands have considerably higher frequencies than those of 2G and 3G. Following the frequency-squared relationship, path loss over the same distance is more than ten times greater than in the 2G/3G era. High-frequency signals are also worse at passing through walls and bending around obstacles, which makes indoor coverage and coverage between buildings harder.

This is the **fundamental physical reason why 5G requires many more base stations**. The limit comes from Maxwell's equations and the Friis formula. Neither higher spending by carriers nor better designs from equipment vendors can get around it. When signals fade quickly in the air, there are only two workable approaches:

- **Shorten the distance**: Build base stations more densely so phones are closer to them;
- **Improve the antenna's "focusing" ability**: Use multi-antenna arrays to concentrate energy toward a target direction and make up for part of the loss. Section 8.4 covers this in detail, together with Shannon's theorem and MIMO technology.

Why, then, do carriers still use higher bands? Because higher bands offer more available bandwidth and can carry higher data rates. Smaller coverage in exchange for a higher speed limit is the trade-off 5G makes at the physical level.

## 8.1.4 Summary: The Two Ends Joined by a Slit

The antenna slit in a phone's frame connects two things. At one end is the high-frequency current from the power amplifier. At the other end is the electromagnetic field described by Maxwell's equations. Changing electric and magnetic fields excite each other, which lets energy leave the phone and travel at the speed of light. The Friis formula shows that the losses along the way grow much larger as frequency rises. So when you see a newly installed small cell on a streetlight pole or rooftop, you can link it to those few slits in your phone's frame. Both follow the same set of physical laws.

Radio solves the problem of how information leaves the phone. The next section turns to how information enters it. Photons in front of the camera lens pass through the photoelectric effect and become a set of numbers on the image sensor.
