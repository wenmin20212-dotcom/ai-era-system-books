# 8.4 Shannon Channel Capacity and Multiple Antennas (MIMO): The Information Theory That Pushes Past the Limits of Air

Open a speed-test app in a subway station and your download speed may reach several hundred megabits per second. Step into an elevator and your signal drops to a single bar, and web pages start spinning. Many people assume this is simply the difference between a "strong signal" and a "weak signal": the stronger the signal, the faster the connection. That intuition is only partly right. The maximum amount of data a wireless link can carry is set by a formula written down more than seventy years ago. It draws a ceiling that no communications engineer can cross. This section starts from that formula. It explains why phones don't get faster by "shouting louder." It also shows how the few antennas inside a 5G phone open several non-interfering lanes in the same stretch of air.

## 8.4.1 A Ceiling Defined by One Formula

In 1948, the American mathematician Claude Shannon published the foundational paper of information theory. Its most frequently cited result is known as the **Shannon theorem**, also called the channel capacity formula:

$$C = B \cdot \log_2\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right)$$

The symbols in the formula mean the following:

- **C (Channel Capacity)**: the theoretical maximum number of bits per second that a channel can transmit without error, measured in bit/s.
- **B (Bandwidth)**: the width of the frequency range occupied by the signal, measured in hertz (Hz).
- **S/N (Signal-to-Noise Ratio, often written as SNR)**: the ratio of useful signal power to noise power at the receiver.

Think of a wireless channel as a highway. Bandwidth B is the width of the road, and the signal-to-noise ratio S/N is how clear the driving conditions are. The thicker the fog and the more noise there is, the less willing drivers are to go fast. Shannon proved that no matter how clever the coding scheme, the actual data rate can only approach C and can never exceed it. What engineers can do is find ways to make C itself larger.

## 8.4.2 The "Stinginess" of the Logarithm: Why Not Boost Speed with More Power

The most noteworthy part of the formula is the logarithm, $\log_2$. Because the signal-to-noise ratio sits inside the logarithm, its contribution to capacity grows very slowly.

A bit of simplified arithmetic makes this clearer. Suppose the SNR rises from 1 to 3. The value in the parentheses goes from 2 to 4, $\log_2$ goes from 1 to 2, and capacity doubles. To double it again, the value in the parentheses must go from 4 to 16, so the SNR has to rise from 3 to 15. For the next doubling, the SNR must climb to 255. Each additional share of capacity costs a multiplied amount of power.

This is bad news for phones. The most direct way to raise the SNR is to increase transmit power, but a phone is a sealed, battery-powered device. Every bit of extra output from the power amplifier means more battery drain and more heat. Chapter 5 explained that the power amplifier in the RF front end is already a major heat source, and Chapter 6 explained that the thermal wall forces the system to throttle. Spending large amounts of energy and heat for a small logarithmic gain is a poor trade. Transmit power is also capped by electromagnetic radiation safety standards, so it cannot be raised at will.

By contrast, bandwidth B sits outside the logarithm and has a **linear relationship** with capacity: double the bandwidth, and capacity doubles directly. That is why every generation of mobile communications has fought for wider spectrum, and why techniques such as carrier aggregation stitch multiple scattered pieces of spectrum together.

The routes compare as follows:

| Method | Position in the formula | Effect on capacity | Cost to the phone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase transmit power (raise S/N) | Inside the logarithm | Diminishing returns; gains get harder and harder | Higher power consumption, more heat, limited by safety standards |
| Widen bandwidth B | Outside the logarithm, multiplied linearly | Doubling bandwidth roughly doubles capacity | Depends on spectrum, which is limited |
| Add spatial channels (MIMO) | Equivalent to making multiple copies of the whole formula | Capacity grows roughly in proportion to the number of channels | Requires multiple antennas and complex signal processing |

The problem is that spectrum is a scarce resource, planned and allocated by each country's regulators. Wide contiguous bands cannot be obtained without limit. When the bandwidth route also reaches its end, engineers turn to a third route: space.

## 8.4.3 From "Interference" to "Resource": Turning Multipath on Its Head

In a city, electromagnetic waves from a base station rarely reach your phone in a straight line. They bounce off building facades, the ground, and vehicles, and arrive one after another along many paths of different lengths. This phenomenon is called **multipath**. In early communications, multipath was a nuisance. Signals from different paths added together and sometimes canceled each other out, making the signal fluctuate between strong and weak.

**MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output)** technology looks at the problem from a different angle. Each path behaves differently, so the multiple antennas on the base station and the multiple antennas on the phone form many distinct "spatial combinations." If these combinations can be clearly distinguished, several different data streams can be sent at the same frequency and at the same moment.

Here is an analogy. In a noisy hall, four people speak to you at once. With only one ear, you hear nothing but a jumble. Now suppose you place four ears in different positions and know exactly how much each person's voice is weakened and delayed on its way to each ear. Then you may be able to compute and recover what each of the four people said.

## 8.4.4 Matrix Decomposition: Opening Orthogonal Lanes Mathematically

"Knowing exactly how each person's voice reaches each ear" corresponds, in communications, to a table called the **channel matrix**. With 4 transmit antennas and 4 receive antennas, it is a 4×4 matrix. Each of its 16 elements describes how much the signal is altered between a given transmit antenna and a given receive antenna. The phone measures reference signals sent by the base station, continuously estimates this matrix, and feeds the information back to the base station.

Once it has the matrix, the system uses a linear algebra tool called **singular value decomposition (SVD)**. SVD splits an entangled matrix into three parts: "precoding directions" at the transmitter, "recovery directions" at the receiver, and a set of mutually independent gains in between. The transmitter pre-"arranges" the data along specific directions before sending it, and the receiver "unpacks" it along the corresponding directions. The originally interwoven signals then become several mutually **orthogonal** independent channels. Orthogonal here means perpendicular and free of crosstalk, like lanes on different levels of a highway interchange.

These lanes cannot be seen or touched. They do not correspond to any particular antenna or any particular reflection path. They are **virtual spatial lanes** constructed mathematically. The Shannon formula applies to each lane separately, and the total capacity is the sum of the capacities of all the lanes.

## 8.4.5 4×4 MIMO: The Four Antennas Inside a Phone

5G phones commonly use **4×4 MIMO spatial multiplexing**. On the downlink, they receive with 4 physical antennas and use the multipath environment created by building reflections to open 4 orthogonal virtual lanes. When channel conditions are good and multipath is rich enough, the data rate can rise to nearly four times that of a single antenna.

This also explains several things that came up in Chapters 2 and 4. The phone's metal frame is divided into segments by nano-molding gaps, and each segment may serve as an antenna. The RF front end needs multiple receive chains. The baseband chip must continuously estimate the matrix and run calculations on it. The four antennas must also be spaced apart inside the body. Otherwise the signals they "hear" are too similar, and the matrix cannot be decomposed into enough independent lanes.

Note that "nearly four times" is an upper limit under ideal conditions. In open countryside, there are few reflection paths, so fewer independent lanes are available. When your hand covers the antennas, some channels also degrade. The benefit of MIMO always depends on the real environment.

## 8.4.6 Summary: Finding a Way Out Within Constraints

The Shannon theorem tells us that the speed of wireless communication is not a matter of will. It is jointly bounded by bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio. Phone engineers' choices reflect a clear systems mindset. The power route is constrained by the logarithm, the battery, and heat. The bandwidth route is constrained by spectrum. So they turn to the spatial dimension. They take what was once interference, multipath reflection, and turn it into a usable resource, multiplying capacity with multiple antennas and matrix computation. The next time your speed-test results rise and fall from place to place, consider how many "spatial lanes" are available around your phone at that moment.
