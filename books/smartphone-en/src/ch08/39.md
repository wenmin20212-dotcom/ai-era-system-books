# 8.2 The Photoelectric Effect and Semiconductor Imaging Physics: How Photons Become a Digital Matrix

Open your phone's camera in a dark room, and the image in the viewfinder turns dim and blurry, covered in flickering colored specks. Many people assume this is because the lens is too small or the phone isn't powerful enough. The real reason lies deeper. Every pixel in a photo comes from counting photons, one arrival at a time, and the arrival of photons is inherently random. Chapter 5 described the relay of computational photography that begins when you press the shutter. This section returns to the starting point of that pipeline: how an image sensor turns a beam of light into a digital matrix, and why it "miscounts" at night.

## 8.2.1 Starting from "Light Is a Particle"

The light-sensing chip in a phone camera is called a CMOS Image Sensor (CIS). Its working principle traces back to Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect. Light does not flow continuously like water. It arrives in discrete packets of energy called photons. The energy of each photon is determined by its frequency:

> **E = hν**
> where h is Planck's constant and ν is the frequency of the light.

The higher the frequency (the shorter the wavelength), the more energy a single photon carries. Blue photons carry more energy than red photons, and infrared photons carry even less. Seen this way, a CIS is a **quantum counter**. It does not measure "brightness" directly. It counts how many photons are successfully captured during the exposure time.

## 8.2.2 Silicon's Threshold: The 1.12 eV Band Gap

When a photon strikes the silicon, whether it gets recorded depends on whether it can "kick" an electron into a state where the electron can move freely.

In semiconductor band theory, electrons in silicon normally sit in the **valence band**, low down and bound to their atoms. To become free electrons that can conduct current, they must jump up to the higher **conduction band**. The energy difference between the two is called the band gap. For silicon it is about **1.12 eV** (electron volts).

Think of it this way. The valence band is the ground floor, the conduction band is the second floor, and between them is a staircase 1.12 eV high that cannot be climbed in stages. If a photon has enough energy to make the leap in one go, the electron reaches the conduction band. It leaves behind a positively charged "vacancy" at its original position, called a hole. This **electron-hole pair** counts as one photon detected. If the photon's energy falls short, it simply passes through the silicon or turns into heat, and the sensor never records it.

Converted to wavelength, 1.12 eV corresponds to a cutoff of roughly **1100 nm**. Photons with wavelengths shorter than this have enough energy. All visible light (about 400–700 nm) falls within this range, which makes silicon a natural material for visible-light sensors. Part of the near-infrared also falls within it. That is one reason phone lens assemblies include an infrared-cut filter: it removes this light, which the human eye cannot see, so that colors don't shift.

| Type of light | Approximate wavelength range | Can it create electron-hole pairs in silicon? |
|---|---|---|
| Blue, green, red light | About 400–700 nm | Yes; the main light used for imaging |
| Near-infrared (shorter than about 1100 nm) | 700–1100 nm | Yes; usually blocked by a filter |
| Longer-wavelength infrared | Longer than about 1100 nm | No; photon energy is insufficient |

## 8.2.3 From Charge to Numbers: The Journey of a Pixel

At the bottom of each pixel sits a photodiode, which works like a small bucket that collects the electrons generated during exposure. The process has four steps:

1. **Collection**: Photons excite electrons, which an electric field gathers and accumulates within the pixel;
2. **Conversion**: When the exposure ends, the pixel circuitry converts the accumulated charge into a voltage;
3. **Quantization**: An Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) turns the voltage into an integer;
4. **Arrangement**: Millions of pixels each output a number, arranged in rows and columns to form a digital matrix.

Each pixel is covered by an array of red, green, and blue filters, so each pixel records the photon count for only one color. The full color information is reconstructed afterward by the Image Signal Processor (ISP), which estimates it from neighboring pixels.

## 8.2.4 Two Kinds of "Uninvited" Noise

In principle, more photons mean larger values and a brighter image. Reality is more complicated, because not every electron in the bucket comes from a photon.

**The first is dark current.** At room temperature, the atoms in the silicon lattice are always vibrating thermally. Occasionally an electron gains enough energy from heat alone to cross the band gap into the conduction band. These electrons cannot be told apart from photon-excited electrons, so they are counted too. Even with the lens completely covered, the sensor outputs a faint "false signal." The higher the temperature, the stronger the thermal vibration and the more noticeable the dark current. This is one reason image quality degrades when a phone heats up during long video recording. It also relates to the thermal management mechanisms discussed in Chapter 6.

**The second is shot noise.** This noise comes from the particle nature of light itself. Photons do not reach a pixel at evenly spaced times. They arrive more like raindrops hitting the ground, following a **Poisson distribution**. Suppose a pixel receives 100 photons per exposure on average. The actual count might be 92, or it might be 108. Under a Poisson distribution, this fluctuation is roughly the square root of the average. So:

- In daylight, photons are plentiful and the average is large, so the fluctuation is a small fraction of the signal and the image is clean;
- At night, photons are scarce and the average is small, so the fluctuation becomes a much larger fraction, the **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)** drops sharply, and the image turns grainy.

The key point is that shot noise is not a flaw in circuit design. It is randomness built into physics itself. Even the most precise circuitry can only faithfully record these random fluctuations. No hardware can eliminate them.

| Noise source | Cause | Main affected scenarios |
|---|---|---|
| Dark current | Lattice thermal vibration causes spontaneous electron transitions | High temperature, long exposure |
| Shot noise | Photon arrivals follow a Poisson distribution | Low light, night scenes |

## 8.2.5 Using Mathematics Against Quantum Fluctuations

If a single count is unreliable, the practical solution is to count several times. That is exactly why night mode captures multiple photos in a row.

When multiple frames are aligned and averaged, the real scene signal appears in every frame and steadily accumulates, while the random fluctuations partly cancel each other out. It's like measuring a table with a ruler: a single measurement may be off, but the average of ten measurements is far more reliable. A phone's computational photography pipeline must also correct the misalignment caused by hand shake and handle moving objects in the scene. This work is done jointly by the ISP and NPU introduced in Chapter 3. Night photos come out cleaner mainly because the algorithm reduces the effect of quantum fluctuations statistically, not because the lens got bigger.

## 8.2.6 Summary

A phone photo begins with a chain of physical processes. Photons carrying energy E = hν strike the silicon. Those that clear the 1.12 eV band gap create electron-hole pairs. The charge is converted into a voltage and finally quantized into numbers in a matrix. Dark current and shot noise set an upper limit on the precision of a single measurement, and multi-frame algorithms are how engineers work within that limit. The division of labor is clear: hardware records photons as faithfully as possible, and software corrects the random errors statistically. The final image comes from the two working together.
