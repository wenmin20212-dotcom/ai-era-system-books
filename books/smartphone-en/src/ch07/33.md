# 7.1 An Illustrated Guide to Core Logic Board Chips and Decoding Their Silkscreen Markings

Open up a phone and lift off the shielding cans, and you'll see a densely packed green or black circuit board. Dozens of black squares of various sizes sit on it, each printed with strings of letters and numbers that seem to follow no pattern at all. Many people stop right there: the chips all look about the same, so how can you tell which is which?

In fact, these characters are not decoration. They are called **markings**, and they are the "ID cards" that chipmakers laser-etch or ink onto the package surface. Learning to read markings is like learning to read house numbers: once you can do it, the layout of the motherboard's "chip city" becomes clear. Using the three major mobile chip camps and the RF front-end vendors as examples, this section teaches you to identify core chips on a real board.

## 7.1.1 Find "Downtown" First: Where the SoC Sits

Whatever the brand, the most important chip on the motherboard usually sits near the center. This is the **System on Chip (SoC)**, which integrates the CPU, GPU, NPU, baseband, and other core units.

However, the most prominent square package you see on a real board often does **not carry the SoC's own marking**. Recall **PoP (Package-on-Package)** from Chapter 2: to save area and shorten traces, the **LPDDR memory** (Low-Power Double Data Rate memory) is stacked directly on top of the SoC. Viewed from above, then, the first thing you read is often the memory maker's marking, with the SoC "pressed" underneath.

It's like a two-story house with the house number hanging on the second-floor balcony, while the real owner lives on the ground floor. Confirming the SoC's identity often requires looking from the side, consulting teardown materials, or, in repair settings, separating the memory layer to reveal the SoC's own marking.

## 7.1.2 The Marking "Dialects" of the Three Major Camps

Each vendor has its own naming habits. Once you learn the patterns, you can tell the camp at a glance.

### 7.1.2.1 Qualcomm: Starts with "SM"

Qualcomm mobile platform chips typically carry part numbers beginning with **SM**, followed by four digits. For example:

- **SM8650**: Snapdragon 8 Gen 3;
- **SM8550**: Snapdragon 8 Gen 2.

The pattern is intuitive: a larger number usually means a newer flagship generation. Around the SoC, you'll also see a ring of "attendants"—**Power Management ICs (PMICs)** with part numbers beginning with **PM**, such as the **PM8550 series**. These are power chips designed specifically to pair with the SoC. They split the battery voltage into multiple precise low-voltage rails, feeding the CPU's big cores, little cores, GPU, and other units separately. SoC and PMIC model numbers often mirror each other, so if you see PM8550, you can reasonably infer it serves the same-generation platform.

### 7.1.2.2 MediaTek: Starts with "MT," with a Logo Graphic

MediaTek chips often bear the company's **double-diamond or lettered logo**, with part numbers beginning with **MT**. For example:

- **MT6989**: Dimensity 9300.

It, too, has a family of companion chips:

- **MT63xx** series: power management chips;
- **MT66xx** series: Wi-Fi and other wireless connectivity chips.

Here, "xx" denotes different models within the same series. The first two digits act like a "department code": 63 handles power, 66 handles connectivity. Remember this, and you can quickly sort MediaTek's "family members" on the board.

### 7.1.2.3 Apple: Apple Logo plus an "APL" Number

Apple's in-house chips are the easiest to recognize—they bear the **iconic Apple logo**, with part numbers beginning with **APL**. For example:

- **APL1V02**: A17 Pro.

Unlike the other two, Apple's part numbers have no obvious numeric relationship to product names. They resemble internal engineering codes and must be matched using public teardown materials.

## 7.1.3 The RF Front End: Small, Dense "Border Outposts"

Leaving downtown, along the edges of the board or near the antenna feed points, you'll find another cluster of chips—the **Radio Frequency Front End (RFFE)**. The key player here is the **Power Amplifier (PA)**, which boosts weak transmit signals to a strength sufficient to reach the base station.

A few vendors dominate this field:

- **Skyworks**: markings often begin with **SKY**, such as **SKY58255**. This is a **PAMiD module** (PA Module with integrated Duplexer), packaging the power amplifier, switches, filtering, and other functions in a single package to save space;
- **Qorvo**: markings such as **QM77048**, likewise a common identity for RF front-end modules.

These two have long held a dominant position in the main PA market.

In the RF area you'll also see many tiny components with no legible text, only a fine **laser dot-matrix code**. Most of these come from Japan's **Murata Manufacturing** and are **miniature microwave ceramic filters** and **duplexers**. A filter acts like a "sieve," letting through only signals in a specified band; a duplexer lets a single antenna transmit and receive simultaneously without interference. Because they are too small to hold full characters, the manufacturer marks them with dot-matrix codes. These are hard for ordinary readers to decode directly, but when you see this appearance, you can generally conclude, "This is an RF filtering component."

## 7.1.4 Motherboard Marking Quick Reference

| Area | Vendor | Marking Feature | Example | Identity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central SoC | Qualcomm | SM + digits | SM8650 / SM8550 | Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 / 8 Gen 2 |
| Power around SoC | Qualcomm | PM + digits | PM8550 series | Companion power management chip |
| Central SoC | MediaTek | Double-diamond or lettered logo + MT | MT6989 | Dimensity 9300 |
| Power / Connectivity | MediaTek | MT63xx / MT66xx | — | Power management / Wi-Fi |
| Central SoC | Apple | Apple logo + APL | APL1V02 | A17 Pro |
| RF power amplifier | Skyworks | SKY + digits | SKY58255 | PAMiD module |
| RF power amplifier | Qorvo | QM + digits | QM77048 | RF front-end module |
| Filtering / Duplexing | Murata | Laser dot-matrix code | — | Ceramic filters, duplexers |

## 7.1.5 Three Practical Rules for Reading Markings

1. **Locate first, then read**: The large central square is the SoC; the edges near the antennas are RF; the clusters of small chips hugging the SoC are mostly power. Position itself is a clue.
2. **Remember prefixes, not full names**: SM, PM, MT, APL, SKY, QM—just a handful of prefixes cover the camp affiliation of most core chips.
3. **Watch for stacking**: The most prominent line of text doesn't necessarily belong to the chip you're looking for; PoP structures let the memory "steal the spotlight."

The point of decoding markings isn't just recognizing a few model numbers. It's grounding the abstract modules from Chapters 2 and 3 in concrete objects you can see under a magnifying glass. From now on, the motherboard is no longer a field of silent black squares but a map you can read.
