# 7.4 A Guide to Reading Phone Datasheets: Fuel Gauge and Charge-Pump Chips as Examples

Remove a phone's back cover, and on the battery protection board or in a corner of the mainboard you can often find a chip no bigger than a grain of rice. It does no computing and transmits no signals, yet it determines whether the battery percentage in the top-right corner of your screen can be trusted. The most reliable way to truly understand such a chip is through the **datasheet** published by its manufacturer. A datasheet is a technical document written for engineers, often dozens of pages long and packed with tables, curves, and abbreviations. This section uses Texas Instruments' (TI) BQ27Z561 fuel gauge as an example to walk you through the basic structure of a datasheet, then applies the same reading method to a charge pump chip.

## 7.4.1 A Datasheet Is Not a User Manual, but a "Contract"

The difference between a datasheet and a product manual can be explained with an analogy from renting an apartment. A manual is like a real estate agent's pitch, telling you "what's great about this place." A datasheet is more like a formal lease, spelling out clause by clause the load limits, electrical capacity, and prohibited uses. If something goes wrong outside the agreed terms, the manufacturer is not responsible.

That is why engineers usually read a datasheet in this order:

1. **Limits first**: under what conditions will the chip be damaged;
2. **Then characteristics**: how accurate, how fast, and how power-efficient it is in normal operation;
3. **Finally, functions and applications**: what modules it contains internally and how to build the surrounding circuitry.

This order reflects a basic engineering principle: first make sure it "doesn't break," then aim for "works well."

## 7.4.2 The Life-or-Death Line on the First Pages: Absolute Maximum Ratings

The BQ27Z561 datasheet prominently states the voltage range its supply pin can withstand: from a negative voltage slightly below zero up to a positive voltage noticeably higher than that of a fully charged cell. This entry is called the **Absolute Maximum Ratings**.

These two numbers mark a "life-or-death line," not a "recommended operating range." Think of them as the weight-limit sign on a bridge: a posted limit of so many tons doesn't mean it's safe to cross at that weight every day; it only means that exceeding it could cause immediate structural damage.

- **Upper limit**: A fully charged single-cell lithium battery sits well below this value, so there appears to be margin. But phones experience momentary voltage spikes when chargers are plugged in or unplugged, as well as electrostatic discharge, so designers must use protection components to keep these spikes on the safe side of the line.
- **Lower limit**: Dropping even slightly below zero counts as crossing the line. This reminds designers that a reversed battery connection or improper ground return current can also damage the chip.

Once you understand this one line, you'll see why phone mainboards are dotted with tiny protection components: one of their jobs is to guard these lines.

## 7.4.3 Electrical Characteristics: Precision Hides Behind the Decimal Point

Past the limit parameters comes the **Electrical Characteristics** table, which shows what the chip can actually do in normal operation. Two key specifications of the BQ27Z561 are:

| Specification | Datasheet Description | Plain-Language Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage sampling | Microvolt-level resolution | Can detect extremely small changes in battery voltage |
| Coulomb counter current integration | Typical input offset error below 1 µV | Very small error when tallying charge flowing in and out |

One term needs explaining here: the **Coulomb Counter**. The coulomb is the unit of electric charge, and a coulomb counter works much like a household water meter: it continuously measures the current flowing through the battery and accumulates it over time to determine "how much charge has been used." Even a tiny error in current measurement adds up after days of continuous accumulation. The datasheet's statement that the coulomb counter's typical input offset error is below 1 microvolt means this "meter" runs accurately enough.

Voltage sampling needs microvolt-level precision because a lithium battery's voltage curve is quite flat in the mid-charge region: a large change in charge produces only a small change in voltage. If the voltage measurement isn't accurate, there's no way to determine the true charge level from such a flat curve.

## 7.4.4 The Core Algorithm: Why Old Phones "Suddenly Shut Down at 20%"

Many people have experienced this: after two or three years of use, a phone still shows around 20% on screen, then suddenly goes black and shuts down. The problem isn't that the battery is "stealing power"; it's that the charge estimation method is too simplistic.

Batteries age, and aging brings two changes: first, **internal resistance** increases; second, the **chemical full-charge capacity** (the maximum charge the battery can actually store) decreases. With higher internal resistance, high-current activities such as gaming or recording video pull the battery's terminal voltage down significantly, which may hit the system's shutdown voltage early. If the fuel gauge is still estimating with a new battery's parameters, the displayed percentage drifts away from reality.

TI's patented **Impedance Track** algorithm targets exactly this problem. During every charge-discharge cycle, it dynamically learns the cell's current internal resistance and chemical full-charge capacity. It's as if the fuel gauge gives the battery a "fresh checkup" every day and uses the latest health data to calculate remaining charge. This way, even as the battery ages, the displayed charge stays consistent with the actual usable charge, fundamentally eliminating the chronic problem of old batteries abruptly dropping and shutting down around 20%.

In a datasheet, this material usually sits in the "Functional Description" section. When reading it, focus on one question: which measurements does the chip rely on, and what results does it derive from them?

## 7.4.5 Hardware Encryption: Batteries Must "Prove Their Identity" Too

The BQ27Z561 also includes a built-in **SHA-256 hardware encryption engine**. SHA-256 is a widely used hash algorithm that converts any data into a fixed-length "digital fingerprint," one that is practically impossible to reverse or forge.

In a phone, it works like an exchange of secret passwords:

1. At startup, the **AP** (Application Processor, the phone's main processor) generates a random challenge code and sends it to the fuel gauge;
2. The fuel gauge uses its internally stored key and the SHA-256 engine to compute a signature over the challenge code, then returns the result;
3. The processor performs the same calculation using the same rules; only if the two results match is the battery recognized as genuine.

Because the challenge code is random each time, a counterfeiter who records one exchange cannot replay it. This is the basic principle behind authenticating genuine batteries. It matters not only for brand interests but also for safety: batteries of unknown origin may lack proper protection circuits and pose an overheating risk.

## 7.4.6 Applying the Same Method to Charge Pump Chips

As introduced in Chapter 5, the **charge pump** in fast charging uses switched capacitors to step a higher input voltage down proportionally while multiplying the current, then delivers it to the battery. When you pick up a charge pump chip's datasheet, you can use the same three-step method:

| Reading Step | Fuel Gauge Focus | Charge Pump Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Check limits | Supply voltage tolerance line | Input voltage tolerance, maximum allowable current |
| Check characteristics | Sampling and integration accuracy | Conversion efficiency curve, heat generation |
| Check functions | Impedance tracking, encrypted authentication | Overvoltage, overcurrent, and overtemperature protection; coordination with charging protocols |

In a charge pump datasheet, the **efficiency curve** usually deserves the closest look. The higher the efficiency, the less energy is wasted as heat, which directly determines whether the phone gets hot during fast charging. When reading the curve, note that the horizontal axis is load current: efficiency is often not a fixed value but varies with current, and the "peak efficiency" cited in marketing is merely the highest point on the curve.

## 7.4.7 Summary

A datasheet is a chip's "contract": the absolute maximum ratings tell you where the boundaries are, the electrical characteristics tell you how capable it is, and the functional description tells you how it achieves that. Taking the BQ27Z561 as an example, from the voltage tolerance limits of its supply pin, to its microvolt-level coulomb counter error, to the Impedance Track algorithm and SHA-256 authentication, a tiny fuel gauge carries three layers of engineering design: measurement, algorithms, and security. Master this reading method, and whenever you encounter an unfamiliar chip, you can start from its datasheet and see clearly the role it plays in the overall system.
