# 4.4 The Evolution of Display Packaging: From COG to COP and the Limits of Folding

Turn on your phone's screen and look at its four borders. Older phones often had a conspicuous black strip along the bottom of the display, commonly called the "chin." Many of today's flagships have nearly uniform borders on all four sides, with a bottom bezel so narrow it is barely noticeable. Many people assume designers simply "drew" the borders thinner. What actually happened is that a piece of circuitry at the bottom of the screen was moved somewhere else. The chin shrank because packaging technology changed.

## 4.4.1 What's Hidden in the Chin

First, a basic question: why does a screen need a border at all?

A display is made up of millions of pixels. When each pixel lights up, and how brightly, is controlled by a dedicated chip called the **display driver IC** (DDIC). It receives image data from the motherboard and then distributes voltage signals to every row and column of the screen through a dense web of fine traces.

The driver chip does not emit light, and it cannot sit directly on top of the pixels. It has to be placed outside the active area. The screen's traces also have to run out of the active area, converge on the chip's pins, and then connect to the flex cable leading to the motherboard. Together, the chip, the fan-out traces, and the cable connector take up a real strip of space. When that strip sits on the front of the screen, it becomes the chin.

So "eliminating the chin" does not mean making the chip disappear. The chip is still there. The engineering problem is **where to put it**. Screen packaging has gone through roughly three generations of solutions to this problem.

## 4.4.2 Generation One: COG — The Chip Pinned to the Edge of the Glass

**COG** (Chip on Glass) means mounting the driver chip directly onto the display's glass substrate. Early LCDs (liquid crystal displays) commonly used this approach.

Its advantages are a mature process and a straightforward structure. The chip and the screen's traces connect on the same piece of glass, so the signal paths are short and reliability is good. The drawback is just as obvious: glass can't bend. Because the chip is mounted on the glass, it has to lie flat along the front edge of the screen, right beside the active area.

Picture a poster on a wall with a rigid wooden strip nailed along its bottom edge, and all the wiring hanging from that strip. The strip can't be folded behind the wall, so it stays exposed. That is where the chin several millimeters wide at the bottom of a COG screen comes from. It holds the driver chip and its traces.

## 4.4.3 Generation Two: COF — Moving the Chip onto a Flexible Cable

If the problem is that glass can't bend, the solution is to get the chip off the glass. **COF** (Chip on Film) mounts the driver chip on a short length of flexible film cable instead of on the glass substrate.

Only a very narrow strip along the edge of the glass substrate is kept for connections. The film cable extends from there and carries the chip as it folds slightly toward the back of the screen. The chip thus moves from the front to the back, and the chin narrows accordingly.

To continue the poster analogy: the rigid wooden strip at the bottom has been replaced by a soft fabric band with a junction box sewn onto it, which can be tucked behind the poster. However, a rigid connecting section is still needed between the band and the poster, and the screen glass itself still can't bend, so that edge section remains visible on the front. COF makes the chin narrower but cannot eliminate it. It strikes a reasonable balance between cost and results, which is why it is common in mid-range models.

## 4.4.4 Generation Three: COP — Letting the Screen Bend Itself

To go further, the screen substrate itself has to be bendable. That requires OLED (organic light-emitting diode) displays.

Each OLED pixel emits its own light, so there's no need for an LCD-style backlight module, and the display layers can be built on a flexible material. In the flexible OLEDs common in flagship phones, the base is no longer glass but a heat-resistant plastic film called **polyimide** (PI). This material is thin, tough, and bendable.

**COP** (Chip on Plastic / Chip on PI) takes advantage of this. The PI plastic substrate at the very bottom of the display, along with the traces on it, is curled a full 180 degrees toward the back of the screen, forming a fold with an extremely small radius. The driver chip sits directly on this folded-back section of substrate, hidden right behind the screen's active area.

This time no extra fabric band is needed. The bottom edge of the poster is itself flexible and is folded straight behind the wall, with the junction box attached to the back of the poster. Seen from the front, the active area extends almost all the way to the edge of the body, and the bottom bezel can shrink to about 1.5 mm.

## 4.4.5 Comparing the Three Generations

| Packaging Method | Full Name | Chip Location | Screen Type | Bottom Bezel | Typical Market Position |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| COG | Chip on Glass | Edge of the glass on the front of the screen | Early LCD | A conspicuous chin several mm wide | Early models |
| COF | Chip on Film | On a flexible film cable, folded slightly to the back | LCD / OLED | Noticeably narrower, but an edge remains | Mid-range models |
| COP | Chip on Plastic | PI substrate curled 180 degrees behind the active area | Flexible OLED | Can shrink to about 1.5 mm | Flagship models |

The table shows that all three generations are answering the same question: whichever material can bend, the chip can follow it around to the back. In COG, nothing bends. COF makes the cable bendable. COP makes the screen substrate itself bendable.

## 4.4.6 The Cost of the Ultimate Fold

A 180-degree fold sounds simple, but it is actually a very demanding process step.

- **The traces must withstand bending**: The metal traces on the PI substrate are folded into a tight curve, with the outer side under tension and the inner side under compression. Both the materials and the trace design must prevent cracking.
- **Yield and cost**: The folding step adds more chances for defects, and flexible OLEDs are already more expensive than LCDs. That is why COP mostly appears in flagship phones.
- **Space on the back is consumed**: Once the chip is hidden behind the screen, it takes up some of the internal thickness and area of the body, which has to be re-planned together with the motherboard, battery, and flex cables.
- **Repairs become harder**: The screen, the fold area, and the driver chip form a single unit. If it is damaged, the whole assembly usually has to be replaced.

This matches what the earlier sections of this chapter described: space inside a phone never just appears out of nowhere. The chin saved on the front of the screen has moved into the body, where it shows up as higher demands on materials, processes, and component stacking.

## 4.4.7 Summary

Narrow bezels come from where the driver chip is placed, not from cosmetic design. COG keeps the chip on the front. COF hangs it on a flexible cable folded toward the back. COP lets the screen substrate fold itself, hiding the chip directly behind the active area. Next time you compare the chins of two phones, ask yourself whether a strip of plastic at the bottom of the screen has been folded around to the back.
