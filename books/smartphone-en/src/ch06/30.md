# 6.3 Sensor Fusion: Digital Calibration of Faint Physical Signals

Pick up your phone and open a map app. The little arrow that represents you turns as you turn. Rotate the phone sideways, and the video automatically fills the screen. In a motion-controlled racing game, a gentle tilt of the handset makes the car take a corner. These actions seem utterly ordinary, yet behind them lies a problem that is easy to miss: none of the sensors in the phone that track its orientation is reliable on its own. The phone has to combine several flawed measurements to work out which way the device is pointing at this moment. This process is called sensor fusion.

## 6.3.1 Two "Witnesses," Each with a Weakness

First, meet the two protagonists. Both are MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) devices: tiny components with micrometer-scale mechanical structures machined into silicon. Each is usually about the size of a grain of rice and barely noticeable once soldered onto the motherboard.

The **gyroscope** measures rotational speed, meaning how many degrees the device turns each second. Inside it, a tiny mass vibrates continuously. When the device rotates, the Coriolis force pushes the mass slightly off its path, and a circuit converts this faint displacement into an electrical signal. The gyroscope's strength is that it is **sensitive and fast**. It can detect even a slight flick of the wrist.

The trouble is that a gyroscope only tells you *how fast* the device is turning. To know *where* it has turned to, you must add up the rotation rate at every instant, which mathematicians call integration. Every measurement carries a tiny error, and these errors pile up as they are summed. It is like walking blindfolded while counting your steps. Each step is off by a little, and after a few hundred steps the error becomes too large to ignore. This phenomenon is called **integration drift**. If a phone relied on the gyroscope alone, it could be lying still on a desk while its calculated orientation slowly "rotated by itself" over a few minutes.

The **accelerometer** measures acceleration. Earth's gravity always points straight down, so when the phone is at rest, the accelerometer detects the direction of gravity and can tell which side of the device faces the ground. This reference comes from the Earth itself. It is **stable over the long term and does not drift**, and that is the accelerometer's strength.

Its weakness is just as obvious. When a person walks, runs, or rides in a vehicle, the device shakes constantly. The accelerations caused by this mechanical vibration mix with gravity, and the accelerometer cannot tell which part is gravity and which part is shaking. On a bumpy bus, its sense of "down" jumps around. In addition, gravity alone can reveal only pitch and roll. It cannot tell whether the phone is facing east or west around the vertical axis.

That gap is filled by a third member, the **magnetometer**. It senses Earth's magnetic field and works as an electronic compass, giving a long-term reference for horizontal heading. But it, too, is easily disturbed by nearby metal, speaker magnets, and electric currents.

| Sensor | What It Measures | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gyroscope | Angular velocity | Highly sensitive at high frequencies, fast response | Errors accumulate through integration, causing drift |
| Accelerometer | Linear acceleration (including gravity) | Uses gravity as a reference, stable over the long term | Easily disturbed by mechanical noise from walking, bumps, and similar motion |
| Magnetometer | Direction of Earth's magnetic field | Provides a horizontal heading reference | Easily disturbed by nearby magnetic objects |

## 6.3.2 Cross-Checking the Witnesses: The Extended Kalman Filter

The three witnesses complement one another neatly. The gyroscope is accurate over short periods but wanders off over time. The accelerometer and magnetometer are noisy at any given instant but steady when averaged over the long run. The idea behind fusion is therefore to **trust the gyroscope in the short term and let the accelerometer and magnetometer correct it in the long term**.

The algorithm commonly used to carry out this idea is the **Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)**. Picture a seasoned ship's captain fixing a position at sea. First he estimates roughly where the ship is from its speed and heading, which corresponds to the gyroscope's integrated prediction. Then he takes a sighting of the stars with a sextant to get a position fix, which corresponds to the accelerometer and magnetometer measurements. When the two disagree, the captain does not simply average them. He weighs which one is more trustworthy. If the waves are high and the stars are hard to see, he leans on dead reckoning. If dead reckoning has been running for a long time and its error has grown, he leans on the observation.

This **probabilistic weighting** is exactly what a Kalman filter does. It tracks a "degree of uncertainty" for each estimate. Each time a new batch of data arrives, it assigns weights according to how trustworthy each source is, computes a combined result, and updates its own uncertainty. When the phone shakes violently, the accelerometer's weight drops automatically. When the phone is still, the accelerometer can pull back the drift that the gyroscope has quietly accumulated. The word "extended" refers to the fact that rotations involve nonlinear mathematical relationships. The filter must make a local linear approximation at each step before the Kalman framework can be applied.

The output of fusion is usually a **quaternion**, a set of four numbers that can describe any rotation in three-dimensional space. Compared with the familiar three angles of pitch, roll, and yaw, quaternions do not cause the calculation to lock up in special orientations, such as when the device is nearly vertical. They also make smooth interpolation easy, which is why game engines, navigation software, and augmented reality apps use them widely.

## 6.3.3 Who Does the Grunt Work: The Sensor Hub

Fusion calculations must run nonstop, 100 to 200 times per second (100 Hz to 200 Hz). If this job went to the main processor's big cores, they would hardly ever get to sleep, and the battery would drain quickly.

That is why phones include a dedicated **Sensor Hub**. It is either a separate low-power microcontroller or an always-on small core built into the SoC, and its sole job is to collect data from the various sensors and run the EKF. It draws current only at the microampere level, far less than the main processor's big cores. The main processor can sleep undisturbed and read the ready-made quaternions from the Sensor Hub only when it needs them. This division of labor follows the principle of "dedicated chips for dedicated tasks" described in Chapter 3. It also ties in with the sleep mechanisms used when the screen is off, discussed in Chapter 5. Features such as step counting and raise-to-wake keep working with the screen off precisely because of this small chip.

## 6.3.4 Summary

Sensor fusion illustrates a common approach in system design. No single component is perfect, so engineers lay out each one's flaws and let the components correct one another, producing a result more reliable than any single part could deliver. From tiny masses vibrating on silicon to the hundreds of probabilistic calculations the Sensor Hub runs every second, the phone turns these faint physical signals into a trustworthy picture of its orientation in space. Orientation data affects the user experience. Information such as payment passwords and fingerprints, however, affects users' money and privacy and needs stricter protection.
