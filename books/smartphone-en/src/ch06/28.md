# 6.1 The Energy Game of Mobile Operating Systems: From EAS Scheduling to Process Tombstoning

Pick up your phone and scroll through short videos for ten straight minutes. The body grows slightly warm and the battery drains fast. Leave it on the desk overnight, and by morning the battery has barely dropped. Behind both states, the same "invisible steward" is making the decisions: the mobile operating system. It has to answer two questions. **Which core should handle the task at hand right now?** And **do the apps you can't see still deserve to keep consuming power?** The scheduler answers the first question, and background control mechanisms answer the second. This section follows both threads to see how the operating system makes fine-grained trade-offs between performance and battery life.

## 6.1.1 Why "Running Fast" Is Not the Same as "Scheduling Well"

As Chapter 3 explained, the CPU in a phone's SoC (System on Chip) usually combines "big cores" and "little cores." A big core is like a large-displacement sports car: powerful acceleration, but heavy fuel consumption. A little core is like an e-bike: limited speed, but extremely economical.

The scheduling philosophy of traditional computers is fairly simple. Whichever core is idle or most powerful gets the task, and the goal is to "finish as quickly as possible." On a phone, this approach causes problems. Imagine a small background task that syncs your contacts. If it is sent to a big core, that core must raise its voltage and frequency to run it, and the added energy cost far outweighs the value of the task itself. Battery capacity is limited and space for heat dissipation is extremely tight. So a phone's scheduling goal can't simply be "fast." It has to be "**get the job done with the least possible energy**."

## 6.1.2 EAS: A Scheduler That Decides with the "Electricity Bill" in Hand

To solve this problem, ARM and Google jointly led the development of **EAS (Energy Aware Scheduling)** and brought it into the Linux kernel used by Android.

At the heart of EAS is an **Energy Model** table built into the system. Understanding it requires one term: **OPP (Operating Performance Point)**. A CPU core doesn't have only two states, "on" and "off." It has a series of voltage-and-frequency combinations, much like the gears of a car. The energy model records **the actual power draw of each core at each voltage-frequency level, accurate to the milliwatt**.

With this table, EAS behaves like a property manager holding the electricity bill. Whenever a new task arrives, it doesn't just ask "who is least busy?" Instead, it runs the numbers for each option:

> If this task is assigned to core X, is the increase in total system energy consumption across the SoC the lowest among all options?

Two key phrases here deserve a closer look.

- **"Across the SoC"**: EAS looks at the total bill for the whole chip, not the local bill of a single core. Placing a task on a little core that is already running may require only a slight bump in its performance level. Waking a sleeping big core costs extra just to "turn the lights on."
- **"The increase in energy"**: It compares marginal cost, meaning how much more power it takes to do this one extra thing.

The simplified scenarios below help illustrate the difference:

| Scenario | Crude scheduling approach | How EAS thinks |
|---|---|---|
| Syncing email in the background | Hand it to an idle big core and finish quickly | A little core at a low level can handle it with lower total energy, so it goes to the little core |
| Scrolling a list, opening an app | Distribute evenly | The calculation shows a little core would need to run at full level and would still stutter, so a big core is actually the better deal |
| Several light tasks arriving at once | Wake multiple cores to spread them out | Concentrate them on cores that are already awake as much as possible, letting the others stay asleep |

As this shows, EAS does not blindly "favor little cores." It lets the data decide. The more accurate the energy model, the more sensible the scheduling trade-offs. That is why chip vendors must carefully calibrate this table for every SoC.

## 6.1.3 The Other War in the Background: Making "Invisible Apps" Truly Stop

The scheduler manages how active tasks are assigned, but there is a stealthier power drain: apps you have already switched away from that keep running quietly. They may connect to the network on a timer, refresh data, or even wake the processor repeatedly. This behavior is often called "rogue wakeups." A phone with dozens of apps installed, each making constant small moves in the background, will see its standby battery life eaten away.

The two major mobile operating systems take similar approaches with different implementations:

**iOS's tombstoning mechanism.** When you send an app to the background, iOS fully suspends its threads within just a few seconds. The app now has a "tombstone": its state is preserved in memory, with the interface and progress intact, but it no longer receives any CPU time. When you tap it again, it is "resurrected" from the tombstone and looks as though it had been waiting for you all along.

**Android's cgroups freezing.** Android uses the Linux kernel's **cgroups (Control Groups)** mechanism to group and manage processes by category, such as foreground and background. Once an app moves to the background, the system can send it a **SIGSTOP signal**, a kernel instruction that pauses a process immediately, cutting it off from the CPU's run queue. A frozen process can no longer wake the processor on its own, so rogue wakeups have no way to occur.

An analogy helps here. Tombstoning and cgroups freezing both resemble a library at closing time that photographs each reader's desk before asking everyone to leave. The books stay exactly where they are, but all the lights can be switched off. When readers return, their desks are restored and they pick up where they left off.

| Comparison | iOS tombstoning | Android cgroups freezing |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Within seconds after the app is switched out | After the app moves to the background |
| Implementation | Fully suspends the app's threads | Sends SIGSTOP via control groups, cutting off the run queue |
| Core effect | Preserves state, uses no CPU | Eliminates rogue background wakeups |

Of course, tasks that genuinely need to run in the background, such as music playback, navigation, and downloads, are allowed through dedicated permissions and service channels rather than being blocked across the board.

## 6.1.4 Summary: Saving Power Is a Matter of "Doing the Math"

Taken together, the two threads sum up energy management in mobile operating systems: **careful budgeting for foreground tasks, strict control over background apps**. EAS relies on the energy model to compute the globally optimal energy cost for every task assignment. Tombstoning and cgroups freezing make the apps outside your view truly fall silent. This is the software foundation that lets the same phone run smoothly in your hand and sit quietly on your desk through the night.

Yet no matter how shrewd the accounting, it cannot stop heat from steadily building up under heavy load. When the chip's temperature nears its limit, the system must call on a tougher set of self-protection measures: the thermal wall and thermal throttling, which the next section discusses.
