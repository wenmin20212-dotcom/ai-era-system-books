# 9.1 Multimodal Object Recognition: Making AI Your Teardown Mentor at Hand

Open up an old phone and you will see dozens of small black squares lined up on the mainboard. Each chip has one or two lines of tiny characters engraved on its surface, legible only under a magnifying glass. In the past, figuring out what those characters meant required digging through manufacturers' datasheets or asking veteran technicians on repair forums. Now there is another route: photograph the chip surface with your phone and hand it to an AI that can "look at a picture and talk about it." Guided by your questions, it will help you work out where the chip came from and what it does.

This section introduces this way of learning: how to ask questions, what you can expect to get, and how to verify the AI's answers.

## 9.1.1 What "Multimodal" Means: AI Has Learned to See

Ordinary chat-style AI handles only text. A **Multimodal Large Language Model** (multimodal LLM for short) can understand text, images, and screenshots together; "multimodal" refers to these multiple modes of information. For anyone learning about phones, this means three kinds of material can serve directly as the starting point for a question:

| Input material | How to obtain it | Questions it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Macro photo of chip marking | Shot in the phone's macro mode or through a clip-on magnifier | Who made this chip? What function does it serve? |
| Top-down view of the opened interior | Overall shot after removing the back cover | Where are the mainboard, battery, cameras, and antennas? |
| Engineering-mode screenshot | Screenshot of a hidden system menu or debug screen | What do these signal strengths, bands, and battery parameters mean? |

As Chapter 7 explained, the characters on a chip's surface are called the **marking**. They are usually laser-etched and contain information such as a model code and a batch number or production date. Reading the characters is not hard for the human eye; the hard part is mapping them onto the whole industry chain. Multimodal AI has read a large volume of public technical material during training and can take on the preliminary lookup and reasoning for this step on your behalf.

## 9.1.2 A Structured "Teardown Mentor" Prompt Template

If you want the AI to give an organized answer, your question needs to spell out the role, the material, and the task. The template below can be used as is:

> **💡 AI Collaboration Interface: Prompt for Decoding Mainboard Chip Markings and Pins from Macro Photos**
>
> **[Context Role]**: You are a senior hardware system architect for mobile devices. I am reading *Foundations of System Knowledge for the AI Era: The Smartphone*.
>
> **[Input Material]**: [Upload your macro photo of the chip, or enter the etched text, for example: "QM77048 / 2341" or "SM8650-AB"]
>
> **[Analysis Task]**: Based on public information about the global mobile semiconductor supply chain, please analyze the physical details of this chip for me:
> 1. **Original manufacturer and full industrial part number**: Who manufactures this chip? What is its full industrial Part Number?
> 2. **Functional role and subsystem membership**: Does it belong to the RF front end (PA/LNA/filter), power management (PMIC/charge pump), sensors, or the core SoC?
> 3. **Operating characteristics**: Which system bus does it connect to (e.g., MIPI, I²C, PCIe)? What are its two most important electrical operating limits (e.g., maximum operating current, voltage rating, or supported bands)?
> 4. **System importance**: If this chip develops a micro-crack or a cold solder joint, what typical failure would the phone exhibit?

The four questions in the template correspond to the cognitive ladder built up in the earlier chapters of this book:

- **Question 1 corresponds to the "Component" level**: confirm the chip's identity first. The characters in a marking are usually abbreviated; the full part number is often longer and includes suffixes for package, temperature grade, and so on. This is the same marking-decoding method covered in Chapter 7.
- **Question 2 corresponds to the "Structure" and "Function" levels**: classify the chip. Power amplifiers (PA), low-noise amplifiers (LNA), and power management ICs (PMIC) were all introduced in Chapters 2 and 3. Only once you know which subsystem it belongs to can you understand why it sits where it does on the mainboard.
- **Question 3 corresponds to the "Connection" and "Flow" levels**: chips communicate over buses. MIPI is the high-speed interface commonly used by cameras and displays; I²C is the low-speed control bus commonly used by sensors and power chips; PCIe is mostly used for high-speed storage or communication modules. The electrical limits determine how much current and how high a voltage the chip can withstand, and which bands it can operate in.
- **Question 4 corresponds to the "Mechanism" level**: infer function from failure. A cold-soldered RF chip may show up as a signal that comes and goes; a faulty power chip may cause a failure to boot or abnormal charging. This question effectively asks you to "remove" the chip in your mind and see how the whole system is affected.

## 9.1.3 A Real Usage Scenario

Suppose you open an old phone and find a chip under the shielding can near the antenna, marked "QM77048 / 2341". You send the characters along with the photo to the AI.

A useful answer would unfold roughly like this: first, infer the manufacturer and product line from the model prefix; next, combine that with the chip's position on the mainboard (close to the antenna feed point) to judge that it belongs to the RF front end; then explain which type of control bus manages it and its approximate band range; and finally, remind you that cracked solder joints on this kind of chip commonly show up as poor signal on certain bands, or intermittent calls after a drop. As for the "2341" after the slash, a common reading is the production year and week, but encoding rules differ between manufacturers and need to be checked case by case.

The other example, "SM8650-AB", belongs to a different category of chip. The "SM" prefix is commonly used in the industry to identify a certain vendor's mobile platform SoCs, and the suffix after the hyphen usually distinguishes batches or versions. The AI would remind you that on the mainboard this kind of chip is often stacked with memory—the PoP (package-on-package) stacking discussed in Chapter 2—so the marking you see from above may belong to the memory on top rather than the SoC beneath it.

That reminder says a lot: the AI's value lies not just in reporting a model number but in placing a chip back into the whole system to explain it.

## 9.1.4 Don't Treat AI as the Answer Key

AI is a mentor, not an authoritative database. Keep three principles in mind when using it:

1. **It may fabricate.** Large language models can generate model numbers or parameters that look plausible but do not actually exist—what practitioners call "hallucination." Any specific figure, such as maximum current or voltage rating, should be checked against the original manufacturer's datasheet.
2. **Photo quality determines the result.** Glare on the marking, worn characters, and a tilted shooting angle can all cause misrecognition. Transcribe the characters clearly by hand first, then submit them together with the photo; accuracy will improve noticeably.
3. **Information on custom chips is limited.** Phone makers often commission custom chips from suppliers, and the marking may be an internal code name that cannot be found in public sources. In such cases, the AI should answer "cannot be determined" rather than force a conclusion. If it doesn't flag its uncertainty, you can follow up: "What is the basis for this point? How reliable is it?"

Treating the AI's answer as a hypothesis to be verified, and then testing it against datasheets, teardown reports, and the physical hardware, is itself basic engineering training.

## 9.1.5 Summary

Multimodal AI turns a photo, a line of marking, or a screenshot into an entry point for learning. With structured questioning that moves step by step from "what is this" to "what does it connect to" and "what happens if it fails," you can walk the book's nine-level ladder on a real chip. Identifying parts, however, is only the first step.
