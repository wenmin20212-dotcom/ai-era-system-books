# 3.5 A Panoramic Comparison of the System Roles of Core Miniature Modules

In the previous four sections, we followed several threads (computing, imaging, RF, haptics and acoustics) and got to know the phone's micro-modules one by one. Now let's put them back into the same machine. The moment the phone in your hand lights up its screen, no single chip is working alone. More than a dozen subsystems go to work at once. This section gathers their responsibilities, the metrics used to judge them, and the companies behind them into one panoramic comparison table. A short scenario then shows how they hand off tasks to one another within milliseconds.

## 3.5.1 One Table to See "Who Does What"

The table below is organized by functional subsystem. Each row answers four questions: What is the hardware? What task does it perform? Which metrics measure how good it is? Which companies mainly design or manufacture it?

| Functional Subsystem | Core Hardware | Task and Functional Essence | Key Performance Metrics | Representative Semiconductor and Manufacturing Companies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| General scheduling and computing | CPU big/mid/little core clusters | Complex logic decisions, system responsiveness, and multithreaded scheduling | Single-/multi-core IPC, peak clock speed (GHz) | ARM, Qualcomm, Apple, MediaTek |
| Graphics rendering and display | Mobile GPU cores | Vertex transformation for 3D games, shader computation, and UI frame composition | Floating-point throughput (TFLOPS), performance per watt | Qualcomm Adreno, ARM Immortalis |
| On-device AI inference | Dedicated NPU matrix units | Accelerating local large models, computer vision, and speech inference | Dense compute (TOPS), quantization support (INT4/8) | Qualcomm Hexagon, Apple Neural Engine |
| Imaging and photoelectric conversion | CIS image sensor + ISP | Photon capture, analog-to-digital conversion, RAW-domain computational photography algorithms | Sensor size (e.g., 1-inch), dynamic range (dB) | Sony LYTIA, Samsung ISOCELL, OmniVision |
| Stabilization and zoom mechanics | Periscope prism + voice coil motor | Millisecond-scale physical lens shifts to compensate for hand shake and to focus | Driving force, focus latency (ms), stabilization angle | Largan Precision, Sunny Optical, Alps Alpine |
| Wireless microwave amplification | RF power amplifier (PA) | Amplifying the transmit signal thousands of times so it reaches distant base stations | Output power (dBm), power-added efficiency (PAE) | Skyworks, Qorvo, Vanchip |
| High-frequency signal filtering | BAW / SAW filters | Filtering out-of-band electromagnetic noise to achieve full-duplex isolation | Insertion loss (dB), out-of-band rejection | Broadcom, Murata Manufacturing |
| Transient haptic simulation | X-axis linear vibration motor | Millisecond-scale horizontal, directional back-and-forth vibration that mimics button feedback | Steady-state acceleration (Grms), start/stop latency (ms) | AAC Technologies, Luxshare Precision |

## 3.5.2 Three Keys to Reading the Table

This table is dense. Reading it across the rows and down the columns reveals different things.

**Key one: Read the "Functional Essence" column to tell general-purpose from specialized.** The CPU is like a general manager who can handle anything. It excels at decisions and scheduling, but it is a poor choice for large batches of repetitive computation. The GPU is more like a team of assembly-line workers that handles thousands of pixels at once. The NPU (Neural Processing Unit) specializes in matrix multiplication. The ISP (Image Signal Processor) serves only camera data. The further down the table you go, the more "single-minded" the module becomes. When a task goes to one of these specialized units, it usually uses far less power than having the CPU brute-force it. This is exactly the heterogeneous computing idea from Section 3.1: let the right chip do the right job.

**Key two: Read the "Key Metrics" column to see where launch-event specs come from.** GHz, TFLOPS, TOPS, the "1-inch sensor," dB: every number you hear at a launch event has a home in this column. Keep two things in mind:
- A single peak number does not equal real-world experience. A high CPU clock speed does not guarantee real speed if the IPC (Instructions Per Cycle) is low. A GPU with high floating-point throughput but poor performance per watt will heat up and throttle after extended play.
- Some unfamiliar-looking units all point to either "loss" or "efficiency." For a filter, lower insertion loss is better. For a PA, higher power-added efficiency (PAE) saves more power. The quality of the RF section shows up mostly in battery life and signal strength, not in benchmark scores.

**Key three: Read the "Companies" column to understand the division of labor.** ARM licenses the architecture for computing chips, and Qualcomm, Apple, and MediaTek design SoCs on top of it. Sony, Samsung, and OmniVision supply image sensors. Largan Precision, Sunny Optical, and Alps Alpine provide lenses and motors. RF components are concentrated among Skyworks, Qorvo, Broadcom, and Murata, with Vanchip as a domestic Chinese player. Vibration motors come from AAC Technologies and Luxshare Precision. It is almost impossible for one company to build a phone from start to finish, a point Chapter 7 will develop.

## 3.5.3 A Short Scenario: The Relay Behind a Night-Mode Shot

A division of labor on paper becomes clear only in real use. Imagine you raise your phone on a dim street corner and press the shutter:

1. **The CPU takes the order first.** The touch controller reports that the shutter was pressed. The CPU recognizes a photo event, wakes the camera pipeline, and sets clock frequencies for each unit.
2. **The mechanical parts move in sync.** The voice coil motor drives the lens to focus while optical image stabilization continuously counteracts hand shake. If the telephoto camera is in use, the light also bends through the periscope prism.
3. **The CIS exposes continuously.** In low light, the sensor rapidly captures multiple frames. It converts photons into electrical signals and then into digital signals.
4. **The ISP and NPU split the processing.** The ISP aligns, denoises, and merges the frames in the RAW domain. The NPU identifies the sky, faces, and lights in the scene and optimizes each region.
5. **The GPU handles presentation.** The GPU composites the preview and the finished thumbnail onto the screen.
6. **The haptic endpoint wraps things up.** The X-axis linear motor delivers a short, crisp "click" to tell you the shot is done.

If the RF PA is uploading to a cloud album at the same time, it works alongside everything else. The main heat source here is usually the SoC region that houses the ISP and NPU, along with the continuously running CIS. The system's physical bottleneck is controlling power and temperature at the same time inside a tiny sealed body. This is the starting point for the thermal management mechanisms discussed in Chapter 6.

## 3.5.5 Summary

This chapter answered the question "What does each module do?" The CPU schedules, the GPU renders, and the NPU runs inference. The CIS and ISP handle imaging. Motors and prisms handle mechanics, PAs and filters handle electromagnetic waves, and the linear motor handles touch. Once the division of labor is clear, a new question follows: how do all these modules fit into a body less than a centimeter thick?
