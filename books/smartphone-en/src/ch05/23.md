# 5.1 RF Electromagnetic Flow: The Leap from Digital Packets to Airborne Microwaves

You tap "Send" on your phone, and a photo sets off. The trip from the button press to the signal leaving the device covers only a few centimeters and takes too little time to notice. Along the way, though, the data changes form several times. It starts as a string of 0s and 1s and becomes an undulating analog waveform. That waveform is shifted up to a frequency that oscillates billions of times per second and amplified thousands of times over. Finally it radiates out through the gaps in the metal frame as an electromagnetic wave in the air.

This section follows that route to see how a data packet turns from a digital signal inside a chip into a microwave in the air.

## 5.1.1 First Stop: The Baseband "Paints" Bits into the Waveform

The data packet is first handed to the baseband chip. The baseband handles communication protocols and encoding. The first problem it has to solve is this: an electromagnetic wave is just a continuous oscillation, so how can it carry 0s and 1s?

The answer is to change the shape of the wave. A sine wave has two properties that are easy to adjust. One is its **amplitude**, which is the height of the peaks. The other is its **phase**, which is the moment at which the wave begins to rise and fall. Modern phones use **QAM (Quadrature Amplitude Modulation)**, which adjusts both properties at once. Each "amplitude + phase" combination stands for a fixed group of bits.

An analogy helps here. Suppose you can only send messages to a distant friend with a flashlight:

- If you only use "on/off," each flash carries just 1 bit.
- If you split the brightness into several levels and also define several distinct starting points for each flash, you have many more combinations, so each flash can express more information.

QAM follows the same idea, only with far finer levels. Plotting every combination on a flat coordinate grid produces a neat array of points, which engineers call a "constellation diagram." **1024-QAM** means the constellation has 1,024 points. Because 2 to the 10th power is 1,024, each point corresponds to exactly one group of 10 bits, so a single symbol period carries **10 bits**.

| Modulation Scheme | Constellation Points | Bits per Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Simple on/off | 2 | 1 |
| 1024-QAM | 1024 | 10 |

The denser the points, the more data gets through per unit of time. The cost is that neighboring points sit closer together, so a little noise can make the receiver pick the wrong one. That is why a phone uses high-order modulation when the signal is strong and falls back to schemes with fewer, more error-resistant points when the signal is weak. The theoretical limit behind this tradeoff is covered in Chapter 8, in the discussion of Shannon's theorem.

## 5.1.2 Second Stop: The Transceiver "Relocates" the Signal to 3.5 GHz

The waveform coming out of the baseband is relatively low in frequency and cannot go straight to the antenna. One reason is that an antenna's size depends on its operating frequency, and a low-frequency signal would need an antenna far larger than the phone itself. Another is that countries have already divided the airwaves among different uses, so phones must transmit within licensed frequency bands.

So the signal enters the **transceiver**, where it is "relocated" in a process called **mixing**. Inside the transceiver, a local oscillator generates a stable high-frequency carrier. When the analog sine wave from the baseband is mixed with this carrier, the original waveform shifts as a whole up to a high frequency. The amplitude and phase information in the waveform is not lost. It simply moves to a higher frequency.

Think of it this way: the baseband has written a letter, and the letter's contents are the modulated waveform. Mixing is like putting that letter into a vehicle that travels on a designated highway. Take the **n78 band**, which is widely used for 5G. Its highway sits near **3.5 GHz**, or about 3.5 billion oscillations per second. The vehicle has changed, but the letter's contents stay the same.

## 5.1.3 Third Stop: The Power Amplifier Makes the Signal "Shout"

Once shifted to 3.5 GHz, the signal is extremely weak. If it were sent out as is, it would drown in noise a few meters away. To reach a base station hundreds of meters away or more, it must first be amplified. This job falls to the **power amplifier (PA)**, which sits in the RF front end. We saw it during the teardown in Chapter 2.

The PA boosts the signal's power by **thousands of times**, raising it to about **200 milliwatts**. Communications engineers usually express power in dBm, a logarithmic unit referenced to 1 milliwatt. 200 milliwatts is roughly **23 dBm**. A logarithmic scale is used because signal strength spans an enormous range: the faint signal picked up by an antenna and the signal sent out differ by many orders of magnitude, which is awkward to write in ordinary numbers.

200 milliwatts is close to the power of a small indicator light, which doesn't sound like much. Yet the PA is one of the most power-hungry components in the whole phone. Its efficiency at converting electrical energy into RF energy is limited, and most of the energy that doesn't get converted becomes heat. The PA is one of the main reasons the side of the phone against your ear gets warm during a call.

## 5.1.4 Fourth Stop: Envelope Tracking Makes the Power Supply "Follow the Shape"

The PA wastes power in another way as well. A QAM-modulated signal's amplitude changes constantly, with high peaks and low troughs. Feeding the PA a constant high voltage does guarantee that the peaks come through without distortion, but in the troughs most of that voltage goes unused and simply becomes heat.

It's like driving with the gas pedal floored whether you're going uphill or downhill, and controlling your speed with the brakes.

**Envelope tracking (ET)** chips address exactly this problem. The "envelope" is the outer contour you get by connecting the signal's peaks. An ET chip tracks this contour **millions of times per second** (at several megahertz). When the signal is about to surge, it immediately raises the supply voltage, and when the signal drops back, it lowers the voltage right away. Power is supplied only as needed.

| Power Supply Method | Approach | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed voltage | Always supplies enough for the peaks | Heavy waste and extra heat in the troughs |
| Envelope tracking (ET) | Voltage follows the signal contour in real time | Clearly higher amplifier efficiency |

The **clear gain** in amplifier efficiency means longer battery life for calls and browsing, and a cooler phone. The data passes through this stop unchanged, but this stop determines how much power it takes to send that data.

## 5.1.5 Final Stop: The Antenna Gaps Turn Current into Electromagnetic Waves

By now the amplified signal is a high-frequency alternating current, and it is sent to the antenna. In many phones, the antenna is the metal frame itself, divided into several segments by thin plastic-filled slits. As described in Chapter 4, these slits are made with a nano-injection molding process.

The high-frequency current surges rapidly back and forth along this piece of metal, setting up alternating electric and magnetic fields around the gaps. A changing electric field produces a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field produces an electric field. Each sustains the other as they break away from the phone and travel toward the base station at **the speed of light**. From this moment on, your photo is an electromagnetic wave in the air. Why an alternating current can produce an electromagnetic wave that "flies away" comes down to Maxwell's equations, which Chapter 8 discusses in detail.

## 5.1.6 Summary: A Five-Stage Relay

Looking back, the division of labor along the way is as follows:

1. **Baseband**: Uses 1024-QAM to encode bits into amplitude and phase, with each symbol carrying 10 bits.
2. **Transceiver**: Uses mixing to shift the signal up to the 3.5 GHz n78 band.
3. **Power amplifier**: Amplifies the signal thousands of times, to about 200 milliwatts (23 dBm).
4. **Envelope tracking chip**: Supplies power dynamically according to the signal contour, clearly improving amplifier efficiency.
5. **Antenna gaps**: Convert high-frequency current into electromagnetic waves sent to the base station at the speed of light.

Every stage balances two goals: sending more versus sending reliably, and reaching farther versus saving power and staying cool. RF is the fastest-moving stream of energy in a phone.
