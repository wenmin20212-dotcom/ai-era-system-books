# 5.4 The Dormant Pulse with the Screen Off: Microamp-Level "Suspended Animation" and Wake-Up

Before bed, you set your phone face-down on the nightstand. The screen goes dark and the room falls quiet. When you pick it up the next morning, the battery has dropped only a little. Yet throughout the night it was never truly "off." WeChat messages still came in, calls could still be answered, and when you raise your hand, the screen lights up on its own.

This shows that a phone with its screen off is in a kind of "suspended animation": most of its organs stop working, while a few sentries stay on duty. The previous sections dealt with high-intensity flows such as RF, fast charging, and photography. This section looks at the opposite end: the weakest flow in the device, which is also the one that lasts longest, namely the current drawn in standby.

## 5.4.1 Once the Screen Goes Dark, Who Gets "Switched Off"?

Start with an analogy. Late at night in an office building, the lights, air conditioning, and elevators in the office areas are all turned off. Only one small lamp still burns in the security office by the entrance. The guard watches the monitors, and only when someone swipes a card at the door does he turn on the lights for one floor.

A phone does something similar when its screen goes off. Once the screen is dark, the **power rails** feeding several of the biggest power consumers are cut completely. A power rail is a supply line that the power management chip assigns to a particular group of circuits. You can think of it as a zoned circuit breaker in the building. Three main categories are cut off:

- **The high-performance compute cores of the main SoC**: the big and mid-sized CPU cores that normally run apps and compute the interface;
- **The GPU (Graphics Processing Unit)**: it renders the display, so it has nothing to do while the screen is dark;
- **The power supply to the screen itself**: the display panel and its driver circuitry are among the most power-hungry parts of the phone.

The word here is "cut off," not "throttled." Throttling is like dimming a light. Only opening the breaker stops the power draw entirely. Even when digital circuits perform no computation, their transistors still leak a tiny amount of current, and the larger the chip, the more that leakage adds up. So for standby, powering down an entire circuit block saves far more energy than letting it "idle at low speed."

## 5.4.2 The Always-On Hardware Island: The "Security Office" Inside the SoC

If the main system has lost power, what detects you raising the phone to wake the screen? The answer lies in a special region inside the SoC called the **Always-On Island**.

The word "island" fits well. The SoC is divided into many power domains, each of which can be switched on and off independently. The always-on island is a small domain whose power is never interrupted. It is isolated from the powered-down areas around it, like an island running on its own generator in a city hit by a nighttime blackout.

Stationed on this island is a low-power microcontroller, usually called the **Sensor Hub**. Its structure is much simpler than the main CPU's: it has a low clock speed and few transistors, and it works with a narrow focus:

1. It continuously reads data from the **accelerometer** and the **gyroscope**. The accelerometer senses the phone's acceleration in each direction and the direction of gravity, and the gyroscope senses the phone's angular velocity as it rotates;
2. It strings this data into curves that change over time and searches them for the signature of a "raise to wake" motion;
3. Only when it detects a **rising edge** in the curve characteristic of the raising rotation, meaning the values jump sharply from a steady state in a pattern that matches "flipping the phone from lying flat to facing you," does it send a signal to wake the main system and light the screen.

Throughout this process, the Sensor Hub draws current only at the **microamp (μA) level**. One microamp is one-thousandth of a milliamp. When the phone runs a game, its current is often measured in amps or hundreds of milliamps. By comparison, the Sensor Hub is like the small desk lamp in the security office set against the central air conditioning of the whole building.

Why not let the main CPU watch the sensors itself? Because sensor data streams in without pause. If the main CPU were woken for every bit of data, large parts of its circuitry would have to power up, restore their state, process the data, and power down again, over and over, and each wake-up carries a considerable cost of its own. Handing the job to a small, dedicated microcontroller that filters around the clock and reports only the events that matter is the way to stay both responsive and power-efficient. This is the energy-efficiency philosophy of "dedicated chips for dedicated tasks," discussed in Chapter 3, applied to the standby scenario.

## 5.4.3 The 5G Baseband's "Napping" Rhythm: Discontinuous Reception

One question remains: why can a phone still receive calls with the screen off? When a call comes in, the base station broadcasts a message over the **Paging Channel**, essentially saying, "Phone with such-and-such number, you have an incoming call, please respond." The phone has to hear this broadcast in order to pick up the call.

But if the baseband chip kept its receiver on all the time, the RF path would stay active for long stretches and the battery would drain quickly. So the 5G baseband uses a mechanism called **Discontinuous Reception**, or **DRX** for short.

The idea behind DRX can be explained with a scenario. You are waiting for an important letter, but you don't need to stand by the mailbox all day. You just agree with the mail carrier to check at set intervals. The phone and the base station make exactly this kind of "agreement":

- For **the vast majority of the time** (the exact proportion depends on the DRX cycle configured by the network), the baseband is in deep sleep and its receive circuitry is essentially shut off;
- Every **few hundred milliseconds**, the baseband wakes briefly for **a few milliseconds**, turns on its receiver, and listens to the paging channel for any message addressed to it;
- If there is none, it goes straight back to sleep. If there is one, it wakes fully, establishes a connection, and hands the call or data to the main system.

The base station knows the rhythm on which the phone wakes and sends paging messages at the corresponding moments, so the clocks on both sides must stay in sync. This rhythm also brings a noticeable trade-off: the longer the interval between wake-ups, the more power is saved, but the later a message gets heard. A delay of a few hundred milliseconds is practically imperceptible when answering a call, and in exchange standby time is greatly extended.

## 5.4.4 Comparing the Two Pulse Lines

Here are the two "sentries" that keep working while the screen is off, side by side:

| Aspect | Sensor Hub (Always-On Island) | 5G Baseband DRX |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Always-on hardware island inside the SoC | Baseband modem circuitry |
| What it listens to | Accelerometer and gyroscope data | Base station paging channel |
| Mode of operation | Low-power reading and computation around the clock | Deep sleep most of the time, with brief periodic wake-ups |
| Time scale | Runs continuously | Wakes for a few milliseconds every few hundred milliseconds |
| Power level | Microamp level (μA) | Extremely low average power, achieved by drastically cutting active time |
| Wake-up condition | Detects the rising edge of the raising-rotation signature | A message for this phone appears on the paging channel |
| Power-saving approach | A small chip stands guard in place of a big one | "Intermittent" operation in time replaces "always on" |

The two use different means. One relies on **division of labor in space**, letting the smallest circuit stand watch. The other relies on **slicing in time**, letting the circuitry that must work go on duty only at key moments. A phone's standby battery life is essentially the combined result of these two approaches.

## 5.4.5 Summary: The Key to Saving Power Is "Sleep When It's Time to Sleep"

A phone with its screen off looks still, but inside it keeps up a low, steady pulse. The Sensor Hub on the always-on island keeps watch on microamps of current, the baseband breathes in and out to the rhythm of DRX, and everything else has its breaker thrown. As soon as a raise-to-wake motion or an incoming page appears, this system can quickly bring the whole phone back to life.

The earlier sections of this chapter showed surges of energy and data at peak moments. This section shows that system design must also deal with the troughs.
