# 5.5 System Metrics Compared Across the Four Miniature Flows

Lay a phone flat on a desk and it looks perfectly still. But beneath that slab of glass and metal, four kinds of "flow" coexist, competing for space, energy, and thermal headroom: electromagnetic waves moving in and out of the antennas, electric charge pouring in through the charging cable, photoelectric signals surging out the instant the shutter is pressed, and the faint current that keeps running even after the screen goes dark. The previous four sections covered each of these in turn. This section places all four in a single table to compare their magnitudes and bottlenecks, and then examines how the system makes trade-offs when they collide.

## 5.5.1 The Four Flows at a Glance

| Flow Type | Medium and Carrier | Typical Rate and Operating Frequency | Typical System Power / Electrical Magnitude | Physical Bottlenecks and Core Challenges |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RF electromagnetic flow | Microwaves in free space and antenna microstrip lines | 3.5GHz (5G Sub-6G); 28GHz (mmWave) | Transmit power about 200mW (23dBm); receive sensitivity down to -100dBm, an extremely weak signal | Attenuation from a hand blocking the antenna, spatial penetration loss of high-frequency signals, conversion heat in the power amplifier (PA) |
| 100-watt pulsed charge flow | Copper busbars, charge-pump capacitors, and lithium ions | Charging current 6A–12A; switching frequency about 500kHz | Input voltage 20V DC; cell charging power 60W–120W | Battery temperature must stay below 45℃ to stay clear of thermal runaway; connector contact resistance; aging caused by lithium plating |
| Computational photography photocurrent | Photodiode charge and differential buses | MIPI transfer rate about 2.5Gbps/lane; billions of tensor operations per trigger | Burst power 5W–8W, lasting several hundred milliseconds | Night-scene noise and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR); burst-shooting slowdown caused by computational heat |
| Sleep-standby microcurrent | DC on dedicated internal power rails | Sensor sampling rate 50Hz–200Hz; baseband paging as pulses of tens of milliseconds | Whole-device standby draw of a few milliamps (mA); Sensor Hub draw of a few hundred microamps | Ceiling on standby battery life (48+ hours), leakage current suppression, blocking false wake-ups |

## 5.5.2 Reading the Table: Four Differences in Magnitude

**First, the frequency span is enormous.** RF operates in the GHz range (billions of oscillations per second), the charge pump switches at about 500kHz, and sensor sampling runs at only tens to a couple hundred hertz. These differ by several orders of magnitude and correspond to three distinct kinds of engineering problems: at GHz the issue is electromagnetic propagation and loss; at kHz it is switching loss and capacitive energy storage; at the Hz level the question is how to keep continuous "watch" using the least possible power.

**Second, the power span is even larger.** From the few hundred microamps of the Sensor Hub to the hundred-plus watts of charging, the difference is roughly a millionfold. Think of it this way: the sleep current is like a single lit match in a room, while fast charging is like switching on several space heaters at once. A single battery and a single power management circuit must cover this entire range, which is one of the hardest aspects of power design.

**Third, the temporal patterns differ.** RF transmission/reception and charging are continuous flows; computational photography is a short burst, ramping to 5W–8W within a few hundred milliseconds before quickly dropping back; sleep current is mostly low-level, with the baseband waking for tens of milliseconds at intervals, forming intermittent pulses. Continuous flows mainly cause heat buildup, while burst flows mainly cause instantaneous current surges and voltage droop.

**Fourth, the ultimate constraint is mostly heat.** Look at the last column: PA heating, the battery's 45℃ red line, and computational heat during burst shooting are all heat-related. Sleep current produces little heat, but the "leakage" it must deal with is still an energy loss. The bottlenecks of all four flows ultimately come down to energy and heat, which is the starting point for the discussion of scheduling and thermal control in Chapter 6.

## 5.5.3 When All Four Flows Happen at Once

Taken individually, each flow has mature solutions. The difficulty arises when they stack up. Imagine this scenario: you're in an underground parking garage with a weak signal, playing a demanding game while charging from a 100W car charger.

- **RF side**: When the signal is weak, the base station asks the phone to raise its transmit power, pushing the PA close to full load and noticeably increasing heat; your grip on the device also blocks the antenna, requiring antenna switching or retuning.
- **Charge side**: The charge pump is continuously moving tens of watts of energy, and the cell itself is heating up.
- **Compute side**: The game keeps the SoC under sustained high load, producing heat that is more persistent than the short bursts of photography.

Three streams of heat are concentrated in a sealed body just a few millimeters thick, and the battery temperature gradually approaches 45℃. At this point the system has no choice but to sacrifice some functions, typically in this order: **first reduce charging power**, because battery safety comes first and users can generally accept slower charging; **then lower the processor frequency**, which drops the game's frame rate; **and finally dim the screen**. Specific strategies vary by manufacturer, but the logic is the same: protect the battery and personal safety first, then consider user experience.

Conversely, when the phone sits on the desk with its screen off, one flow can also transform into another. A WeChat voice call comes in; the baseband receives the signal via a paging pulse; the wake-up chain propagates from the microamp-level Sensor Hub all the way to the application processor; the motor vibrates, the screen lights up, and whole-device power jumps by several orders of magnitude within tens of milliseconds. The quality of sleep design shows in two things: waking promptly when it should, and not being falsely woken when it shouldn't.

## 5.5.5 Chapter Summary and Hands-On Exploration Tasks

This chapter followed four flows from start to finish: data packets becoming microwaves in the air, hundred-watt power delivered into the lithium-ion battery through the charge pump, photons entering the compute units over the MIPI bus, and the system standing watch on microamp-level current after the screen goes dark. They differ enormously in frequency, power, and temporal pattern, yet their limits all converge on energy and heat.

### 5.5.5.1 Hands-On Exploration Tasks
1. Open your phone's battery usage statistics and compare how much charge a night of standby uses versus an hour of gaming, to get a feel for the gap between microcurrent and high load.
2. While fast charging, touch the back of the phone and note which area heats up first; then play a game while charging and watch whether the displayed charging speed drops.
3. In a place with a weak signal (such as an elevator or basement), stay on a call for several minutes, notice how the device's temperature changes, and explain the cause in terms of PA heating.

These phenomena show that a phone needs hardware and software mechanisms to continuously coordinate these flows. The next chapter discusses how the operating system and hardware make scheduling decisions between heat and power consumption.
