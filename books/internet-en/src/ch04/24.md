# 4.4 Internet Governance: Standards Bodies, Address Allocation, and Domain Name Management

Type a web address ending in ".cn" or ".com" into your browser's address bar and press Enter. Three things happen without your noticing. The IP address your phone receives doesn't collide with the address of some server on the other side of the planet. The name gets translated into exactly one correct address. And the devices at both ends speak the same protocol. It all seems to happen on its own, but behind it lie three questions: Who sets the protocols? Who hands out the addresses? Who manages the names? The internet has no "headquarters," yet each of these three tasks has its own set of institutions and processes with clearly divided responsibilities. Together, these arrangements are usually called Internet Governance. This section covers only the three pillars that are most technical and closest to everyday internet use: standards, numbers, and domain names.

## 4.4.1 Who Makes the Rules: The IETF and RFCs

The previous chapters have mentioned TCP, IP, DNS, and HTTP again and again. Most of their official specifications are published as RFCs (Requests for Comments), which are produced under the leadership of the IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force). The IETF differs from a typical international organization in several ways:

- **No membership.** Anyone can subscribe to the mailing lists and join the discussions. Engineers speak as individuals, not as representatives of their employers.
- **"Rough consensus and running code."** A widely quoted IETF saying goes roughly: "We reject kings, presidents, and voting. We believe in rough consensus and running code." A working group adopts a proposal when its members broadly agree and when interoperable implementations exist. A simple vote does not decide the matter.
- **Documents are numbered and never revised.** Once an RFC is published, its content is never changed. An update means publishing a new RFC with a new number that states which older documents it "updates" or "obsoletes." So when you look up a standard, check whether a later RFC has superseded it.

Several related bodies work alongside the IETF. The IAB (Internet Architecture Board) oversees the overall architecture. ISOC (the Internet Society) gives the IETF its organizational and legal home. Many web-level standards, such as the HTML and CSS specifications, are developed mainly by the W3C (World Wide Web Consortium) and WHATWG. Lower-level communication standards, such as those for fiber optics and cellular networks, mostly come from bodies like the IEEE, 3GPP, and the ITU. These are covered in more depth in the related volumes of this series.

You can think of it this way: the IETF is something like an "open-source traffic code." Anyone can help draft it and anyone can download and read it for free. Device manufacturers and software developers each implement it on their own, and as a result their products work together.

## 4.4.2 Who Assigns the Numbers: IANA and the Five Regional Registries

Addresses and the various numbers used in protocols must be globally unique, so there has to be a "master directory." That role belongs to IANA (Internet Assigned Numbers Authority). Today the IANA functions are carried out by PTI, an affiliate of ICANN. IANA manages three kinds of things:

- the top-level allocation of IP addresses and autonomous system numbers (AS numbers; see Section 4.2)
- the DNS root zone
- the registries of protocol parameters, such as port numbers

IANA does not hand addresses directly to your internet provider. Instead, it allocates large blocks to five RIRs (Regional Internet Registries), and the addresses are delegated downward level by level:

| Level | Organization | Coverage or Role |
|---|---|---|
| Global | IANA | Manages the entire address pool and allocates large blocks to each region |
| Regional | AFRINIC | Africa |
| Regional | APNIC | Asia-Pacific (including China) |
| Regional | ARIN | North America |
| Regional | LACNIC | Latin America and the Caribbean |
| Regional | RIPE NCC | Europe, the Middle East, and parts of Central Asia |
| National or local | NIR / LIR | Some countries have a national registry, such as CNNIC in China; internet providers act as local registries |
| End user | Providers assign addresses to homes and businesses | The public address your home router receives |

No single organization sets the rules for address allocation on its own. Instead, the community within each RIR discusses proposals openly and turns the results into "policy."

The IPv4 address space holds only about 4.3 billion addresses. Public sources widely record that IANA allocated its last few blocks of IPv4 addresses to the RIRs in 2011. After that, the regions one by one moved to a stage where they could allocate only small amounts or put requests on waiting lists for reclaimed addresses. This shortage is why NAT came into such widespread use and why IPv6 had to be rolled out, as Section 7.1 of Chapter 7 explains in detail.

## 4.4.3 Who Manages the Names: ICANN, Registries, and Registrars

Domain names are also managed through layers of delegated authority. ICANN (Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers) is a nonprofit headquartered in the United States. It coordinates the creation of top-level domains and sets policy for the domain name industry. ICANN uses a "multistakeholder" model. Governments, businesses, the technical community, and civil society can all join the discussion through their respective supporting organizations or advisory committees, one of which is the Governmental Advisory Committee.

Bringing a domain name into existence involves roughly three kinds of roles:

1. **Registry:** operates the database and authoritative servers for a particular top-level domain. For example, .com is run by an American company (Verisign, at the time of writing), and .cn is run by CNNIC.
2. **Registrar:** the company that sells and manages domain names for users. When you buy a domain on a domain provider's website, this is who you are dealing with.
3. **Registrant:** the individual or organization that purchases the domain name.

Top-level domains fall into two broad categories:

- **Generic top-level domains:** for example .com and .org, plus the large number of new suffixes added in recent years.
- **Country and region code top-level domains:** for example .cn, .jp, and .uk. Their management policies are usually set by local organizations under local rules.

DNS resolution starts at the root zone. Changes to the contents of the root zone file are handled by the IANA functions. The root servers, by contrast, are operated by about a dozen different organizations. Publicly they go by 13 names, lettered A through M. Behind each name, anycast technology deploys a large number of instances around the world, so the actual number of servers is far greater than 13. These details come up again in Section 6.4, which covers how DNS works.

## 4.4.4 Coordination Without Headquarters: Why the Work Is Divided This Way

Look at the three pillars together and you'll notice something they share: **no one is the "owner of the internet."**

- The IETF writes standards but has no power to force anyone to implement them.
- IANA maintains the number registries, but the actual allocation depends on policies set by the RIR communities.
- ICANN coordinates domain names, but each country's country code top-level domain is managed locally.

What actually keeps the system running is the benefit everyone gets from voluntarily following the same rules. You can talk to other networks only if you follow the protocols, and you can be found only if your address is unique.

This arrangement has its controversies. For example, ICANN's historical relationship with the U.S. government drew international attention for many years. When the transition of IANA stewardship was completed in 2016, oversight passed to the global multistakeholder community. Venues such as the UN-backed Internet Governance Forum have continued to discuss what role national governments should play in cyberspace. Issues like data protection and content regulation belong mainly to national law and fall outside the technical scope of this book.

Back to the web address from the beginning. You can open it without a hitch for three reasons:

- The protocol specifications come from publicly available RFCs.
- The addresses were passed down from IANA through APNIC to your internet provider.
- The name is on record in the registry's database.

The layered model lets each protocol do its own job. Negotiation between autonomous systems lets traffic flow across networks. And the governance system lets billions of devices worldwide share the same set of "numbers" and the same "language."
