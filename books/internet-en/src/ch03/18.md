# 3.4 Reliable Delivery: Packet Loss, Retransmission, and Congestion Control

It's eight in the evening, and you open a web page on your phone. The page appears line by line, the images crisp and clear, with not a single piece missing and not a single character wrong. Yet at that very moment, your home router's Wi-Fi is suffering interference from your neighbor's signal, the uplink leaving your neighborhood is being squeezed by hundreds or thousands of households at once, and some distant router, its buffer full, is simply dropping the packets it can't process in time. The network itself does not guarantee that every packet will arrive, and yet the page you see is complete. The gap between those two facts is exactly what "reliable transmission" exists to fill.

## 3.4.1 Why Networks Lose Things

As the previous section explained, routers only make a "best effort" to forward packets to the next hop. They guarantee neither delivery nor that the sender will be told when something goes wrong. The common causes of packet loss fall into a few categories:

| Cause | Where It Happens | Everyday Symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Queue overflow | Buffers in routers and switches | Slow speeds and choppy video at peak hours |
| Wireless signal errors | Wi-Fi, cellular networks | Pages loading in fits and starts when you're far from the router |
| Line or equipment failure | Fiber cables, ports, line cards | Certain websites suddenly won't load |
| Corrupted packets discarded by checksum | Any link | Usually unnoticed by users, masked by retransmission |

The most common of these is the first. Packets arrive at a router like traffic, and when the outgoing link can't keep up for the moment, they wait in a queue in the buffer. Once the queue is full, later packets can only be dropped. The mathematics behind this phenomenon is covered in detail in the "Queueing Theory" section of Chapter 8.

Besides being lost, packets can also arrive **out of order** (a later packet arrives first because it took a different route) or be **duplicated** (the same packet is delivered twice). A reliable transmission mechanism must handle all three problems at once.

## 3.4.2 Putting Reliability at the Ends: The TCP Approach

The Internet's approach is to keep the routers in the middle simple and hand responsibility for "guaranteed delivery" to the computers at either end of the communication. The main protocol that shoulders this responsibility is the **Transmission Control Protocol** (TCP). Its original standard was RFC 793, published in 1981, and the current version is consolidated in RFC 9293. Browsing the web, sending and receiving email, and downloading files mostly run on top of TCP.

You can think of TCP as mailing a manuscript with numbered pages:

1. **Numbering**: The sender labels each segment of data with a sequence number. The receiver uses these numbers to put out-of-order content back in order and to recognize duplicates.
2. **Acknowledgment**: After receiving data, the receiver sends back an acknowledgment (ACK), meaning "I've received everything up to page such-and-such."
3. **Checksum**: Each data segment carries a checksum; if the content is corrupted, it is treated as never received.
4. **Retransmission**: If the sender waits too long without receiving an acknowledgment for a segment, it assumes the segment was lost and sends it again.

In this way, even if the underlying network loses, reorders, or duplicates data, what reaches the application is still a complete, ordered stream of data.

## 3.4.3 When to Retransmit

The hard part of retransmission is deciding "how long to wait." Wait too briefly, and you resend a packet that is actually still on its way, wasting bandwidth; wait too long, and the user is left staring anxiously at a blank page.

TCP detects packet loss in two ways:

- **Timeout retransmission**: The sender continuously measures how long a round trip takes, known as the round-trip time (RTT), and uses it to dynamically calculate a timeout value (the RTO; the calculation method is given in RFC 6298). If no acknowledgment arrives within that time, the data is retransmitted. The RTT to a domestic website is typically a few milliseconds to a few tens of milliseconds, while transoceanic access is often on the order of one or two hundred milliseconds, so the timeout cannot be a fixed value.
- **Fast retransmission**: If the receiver has received segments 5, 6, and 7 but is missing segment 4, it will repeatedly send acknowledgments saying "I've only received up through segment 3." When the sender sees three such duplicate acknowledgments in a row, it doesn't wait for a timeout; it immediately resends segment 4.

It's like a courier who hears the recipient say three times in a row "I'm still missing package number 4" and promptly sends a replacement, rather than waiting a week before doing anything.

## 3.4.4 Congestion Control: Don't Jam the Road

Knowing how to retransmit isn't enough. Imagine that at peak hours everyone's computer notices packet loss and starts frantically resending—the network would only become more congested, lose even more packets, and eventually nobody could get anything through. This actually happened on the early Internet around 1986 and became known as "congestion collapse." Afterward, researchers including Van Jacobson proposed congestion control algorithms, which became a standard part of TCP (the current specification is RFC 5681).

The core idea is: **treat packet loss as a sign of network congestion and slow down proactively**. The sender maintains a "congestion window," which indicates the maximum amount of data it can send in a row without waiting for acknowledgments:

- **Slow start**: When a connection is first established, the window is small. It grows with each acknowledgment received, roughly doubling every RTT, quickly probing for available bandwidth.
- **Congestion avoidance**: Once the window reaches a certain size, it switches to growing only a little per RTT, probing cautiously.
- **Slowing down on loss**: When loss is detected, the window is shrunk sharply; a common approach is to cut it in half.

This "increase slowly, decrease quickly" strategy lets thousands of connections share the same link spontaneously, without any central coordination. When you download a file, the speed first climbs and then fluctuates around some level—that is the visible expression of this process.

Congestion control algorithms are still evolving. At the time of writing, many operating systems use an algorithm called CUBIC by default (RFC 9438), while BBR, proposed by Google, tries to detect congestion by measuring bandwidth and latency rather than relying solely on packet loss, and is already used in some services. Which one is used depends on the configuration of the operating system and service provider; there is no single standard across the whole network.

## 3.4.5 Not Every Application Needs "Absolute Reliability"

Reliability has a cost: waiting for retransmissions means delay. For video calls and online games, an old frame that arrives half a second late is worse than simply skipping it. Such applications often use the **User Datagram Protocol** (UDP), which only sends data, with no acknowledgments or retransmissions, leaving the trade-offs to the application itself.

| Comparison | TCP | UDP |
|---|---|---|
| Guarantees delivery and order | Yes | No |
| Congestion control | Built in | Must be handled by the application |
| Typical uses | Web pages, email, file downloads | Real-time audio and video, DNS queries, games |

It's worth noting that the new-generation QUIC protocol is also built on top of UDP, yet it reimplements reliable transmission and congestion control internally. Chapter 7 will cover it in detail.

Back to that fully displayed web page from the beginning: every one of its bytes may have gone through numbering, acknowledgment, timeouts, retransmission, and slowdowns. It is this quiet "bookkeeping" by the computers at both ends that allows an unreliable network to deliver reliable results. Now that data can arrive intact, the next question is: how do applications such as web pages, email, and video use this channel to provide services to us?
