# 3.3 Forwarding and Routing: How Packets Find Their Way

Open a command line on your computer at home, type a traceroute command (called tracert on Windows) followed by the domain name of a website you visit often, and a dozen or so addresses will appear on the screen, line by line. The first line is usually the address of your home router, something like 192.168.1.1. Next come devices in your internet service provider's facilities. After that, the addresses grow less and less familiar, and some lines show nothing but a few asterisks. Each line is a router the packet passed through on its way. In networking, each such step is called a "hop."

A packet leaving your computer for a server hundreds or even thousands of kilometers away passes through a dozen or more of these devices. No single router knows the whole route. Each one decides only "who do I hand this to next?" This section explains how this hop-by-hop relay actually works.

## 3.3.1 Two Jobs: Forwarding and Routing

When engineers talk about "how a packet finds its way," they split the question into two distinct jobs:

| | Forwarding | Routing |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | Which interface should this packet go out of right now? | Overall, how should traffic reach each destination? |
| Timescale | Done for every packet, in nanoseconds to microseconds | Updated only when the network changes, in seconds to minutes |
| Basis | Looking up a table that has already been computed | Exchanging information with other routers and computing paths |
| Analogy | A driver reading a road sign at an intersection and turning | The transport authority surveying roads and putting up the signs |

Think of it this way. Forwarding is the driver at the intersection: one glance at the sign, turn the wheel, no need to think about the big picture. Routing is the people behind the scenes who survey the roads and put up the signs. When a road opens or a road is cut off, they change the signs. Engineers often call the first the "data plane" and the second the "control plane." In high-end routers, forwarding is handled by specialized chips, while route computation is handled by software running on a processor. The two work separately, which is one reason a single router can handle an enormous number of packets every second.

## 3.3.2 The Routing Table: The Sign at the Intersection

Every router contains a routing table. Each entry holds roughly three things: a destination network, a next-hop address, and the interface to send the packet out of. The "destination network" is not a list of individual addresses but a range of addresses, written using the prefixes introduced in our discussion of IP addresses. For example, 203.0.113.0/24 stands for the 256 addresses beginning with 203.0.113.

A home router's routing table is very simple. It has roughly two kinds of entries:

- For destinations on the home local network (for example, 192.168.1.0/24), send the packet straight out the internal port to the device in question;
- For every other destination, hand the packet to the equipment on the service provider's side.

The second entry is called the default route, written 0.0.0.0/0, meaning "anything we don't recognize goes here." Your home router, your phone, and your computer can send packets into the internet with a single default route. They don't need to know what the rest of the world looks like.

The deeper you go into the network, the larger the routing tables get. Routers at the core of the internet that do not rely on a default route must keep track of every address prefix announced anywhere in the world. According to public routing statistics, at the time of writing the global IPv4 routing table has reached the order of a million entries.

## 3.3.3 Longest Prefix Match: Choosing the Most Specific Entry

A destination address often falls within the range of several entries at once. Suppose a table contains both 203.0.0.0/8 and 203.0.113.0/24, and the destination address is 203.0.113.25. Both entries match. The rule is to choose the entry with the longest prefix, which is the one covering the smallest and most specific range. This is called longest prefix match.

It works the same way as sorting mail. When an envelope lists the province, city, district, and street, the sorter goes by the most detailed level available rather than just the province. The default route 0.0.0.0/0 has a prefix length of zero, so every address matches it. But it is always the last resort, used only when no more specific entry exists.

Once a matching entry is found, the router does a few more small things. It decrements the "Time To Live" (TTL) field in the packet header by one. If the TTL reaches zero, the router discards the packet and notifies the sender, which keeps packets from circling the network forever. The router then re-encapsulates the packet according to the requirements of the link on the outgoing interface, for example by putting on the hardware address of the next-hop device, and sends it on its way. The traceroute command from the start of this section makes clever use of TTL. It sends out probe packets with a TTL of 1, then 2, then 3, and so on, so that each router along the path "identifies itself" when it drops a probe. That is how you get to see a path. Don't be alarmed by asterisks either. Most likely they just mean a router has been configured not to respond to this kind of probe.

## 3.3.4 Where Routing Tables Come From

Road signs don't appear out of nowhere. Entries in a routing table come mainly from three sources:

1. **Directly connected routes**: Whatever network segment is configured on one of the router's interfaces, the router automatically knows where that segment is.
2. **Static routes**: Entered by hand by an administrator. The default route on a home router is usually of this kind, or else pushed down automatically by the service provider.
3. **Dynamic routes**: Computed automatically as routers exchange information with one another using routing protocols.

Dynamic routing is the key to the internet's ability to adapt on its own. Once a network grows beyond a certain size, manual maintenance cannot keep up with the changes. When a fiber-optic cable is accidentally dug up, neighboring routers detect the failure through the protocol and compute a detour within seconds to minutes. At most, users notice a brief stutter.

Routing protocols fall into two broad categories. Inside a network managed by a single service provider or organization, interior gateway protocols are used. Common examples are OSPF (Open Shortest Path First) and IS-IS, which aim to find the shortest, fastest path on a known map of the network. Between the networks of different organizations, the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) is used. Its first concern is not "shortest" but the business agreements and administrative policies between networks. We will explain why there are two such different logics, and why BGP is called a "weak point" of the internet, layer by layer: autonomous systems in Chapter 4, the flow of routing information in Chapter 5, and the mechanics of BGP in Chapter 6. The graph theory behind shortest-path algorithms is left for Chapter 8.

## 3.3.5 Summary: A Relay Without Central Command

Look back at those dozen or so lines of traceroute output, and you can understand them this way. Behind each line is a router that looks only at the packet header, consults only one table, and cares only about the next step. The contents of that table are maintained continuously in the background by routing protocols. The internet as a whole has no central dispatch center. A global path is the result of countless local decisions passed along like a baton. This design lets the network keep growing and lets traffic route around local failures. The price is that paths are not always optimal, and they can be thrown off by bad routing information.

A packet that has found its way is still not guaranteed a safe journey. Routers queue packets and drop them when the queues fill up, and different packets may take different paths and arrive out of order. How reliable transmission can be built on top of such a "best-effort" network is the question the next section sets out to answer.
