# 9.1 Using AI to Understand Networks: Making Sense of Diagnostic Commands and Packet Captures

It is nine o'clock at night, and your video suddenly starts buffering. You open a command line on your computer, type `ping` as some website suggested, and a string of numbers scrolls across the screen: "time=12ms," "time=230ms," "Request timed out." What do these numbers tell you? Is the problem your home router, your internet service provider, or the video site itself?

In the past, reading output like this took experience. Now you have a helper: copy the output to an AI assistant, and it can explain it line by line and suggest what to test next. But whether the AI's explanation is correct depends on whether the material you give it is complete, and on whether you can judge its claims using what you learned in the previous eight chapters. This section covers how to hand diagnostic commands and packet captures to an AI, and how to check its answers.

## 9.1.1 Getting to Know Four Kinds of "Health Reports"

Section 7.5 of Chapter 7 introduced common measurement tools. Below they are reorganized by "the question each one answers," so you can refer to this table when handing their output to an AI:

| Tool | Question it answers | Typical output elements | Related knowledge in this book |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ipconfig` (Windows) / `ifconfig` or `ip addr` (macOS/Linux) | What address did my device get? | IP address, subnet mask, default gateway, DNS servers | 3.1 Addressing, 5.2 DHCP and NAT |
| `nslookup` / `dig` | What address does this domain name resolve to? | Server queried, records returned, TTL | 3.2, 6.4 DNS |
| `ping` | Can the target be reached, and how long does the round trip take? | Round-trip time (RTT), packet loss rate | 6.1 Best effort, 8.2 Queuing |
| `traceroute` / `tracert` | Which routers did packets pass through along the way? | Per-hop addresses and latency, asterisks | 3.3 Routing, 2.3 Backbone networks |

There is also a more detailed kind of material called a **packet capture**: a record of every packet passing through your network interface, one by one. A common graphical tool for this is Wireshark. A packet capture is like a frame-by-frame recording. It lets you see DNS queries, the TCP three-way handshake, the TLS handshake, retransmissions, and other details, which correspond directly to the mechanisms covered in Chapter 6.

These four kinds of tools are listed from simplest to most complex. Troubleshooting usually follows the same order: first confirm you have an address, then confirm names resolve, then check whether you can reach the target, and finally look at which segment of the path is causing trouble.

## 9.1.2 Handing Material to an AI: Three Key Points

For the same `ping` output, the quality of the AI's explanation differs greatly depending on whether you paste only the numbers or also include background. Keep three points in mind when asking.

**First, describe the situation.** State your device type, how you connect (fiber broadband plus Wi-Fi, or mobile data), when the symptoms started, and whether one website or all websites are affected. Chapter 5 explained that traffic has tides, so "it only slows down during evening peak hours" is itself an important clue.

**Second, paste the complete original output rather than paraphrasing it.** If you just say "ping is sometimes high," the AI can only speak in generalities. The complete output includes the packet loss rate and the minimum, average, and maximum latency, which lets the AI tell "occasional jitter" apart from "sustained congestion."

**Third, redact sensitive information before submitting.** The output may contain your public IP address, device name, or home network name, and a packet capture file may even contain sensitive data such as cookies. Before pasting anything to an AI, replace the last few digits of your public address. When capturing packets, capture only the traffic relevant to the problem, or submit only summary statistics rather than uploading a full capture file. Private addresses (such as 192.168.x.x) are reused in many homes around the world (see Section 5.2), so the risk of exposing them is low, and you can keep them to make the analysis easier.

A good question might read like this:

> I have fiber broadband at home, and my computer connects to the router over Wi-Fi. For the past week, videos have stuttered every evening from 8 to 11 p.m., but daytime is fine. Below are the results of pinging my router's gateway and pinging a commonly used website, both run at 9 p.m. Please help me judge whether the problem is more likely in my home Wi-Fi, my ISP's access network, or somewhere farther away, and suggest what I should test next. (Two complete outputs attached)

What you are asking the AI to do here is a **comparative analysis**: testing both "to the gateway" and "to the internet" at the same time splits the path into a home segment and an external segment. This is one way the layering idea from Chapter 4 applies to troubleshooting.

## 9.1.3 Understanding the AI's Interpretation: Checking It Against the Framework

The AI might respond: "Latency to the gateway is stable at a few milliseconds, which shows your home Wi-Fi is basically fine. Latency to the internet rises noticeably in the evening and there is packet loss, so the more likely cause is congestion in the access network or upstream links during peak hours." Whether this reasoning holds up is something you can check yourself using what this book has covered:

- **Where the rising latency comes from.** Section 8.2 explained that the closer a link gets to full load, the faster queuing delay grows. Latency jumping during the evening peak while daytime is normal fits the signature of congestion. If latency is high all day, you should consider other causes.
- **Asterisks in traceroute, and "one hop in the middle with very high latency."** Many routers give diagnostic packets low priority or simply don't respond to them, so if a hop in the middle shows asterisks or high latency while every hop after it is normal, that usually does not indicate a fault. What really deserves attention is **when every hop from a certain point onward gets worse**. If the AI seizes on a single isolated high value in the middle to draw a conclusion, you should push back.
- **Look at DNS and connectivity separately.** If `nslookup` is slow or fails but accessing the IP address directly works, the problem is in name resolution (Section 6.4). If resolution works but you still can't connect, look at the transport layer and routing.

You can check packet capture interpretations the same way. If the AI says "there are a lot of TCP retransmissions," recall Section 6.2: retransmissions mean segments were lost or acknowledgments were slow to arrive, and congestion control lowers the sending rate as a result. That lines up neatly with the observation that "the video quality automatically drops."

## 9.1.4 The Limits of AI: It Helps, and It Also Makes Mistakes

AI assistants are good at three things: translating obscure fields into plain language, offering several possible explanations based on common patterns, and suggesting what to test next. Their limitations are just as clear:

| What AI tends to do well | Where AI tends to go wrong |
|---|---|
| Explaining what fields mean (TTL, RTT, SYN, ACK, etc.) | Treating a single chance measurement as a general rule |
| Listing common failure types and the order to troubleshoot them | Asserting how a particular ISP or website works internally |
| Generating ideas for comparison tests | Giving command options that don't match your system version |

So treat the AI's conclusions as **hypotheses**, then verify them with new measurements: test again at a different time, test again over a wired connection, test again against a different target website. Alternating between hypothesis and verification is far more reliable than simply accepting an answer. In addition, this book's safety principles apply to conversations with AI as well: diagnose only your own devices and network, do not probe other people's systems, and refuse to carry out any suggestion that involves attack techniques.

## 9.1.5 Summary

Diagnostic commands and packet captures are first-hand evidence of how a network is running. AI can help you read this evidence faster, but judging whether its reading is trustworthy depends on the framework map in your head: addresses, names, routing, transport, and queuing, with each layer serving as a basis for checking.
