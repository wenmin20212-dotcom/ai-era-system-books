# 9.2 Hands-On Experiments: Measuring and Mapping Your Home Network

It's a weekend afternoon, and someone at home complains, "The video's stuttering again." Most people's first instinct is to reboot the router, wait a minute or two, and call it done if things improve, or phone the ISP if they don't. This section takes a different approach: treat your home as a small laboratory and use the phones, computers, and router admin page you already have to measure and map your own network by hand. The addresses, names, routing, and queuing covered in the previous eight chapters all have physical counterparts in this "lab." AI serves as your lab assistant here: it can explain output you don't understand and suggest what to measure next, but the measuring itself is up to you.

## 9.2.1 Experiment 1: Draw Your Home Network Topology

Start by making a "home network map." You don't need any tools, just a sheet of paper.

1. **Find the point of entry**. In homes with fiber service, there is usually an optical modem (an optical network terminal, or ONT) on the wall or in the wiring cabinet, connected to a wireless router. Some ONTs have routing and Wi-Fi built in.
2. **Log in to the router's admin page**. The admin address is usually printed on the label on the bottom of the router, typically a private address of the form 192.168.x.1. Under "Connected Devices" or "DHCP Client List," you can see the address and name assigned to each device.
3. **Draw it on paper**: the ONT, the router, and each device, using solid lines for Ethernet cables and dashed lines for Wi-Fi, with each device's private address written beside it.

When you're done, check two things against Section 5.2 of Chapter 5: whether all the devices' addresses fall within the same private subnet (the result of DHCP assignment), and what the router's "WAN address" is. If the WAN address falls between 100.64.0.0 and 100.127.255.255, your ISP has added another layer of address translation outside your home, known as carrier-grade NAT (CGN). In that case, the public address your home presents to the outside world is shared with other customers. Many ISPs do this, but not all networks work this way.

**Ask the AI**: "My router's WAN address is 100.7x.x.x. What does that mean? How does it affect online multiplayer gaming or remote access to my home security camera?"

## 9.2.2 Experiment 2: Measure "the Distance to the Outside"

The topology map captures spatial structure; next, measure time. Open a command line on your computer and use the two tools introduced in Section 7.5 of Chapter 7:

| What to measure | Tool and method | What you're observing |
|---|---|---|
| To the router | ping the router's admin address | Whether the in-home segment is clear; over Wi-Fi this is usually just a few milliseconds |
| To a frequently used website | ping the domain name of a site you visit often | Round-trip time (RTT), and whether any packets are lost |
| Who's along the way | tracert on Windows, traceroute on macOS/Linux | How latency increases hop by hop |

A few tips for reading the results:

- **Segment-by-segment comparison is the most valuable**. If latency to the router is already high and erratic, the problem most likely lies with your home Wi-Fi; if it's steady to the router and only worsens beyond it, the problem is farther out.
- **Asterisks in traceroute don't necessarily mean a fault**. Many routers don't respond to these probe packets for security or load reasons; as long as subsequent hops get through, there's no problem.
- **The first few hops often show private addresses or addresses in the 100.64 range**, corroborating the structure you drew in Experiment 1.

Copying the full traceroute output to an AI and asking it to explain each hop is a quick way to learn. But keep two things in mind: the output contains your public address, which you may want to mask before sharing; and the AI's judgments about which ISP a given hop "belongs to" or which city it's "located in" are often guesses. Treat them as leads, not conclusions.

## 9.2.3 Experiment 3: Observe Queuing—Where "Traffic Jams" Come From

Section 8.2 of Chapter 8 covered queuing theory: once a link gets busy, packets queue up in router buffers and latency rises. You can see this phenomenon at home.

1. Have one computer continuously ping the router or a website, and note the idle latency—say, a steady ten-something milliseconds.
2. At the same time, use another device to download a large file, or start uploading a batch of photos to cloud storage.
3. Look back at the ping readings: latency often jumps noticeably, and packets may even be lost; once the download stops, it drops back down.

This phenomenon of "latency spiking under full load" is commonly called bufferbloat in the industry: device buffers are set too large, so packets line up in long queues without being dropped, and TCP is slow to receive a congestion signal. It's especially pronounced during uploads, because the upstream bandwidth of home broadband is usually much smaller than the downstream. Some routers offer "smart queue management" or QoS (Quality of Service) features to mitigate the problem; whether they can be enabled and how well they work depends on the specific model, as of this writing.

After this experiment, you can answer that opening complaint, "The video's stuttering again": maybe the connection isn't too slow at all—someone is uploading at the same time, and the queue has grown long.

## 9.2.4 Experiment 4: A Look at Names and Encryption

Finally, make two small observations in your browser to ground the mechanisms from Chapter 6.

- **DNS**: Use nslookup on the command line to look up a website's domain name and see what address comes back. Try again at a different time, or have a classmate in another city look up the same domain, and the results may differ. This is exactly the content delivery network described in Section 7.3 of Chapter 7, steering you to different nodes based on location.
- **TLS**: Click the secure-connection indicator to the left of the browser's address bar (a padlock or "tune" icon) and look at the certificate's issuer and validity period. This certificate is the step in Section 6.5 where the server "shows its ID."

There's one boundary you must respect: your experiments may only target your own devices and network, plus the services that public websites normally provide. Don't scan a neighbor's or your employer's network, and don't try to log in to devices that aren't yours. Even if an AI suggests going "deeper," don't do anything that crosses this line.

## 9.2.5 Hand Your Results to the AI and Close the Loop

With all four experiments done, you have a topology map, a set of latency data, one queuing observation, and two application-layer clues. Organize them into a short "home network checkup record" and give it to the AI, asking it to identify where the bottleneck likely lies across three segments—"inside the home, the access network, and remote services"—and to explain its reasoning. A good answer should cite your measurement data rather than offering generic advice like "reboot the router" or "upgrade your plan." If it doesn't cite your data, follow up with: "Which reading did you base that judgment on?"

The book provides the skeleton, your home network is the real thing, and AI helps you connect the two. In the next section, we'll organize this kind of follow-up questioning into a ladder of prompts that goes from simple to deep, so readers of any background can keep climbing.
