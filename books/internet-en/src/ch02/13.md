# 2.5 Servers, Data Centers, and Content Delivery Networks

Tap on a movie on a video site, and within a few seconds it starts playing. Refresh the home page of a shopping app, and hundreds of product images appear all at once. We tend to say "the data is in the cloud," but the "cloud" is not something up in the sky. It lives in servers stacked in row upon row of racks on the ground, and in cache nodes that may be only a few dozen kilometers from your home. The previous sections followed the cables from your phone all the way to the backbone network and the submarine cables. This section goes to the other end of the journey to see where web pages and videos start out.

## 2.5.1 Servers: Computers Dedicated to "Answering Questions"

A server is best understood by the role it plays, not by what it looks like. Your phone sends a request asking, "Please give me this web page." The server receives the request, finds or generates the corresponding content, and sends it back. The side that sends the request is called the client, and the side that responds is called the server. Chapter 4 will discuss this client–server relationship in detail. For now, let's look at its physical form.

In principle, an ordinary laptop with web server software installed can act as a server. But the machines actually used to provide internet services are specially designed for continuous, long-term operation:

| Aspect | Home Computer | Typical Server |
|---|---|---|
| Form factor | Desktop tower or laptop | Flat rack-mounted chassis, installed in a standard rack |
| Operation | Turned on when needed, off when done | Usually runs nonstop all year round |
| Monitor and keyboard | Essential | Usually not attached; managed remotely over the network |
| Power supply, hard drives | Usually one of each | Often has redundant power supplies; drives configured in redundant arrays |
| Network connection | One network port or Wi-Fi | Multiple high-speed network ports, connected to the data center's switching network |

A large website rarely runs on just one server. Requests from users first arrive at a load balancer, which distributes them across many identical servers behind it. It works much like the ticket machine in a bank lobby: customers don't have to pick a window themselves, because the machine sends each person to an available counter. If one server fails, the load balancer simply stops sending work to it, and users generally don't notice.

## 2.5.2 Data Centers: The "Buildings" Where Servers Live Together

Thousands upon thousands of servers need power, cooling, and network connectivity, and the most economical approach is to house them together. Such a facility is called a data center. Walk into a data center and you will see a few basic elements:

- **Racks and servers**: Row after row of standard racks, each holding dozens of stacked servers.
- **Power systems**: In addition to grid power, there are uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) and backup generators to keep things running during outages.
- **Cooling systems**: Servers generate a lot of heat. A common approach is to arrange racks into "cold aisles" and "hot aisles": cool air is drawn in from the front, hot air is expelled from the back, and the heat is then carried away by air conditioning or liquid cooling systems.
- **Network systems**: Switches at the top of each rack connect the servers, which are then aggregated level by level, and finally connected via multiple high-speed lines to the backbone networks of one or more carriers. Some also connect directly to internet exchange points.

The last point is the one most closely tied to the main thread of this book. A data center is itself an important node of the internet: the more external connections it has and the closer it is to the backbone, the better its access speed can be assured. That is why large data centers often cluster near the submarine cable landing stations discussed in the previous section and around the exchange points of major cities.

What people commonly call "cloud computing" can be roughly understood this way: cloud providers build data centers and rent out computing, storage, and networking capacity on demand to businesses and developers. Many websites don't build their own facilities; instead they "rent" servers in the cloud. The internal network architecture of data centers and resource scheduling in cloud computing are covered in detail in a separate volume of this series; this book focuses only on how they connect to the internet.

## 2.5.3 The Cost of Distance: Why a Central Hub Alone Isn't Enough

Suppose a video site built a data center in just one city, and users across the country and around the world all fetched data from there. Two problems would arise.

The first is **latency**. Light travels through optical fiber at roughly 200,000 kilometers per second, about two-thirds of the speed of light in a vacuum. Spanning several thousand kilometers takes tens of milliseconds one way; a round trip across an ocean can take one to two hundred milliseconds. Loading a web page often involves many round trips, and these milliseconds add up into a wait that users can feel.

The second is **congestion**. When a popular series is released, the same video is requested by tens of millions of people at once. If all of it went out through the same exit, both the data center's outbound bandwidth and the backbone links along the way would be overwhelmed.

A more sensible approach is to put the content close to users. That is the starting point of the content delivery network.

## 2.5.4 Content Delivery Networks: Moving Content "to Your Doorstep in Advance"

A content delivery network (CDN) is a collection of cache servers spread across many cities and many carriers' networks, together forming a widely covering mesh. A website keeps its original content on an origin server, and the CDN copies the frequently accessed parts of it to edge nodes in various locations. When you request an image or a video clip, the system directs you to a node that is close to you and not too heavily loaded, and that node delivers the content locally.

This is much like the warehousing system of a supermarket chain:

| Supermarket System | Content Delivery Network |
|---|---|
| Manufacturer's central warehouse | Origin server |
| Regional distribution centers | Regional cache nodes |
| Neighborhood store | Edge node |
| Customers buy from a nearby store | Users get content from a nearby node |
| Store restocks from the distribution center when out of stock | Node fetches content from the origin server on a cache miss |

How are users "directed" to the nearest node? One common method uses the Domain Name System: for the same web address, users in different places may receive the addresses of different nodes when they look it up. Another method is called anycast, in which many nodes announce the same IP address, and the routing system naturally delivers a user's packets to whichever node is closer along the path. Both mechanisms will make more sense after readers have studied addressing, naming, and routing in Chapter 3, and Chapter 7 will return to the technical details of CDNs.

CDNs are best suited to distributing static content that "many people want the same copy of," such as images, videos, and script files. Content that differs for each user, such as your shopping cart or your private messages, usually still has to be generated by the origin server or application servers. Public information available at the time of writing generally shows that a substantial share of video, software updates, and static resources of large websites is delivered via CDNs. CDNs are offered by specialized providers, built in-house by large internet companies, and also deployed by carriers as cache nodes within their own networks; specific practices vary from company to company.

## 2.5.5 Putting All Three Back into One Picture

Now we can complete the final leg of the journey:

```
Your phone → home network → access network → metro/backbone network
        → (most of the time) a nearby CDN edge node
        → (on a miss) back to the origin server in a data center
```

The server is the individual that answers requests; the data center is the infrastructure where servers run together; and the content delivery network spreads the data center's content out to places closer to users. Together, the three solve the same problem: with billions of users online at the same time, content must be stored reliably and delivered quickly.
