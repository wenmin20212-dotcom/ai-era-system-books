# 2.2 Access Networks: How Fiber, Cellular Networks, and Wi-Fi Get You Connected

On the back of the optical modem at home is a thin yellow or white cable that runs from a wall plate all the way to a small box in the stairwell. Your phone streams video on the subway while its signal bars rise and fall. A card on the wall of a coffee shop lists the Wi-Fi network name and password. These three things correspond to three common paths into the Internet. The previous section looked at the devices in your hands and your home network. This section takes one step outward to examine the stretch between your front door (or your phone's antenna) and the operator's equipment room: the "access network."

## 2.2.1 The "Last Mile": Where the Access Network Sits

The **access network** connects user devices or home networks to the operator's network, and it is often vividly called "the last mile." It does not carry data over long distances; that is the backbone's job. Its task is to gather the lines from countless households and bring them together at the operator's central office equipment.

Urban transportation offers a useful analogy. The backbone is the highway system, and the access network is the side streets and bus stops at the entrance to each neighborhood. Highways are few and carry enormous volumes. Side streets are extremely numerous and spread everywhere. Because the access network must reach every household and every phone, it is often the most expensive and labor-intensive part of an operator's buildout.

The access methods ordinary people encounter most often today can be summarized as follows:

| Access Method | Equipment You Can See | Transmission Medium | Typical Scenario |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiber to the home | Optical modem, fiber wall plate | Optical fiber | Home broadband |
| Cellular mobile network | Phone, SIM card, roadside/rooftop base station | Radio waves | Outdoors, on the move |
| Wi-Fi | Wireless router, wireless access point | Radio waves (short range) | Homes, offices, public venues |
| Copper-based access (e.g., DSL, coaxial cable) | Older "modem," telephone line | Copper wire | Legacy networks in some regions |

One point needs clarifying up front. Strictly speaking, Wi-Fi covers only "the last few meters indoors." It does not connect directly to the operator. Instead, it sits behind a fiber line or another broadband connection. We will return to this later.

## 2.2.2 Fiber to the Home: One Fiber Shared by Dozens of Households

Many urban households use **Fiber to the Home** (FTTH). The mainstream technology is the **Passive Optical Network** (PON). Common standards include the GPON family developed by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU-T) and the EPON family developed by the IEEE.

Its structure can be broken into three parts:

1. **The Optical Line Terminal (OLT) at the central office**: Located in the operator's equipment room, each port sends out one optical signal.
2. **The splitter along the way**: Located in a stairwell or in a fiber cabinet within the neighborhood. It draws no power and performs no computation. It simply divides one beam of light into several parts according to physical laws, which is why the network is called "passive." Common split ratios include 1:32 and 1:64.
3. **The Optical Network Terminal (ONT; also commonly called an Optical Network Unit, ONU) in your home**: This is what people call the "optical modem." It converts optical signals into electrical signals a router can use.

The arrangement resembles a main water pipe that branches into dozens of smaller pipes in the stairwell, one for each household. In the downstream direction, the central office "broadcasts" everyone's data together. Each optical modem takes only the portion that belongs to it, and encryption prevents others from reading the rest. In the upstream direction, everyone shares the same main fiber, so the central office assigns each household time slots for sending. Households take turns transmitting to avoid "collisions."

This structure explains an everyday phenomenon: users under the same splitter share the bandwidth of one central office port. Most of the time, people use the network intermittently, so the sharing is barely noticeable. During the evening peak, however, the connection may slow down if the operator has not reserved enough capacity on this segment. Why light can travel so far through fiber, and how wavelengths are multiplexed, belong to the *Optical Fiber Communications* volume in this series and are not covered here.

## 2.2.3 Cellular Networks: How a Phone "Latches On" to a Base Station

Once you leave home, your phone relies on the **cellular mobile network**. The name "cellular" comes from how operators divide a service area into many adjacent small areas, or cells. Each cell is covered by one base station or a group of them, and on a map the layout resembles a honeycomb.

A phone gets online in roughly the following steps:

- **Air interface connection**: The phone communicates with a nearby base station over radio waves. This segment is called the "air interface." A 4G base station is called an eNodeB, and a 5G base station is called a gNodeB (names that come from the 3GPP standards).
- **Backhaul**: The base station sends data back to the operator's equipment room over fiber (or, in a few cases, microwave). This segment is called the backhaul network. Behind the "wireless network," then, most of the journey still runs through fiber.
- **Core network**: The operator's core network authenticates your SIM card, records which cell you are in, assigns you an address, and ultimately hands your data off to the Internet.

The cellular network's most distinctive capability is **handover**. When you ride in a moving vehicle, your phone is passed from one base station to the next, usually without dropping the connection. Fixed broadband never has to solve this problem, but it is a core challenge for mobile networks. Fewer signal bars and stuttering video in a subway tunnel are often related to base station coverage and the handover process. Details such as the generational evolution of cellular networks and radio resource scheduling are left to the *Mobile Communications* volume in this series.

## 2.2.4 Wi-Fi: The "Last Few Meters" at Home

Back at home, your phone automatically connects to Wi-Fi. **Wi-Fi** is a wireless local area network technology based on the IEEE 802.11 family of standards. "Wi-Fi" itself is a certification trademark of the Wi-Fi Alliance. At the time of writing, common version labels on the market include Wi-Fi 5, Wi-Fi 6, and Wi-Fi 7.

Wi-Fi and cellular networks are both wireless, but they play entirely different roles:

| Comparison | Wi-Fi | Cellular Network |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage range | Typically a few meters to tens of meters | Typically hundreds of meters to several kilometers |
| Spectrum used | Unlicensed bands (e.g., 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz), open to anyone | Dedicated bands licensed to operators |
| Who builds it | Users themselves or venue owners | Operators |
| What it connects to | The home optical modem or another broadband line | The operator's backhaul and core network |

"Unlicensed bands" means that a neighbor's router, Bluetooth devices, and even microwave ovens may operate in the same band. This is why Wi-Fi networks in apartment buildings often interfere with one another. It also leads to a practical rule of thumb. When your home connection is slow, the problem may lie in those few meters of Wi-Fi or in that mile of fiber. Running one speed test with a computer plugged directly into the router by cable can often tell the two apart. Wi-Fi channels, modulation, and multi-antenna techniques are covered in the *Wi-Fi* volume in this series.

## 2.2.5 Different Roads, Same Destination: The Shared Task of Access Networks

The three methods look very different, yet they all do the same thing: **gather dispersed users, verify their identities, and hand them off to the operator's network**. An optical modem registering with the central office, a SIM card authenticating with the core network, and the broadband account you enter into your router all amount to the same principle: "first confirm who you are, then let you in." Once this step is complete, your data packets leave the access network and enter the operator's aggregation layer and backbone.
