# 2.3 Backbone Networks and Internet Exchange Points: How Carriers Connect to One Another

Suppose your home broadband comes from Carrier A, and your friend lives in another city and uses mobile data from Carrier B. You send them a photo, and a few seconds later it arrives. After the photo leaves your home's optical modem, it first enters Carrier A's network, and in the end it has to show up inside Carrier B's network. The two companies are competitors. Each lays its own lines and bills its own customers, yet data passes smoothly between them. This section answers two questions: how are carriers' large networks built, and where and how do different carriers connect to each other?

## 2.3.1 From Metropolitan Networks to the Backbone: The Layers of a Carrier Network

The access network covered in the previous section solves the "last mile" problem by bringing millions of households onto a carrier's network. Above the access network, a carrier's network is roughly divided into several layers. The table below shows a common breakdown. Each carrier's exact names and divisions vary somewhat:

| Layer | Coverage | Main Function | Analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access layer | Neighborhoods, buildings | Gathers user lines together | Neighborhood streets |
| Aggregation layer (metropolitan area network) | A single city | Collects traffic from each district and brings it to the city's core nodes | City arterial roads |
| Core layer (backbone network) | Interprovincial, national, or even international | Moves traffic at high speed between cities | Highways and trunk railway lines |

The backbone network is the part of a carrier's network with the greatest capacity and the highest speed. Its nodes are usually located in core data centers in major cities and are linked to each other by optical fiber. A single fiber uses wavelength division multiplexing to carry many optical signals of different wavelengths at once. Publicly available figures commonly put the total capacity of one fiber at several terabits per second (Tbps) to tens of terabits per second. The principles of optical fiber and wavelength division multiplexing are explained in detail in the *Optical Fiber Communication* volume of this series. For now, just remember this: backbone links are very fat pipes, and the node equipment consists of high-performance core routers that handle enormous volumes of data packets every second.

A backbone network is generally not laid out in a straight line. Its topology is usually a mesh or a set of rings. There is often more than one path from Beijing to Shanghai, so when construction work cuts through one fiber-optic cable, traffic can be rerouted over another path. This redundant design is the foundation of a reliable backbone.

## 2.3.2 Carriers Must "Shake Hands": The Concept of an Autonomous System

Every carrier, every university, and every large internet company can own a network that it manages independently and that follows a single, unified routing policy toward the outside world. Such a network is called an Autonomous System (AS). Each autonomous system has a globally unique number called an AS number (ASN), which is assigned by a Regional Internet Registry. At its core, the internet is tens of thousands of autonomous systems linked together. This is exactly the "network of networks" described in Chapter 1.

Each autonomous system decides for itself how traffic travels inside its own network. For two autonomous systems to communicate, they must first be joined physically by a cable. They must also exchange information about which addresses each one can reach. The protocol responsible for exchanging this information is the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP), and Chapter 6 is devoted to how it works. This section focuses first on where the physical connection is made and how the business arrangement is negotiated.

## 2.3.3 Two Ways to Connect: Private Interconnects and Exchange Points

Carriers connect to each other physically in two main ways.

**Private Network Interconnect (PNI)**: Two networks run a dedicated line directly between them. One end plugs into A's router, and the other end plugs into B's router. This approach works well when the two parties exchange heavy traffic and have a stable relationship, as between a large carrier and a large video platform.

**Internet Exchange Point (IXP)**: High-capacity switching equipment is installed in a neutral data center. Many networks each run a line to this switching equipment, and then they can set up connections with one another on the same platform. To put it another way, a private interconnect is like two households building a private path between their homes. An exchange point is like a public market where everyone sets up a stall. Anyone who wants to trade with someone else can deal directly inside the market, without having to build a road between every pair of households.

If N networks all need to connect with one another, the number of direct links grows with the square of N. With an exchange point, each network needs only one line to the platform. That is how exchange points bring costs down. Public sources put the number of exchange points worldwide in the hundreds to over a thousand. Large exchange points in European cities such as Amsterdam, Frankfurt, and London are often cited as typical examples. The situation in China is different. Interconnection between the basic telecom carriers relies mainly on national internet backbone direct interconnection points set up by the state, and some newer exchange points also operate in various regions. For specific arrangements, refer to information published by the regulatory authorities.

An exchange point itself usually provides only "a place to meet and a platform to connect." It does not decide who exchanges traffic with whom. Whether two networks interconnect is still up to the two networks to negotiate.

## 2.3.4 Peering and Transit: The Business Relationships Behind the Connections

Once the physical link is in place, the parties still have to agree on how the line will be paid for. The two most common relationships between carriers are these:

- **Transit**: A smaller network pays a larger network, and the larger network carries the smaller network's traffic to the rest of the internet. This is like a small courier company handing its out-of-town packages to a national logistics company and paying by volume.
- **Peering**: Two networks of similar size, with roughly balanced traffic between them, agree to exchange traffic between their respective users. Usually neither pays the other, which is called settlement-free peering. However, they exchange only traffic belonging to themselves and their own customers. Neither one forwards the other's traffic to third parties.

Some carriers never need to buy transit from anyone and can reach every network in the world through peering alone. The industry commonly calls them "Tier 1" carriers. They form the top layer of internet connectivity. This label is an industry convention, not a formal certification from any standards body, and lists of which companies belong to this tier differ from source to source.

These business relationships directly affect your online experience. If the interconnection bandwidth between two carriers is insufficient, cross-network access tends to slow down during evening peak hours. This is one of the common causes of the "slow cross-network access" many people have experienced. To deal with this, many websites place servers inside several carriers' networks or use multi-line access, so that users of different carriers can reach a nearby server. Section 4.2 of Chapter 4 will look more closely at the routing policies of peering and transit from the perspective of these relationships.

## 2.3.5 Back to That Photo

Now we can complete the photo's journey from the beginning of this section. The photo leaves your home and enters Carrier A's access network. It is aggregated through the metropolitan area network and reaches a core node on the backbone. At a direct interconnection point, an exchange point, or a private interconnect link in some city, Carrier A hands the data off to Carrier B. The data then travels through Carrier B's backbone, its metropolitan area network, and its mobile access network, and finally reaches your friend's phone. Throughout the trip, each company manages its own network, yet at the agreed handoff points they pass data packets strictly according to shared protocols.

The backbone handles high-speed transport within a country or a continent, and exchange points and interconnection relationships handle the "handshake" between different networks. But when the photo has to cross an ocean, land-based cables are no longer enough. In the next section, we will dive to the seafloor and look at the submarine cables that connect the continents.
