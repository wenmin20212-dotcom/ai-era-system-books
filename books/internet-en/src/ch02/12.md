# 2.4 Submarine Cables and International Links: Tying the Continents Together

Suppose you are in China and open a website whose servers sit on the West Coast of the United States. The page starts to appear within a fraction of a second. In that time, the data has crossed the Pacific Ocean, a one-way trip of more than ten thousand kilometers. Many people assume that transoceanic communication relies on satellites, but in fact the vast majority of international internet traffic travels through optical cables laid on the seabed. A figure commonly cited in public sources is that more than 90 percent of intercontinental data traffic is carried by submarine cables. Satellites are used mainly for remote areas, ships, aviation, and emergency communications; see the volume on satellite communications in this series for details.

This section follows a single submarine cable from the landing station on the shore out into the deep sea, and then back to its place in the internet as a whole.

## 2.4.1 What's Inside a Submarine Cable

Submarine cables vary in thickness. Shallow-water sections need protection from fishing nets and ship anchors, so they are wrapped in steel-wire armor and can reach several centimeters to more than ten centimeters in diameter. Deep-sea sections face little human interference, so their structure is actually simpler, and their diameter is often about that of a garden hose. If you cut open a typical submarine cable, you will find roughly these layers, from the inside out:

| Layer | Function |
|---|---|
| Fiber pairs | The part that actually carries data. Each fiber is thinner than a human hair, and a cable usually contains anywhere from a few to more than a dozen pairs |
| Steel strength member | Withstands tension during laying and on the seabed |
| Copper conductor | Supplies power to the repeaters along the route |
| Insulation and sheath | Waterproofing, electrical insulation, and corrosion protection |
| Armor (shallow-water sections) | Protects against external forces such as ship anchors and trawl nets |

Light gradually weakens as it travels through optical fiber, so transoceanic cables have a **repeater** every few tens of kilometers that amplifies the optical signal before sending it on. Repeaters in modern submarine cables typically use erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFA), which amplify the optical signal directly without first converting it into an electrical signal. Power for the repeaters is delivered from shore-based power feed equipment through the copper conductor, at voltages that can exceed ten thousand volts—a feature unique to submarine cable engineering.

The capacity of a fiber pair depends on how many wavelengths it can carry simultaneously and on the modulation rate of each wavelength. The technique used here is **wavelength division multiplexing** (WDM): treating different colors of light as lanes that don't interfere with one another and packing them side by side into the same fiber. According to public sources, newly built transoceanic cables commonly have total design capacities on the order of tens to hundreds of terabits per second (Tbps). The principles of optical fiber and wavelength division multiplexing are explained in detail in the volume on optical fiber communications in this series.

## 2.4.2 Landing Stations: Where Cables Come Ashore

Near the shore, a submarine cable enters an underground conduit and connects to a **cable landing station** by the sea. The landing station handles power feeding, signal termination, and monitoring. From there, data is carried over terrestrial fiber to data centers and internet exchange points in nearby cities, joining the backbone network discussed in the previous section.

Several considerations commonly guide the siting of landing stations:

- Gentle seabed terrain close to deep water, avoiding earthquake zones and busy shipping lanes as much as possible;
- Proximity to major cities and backbone nodes, making it easy to extend inland;
- A suitable local legal and permitting framework that facilitates construction and maintenance.

For these reasons, landing points around the world are fairly concentrated, often clustering in a handful of coastal cities. This makes connectivity convenient, but it also means that if something goes wrong at one location, the impact can be wide. Readers can open a publicly maintained submarine cable map from organizations such as TeleGeography (for example, the Submarine Cable Map) and see which cities their own country's cables land in.

## 2.4.3 Who Builds Them and Who Uses Them: Consortia and "Private" Cables

A transoceanic cable requires enormous investment. Traditionally, several telecom carriers formed a **consortium** to fund it jointly, with capacity allocated in proportion to each member's contribution. Over the past decade or so, some large content and cloud companies have begun building cables on their own or leading their construction, using them to connect their data centers across continents; Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, and others have publicly reported cable projects. This reflects a shift: a large share of international traffic now flows between the data centers of major platforms, or from those data centers to users.

Once built, a cable's capacity can be used in several ways: the owners use it themselves, lease it to other carriers, or sell it long-term in the form of an Indefeasible Right of Use (IRU). So the cable your data travels over does not necessarily belong to your broadband provider.

## 2.4.4 The Speed of Light, Latency, and Cable Cuts

Light travels through optical fiber at about two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum—roughly 200,000 kilometers per second, or about 5 milliseconds per 1,000 kilometers. From this we can estimate the physical lower bound on latency: if the cable path between China and the United States is a little over ten thousand kilometers, the one-way trip takes at least 50-some milliseconds, and the round trip more than 100 milliseconds. Actual measured round-trip times (RTT) are even higher because of indirect routing, equipment processing, and queuing. This is the fundamental reason why, within the "one second" of Chapter 1, accessing an overseas site is always slower than accessing a local one: latency caused by distance cannot be eliminated by adding bandwidth. It also explains why Section 2.5 covers content delivery networks, whose approach is to move content closer to you.

Submarine cables also fail. According to public statistics, most faults are caused by human activities such as fishing and ship anchors, followed by natural causes such as submarine earthquakes and undersea landslides. Repairs in deep water require specialized cable ships to haul the broken ends to the surface, splice them, and lower the cable back down, which typically takes days to weeks. A cable cut does not necessarily take the network down, for two reasons: first, there are usually multiple cables serving the same direction, and carriers reserve backup routes; second, the routing mechanisms discussed in Chapter 3 redirect traffic onto other paths. What users most commonly notice is that cross-border access becomes slower and latency rises, rather than sites becoming completely unreachable.

## 2.4.5 Seeing the "Network of Networks" Through a Single Cable

Zooming out, several hundred submarine cables in service worldwide, together with terrestrial cables spanning the continents, form the physical skeleton that links the continents. Note, however, that submarine cables only provide the "pipes"; they do not themselves determine how data travels. Which cable your data takes depends on the interconnection agreements between carriers and on the choices made by routing protocols. These belong to the realm of "relationships" and "mechanisms," and are left for Chapter 4 on autonomous system interconnection and Chapter 6 on BGP.
