# 2.6 Chapter Skeleton Summary and Hands-On Exploration Tasks

Let's return to the action from the prologue: you type a web address into your browser's address bar and press Enter. Chapter 1 showed us that a chain of events unfolds within that single second; this chapter zooms in to see the "hardware locations" where those events take place. The request leaves your phone or computer, passes through your home router and optical modem, enters your carrier's network via the access network, travels along the backbone, may switch to another network at an Internet exchange point, crosses submarine cables if necessary, and finally arrives at a server, or, more commonly, at a content delivery node not far from you. This section first organizes that path into a skeleton, then invites you to find its physical counterparts around you.

## 2.6.1 Chapter Skeleton Review: One Path, Five Segments

Compress the content of Chapter 2 into a single table, and you get the five-segment structure between "you" and "the content":

| Segment | Typical Physical Objects | Main Responsibilities | Visible to the Reader? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Devices and home network | Phones, computers, wireless routers, optical modems (optical network terminals, ONTs) | Generating and receiving data; distributing the network within the home; converting between optical and electrical signals | Yes, right next to you |
| Access network | Fiber to the home, optical splitters, cellular base stations, stairwell or neighborhood equipment rooms | Aggregating the lines of countless households into the carrier's network, often called "the last mile" | Partly visible, such as stairwell fiber boxes and roadside base stations |
| Backbone and exchange points | Core routers, long-haul fiber cables, Internet exchange points (IXPs) | High-speed forwarding between cities, provinces, and countries; interconnecting different carriers' networks | Generally not visible; housed in equipment rooms |
| Submarine cables and international links | Submarine cables, landing stations, repeaters | Carrying the vast majority of intercontinental data traffic, commonly cited in public sources as over 90 percent | Not visible, but can be looked up on public submarine cable maps |
| Servers, data centers, and CDNs | Server racks, data centers, content delivery network (CDN) edge nodes | Storing and computing content; caching popular content close to users | Not visible, but can be observed indirectly with network tools |

Behind this table lie three patterns worth remembering:

- **The closer to the middle, the more concentrated.** A home has only one or two devices; the access network bundles an entire neighborhood into a single strand, and the backbone then gathers a whole city into a few major trunk lines. Layer upon layer of aggregation allows a limited number of high-speed lines to serve a great many users.
- **The closer to the middle, the less visible and the more important.** A failure in a backbone cable or an exchange point may affect an entire region; a problem with your home router affects only your household.
- **Content is moving toward you.** With the advent of CDNs and edge caching, many requests no longer need to actually cross an ocean. That "faraway web page" is often stored in an equipment room somewhere in your own city.

## 2.6.2 Two Common Points of Confusion

**An optical modem and a router are not the same thing.** The optical modem handles "translation," converting the optical signal in the incoming fiber into an Ethernet electrical signal; the router handles "distribution," assigning addresses to devices in the home, forwarding data, and providing Wi-Fi. Many carriers supply a combined device, which is why many households have only a single box. At the time of writing, a common practice in China is for optical modems to have routing enabled by default, but the specific configuration varies by carrier and region.

**"Slow internet" is not necessarily a broadband problem.** The bottleneck may lie in the device (an aging phone, background downloads), the home network (Wi-Fi signals weakening through walls), the access network (neighborhood congestion during evening peak hours), or the remote server. The segmented structure of Chapter 2 serves as a map for troubleshooting: first identify which segment is slow, then consider how to fix it. Section 7.5 in Chapter 7 will introduce specific measurement tools.

## 2.6.3 Hands-On Exploration Tasks

The following tasks require only your phone, computer, and the equipment you already have at home. You do not need to change any network settings.

1. **Take "ID photos" of your home network equipment.** Locate your optical modem and router, and photograph the indicator lights on the front and the label on the back. Using the manual or label, figure out what each indicator light means (common ones include power, optical signal, network, and wireless), then check how many ports are on the back and which one connects to the upstream device. Finally, draw a small diagram: where the fiber comes in, which box it connects to, and which devices are connected from there.
2. **Look for the access network in your stairwell and on the street.** Watch for low-voltage cabinets or fiber distribution boxes on stairwell walls (most are locked; observe only from the outside and do not open them), as well as cellular base station antennas by the roadside or on rooftops. Note roughly how far they are from your home, and consider where your data is aggregated from there.
3. **Check your device's address information.** On your phone, tap the current network in the Wi-Fi settings, or on your computer, look at the "IP address," "Router," and "DNS" entries in the network settings. Write down the values; you don't need to understand what they mean yet, as Chapter 3 will explain them one by one. Try a comparison: how do these values differ when your phone is connected to Wi-Fi versus switched to cellular data?
4. **Find submarine cables on a public map.** Search for a public submarine cable map (for example, an online cable map maintained by a telecommunications research firm), find the cable landing city closest to you, and count how many cables depart from there and which countries and regions they lead to.
5. **Time websites near and far.** During the day and again in the evening, open a commonly used domestic website and a website whose server is overseas. Use your phone's stopwatch to roughly time how long it takes for the page to basically finish displaying, testing three times each and taking the middle value. Think about which segment of the path the difference might come from.

## 2.6.4 Questions You Can Ask AI Directly

Copy the following questions to an AI assistant, along with the information you recorded during the tasks, and let it build further on this chapter's skeleton:

- "The indicator lights on my optical modem look like this: [description or photo]. Please tell me what each light means, and which light, when abnormal, indicates that the problem lies in the access network rather than in my home."
- "When I access a website whose server is in the United States from my city, roughly which types of network infrastructure does the data pass through? Please explain in the order of device, access network, backbone, exchange point, submarine cable, and data center, and note which steps a CDN might skip."
- "Why is the same website slower at night than during the day? Please list possible causes at three levels—home network, access network, and server—along with simple troubleshooting methods an ordinary user can try."

When asking AI questions, ask it to indicate which statements are general principles and which are merely the practices of a particular carrier or type of device; when specific figures come up, follow up by asking what kind of source they come from.

## 2.6.5 From "What It Has" to "What It Does"

Chapter 2 answered the question "What is the Internet made of?": by following the path of a web page request, we identified physical entities such as devices, the access network, the backbone, exchange points, submarine cables, servers, and CDNs. But knowing the parts alone does not explain one question: among billions of devices, how does data know where to go, and how is it ensured that nothing gets lost? The next chapter shifts from "parts" to "functions," discussing in turn addressing, naming, routing, reliable transmission, and application services—that is, the responsibilities each part takes on.
