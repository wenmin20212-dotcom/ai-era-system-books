# 5.1 Packet Flows: The Complete Journey of a Web Request

You type a web address into the address bar and press Enter, and in less than a second the page starts appearing piece by piece. Chapter 1 sketched a rough outline of "what happens in one second," and Chapters 3 and 4 covered addressing, routing, layering, and interconnection between carriers. This section takes a different perspective: instead of looking at devices or protocol names, we keep our eyes on the "packet" itself, following it as it leaves your phone, reaches a distant server, and comes back carrying the web page. You will find that "going online" is, at its core, streams of packets flowing through a system.

## 5.1.1 Before Departure: How a Packet Gets "Packed"

Suppose you are at home using your computer to visit a website that uses HTTPS. The first "sentence" the browser sends may be just a one-line request: "Please give me the home page." But this sentence can't simply be tossed onto the wire. It has to go through layer after layer of encapsulation, much like shipping a parcel: you put the item in a box, attach a shipping label, and then load the box into a container:

| Layer | "Packaging" added | Main contents |
|---|---|---|
| Application layer | HTTP request (then encrypted with TLS) | Which page is wanted, browser information, etc. |
| Transport layer | TCP header or UDP header | Source port, destination port, sequence number, etc. |
| Network layer | IP header | Source IP address, destination IP address, time to live |
| Link layer | Ethernet or Wi-Fi frame header | Hardware address of the next-hop device on this link |

One point is worth remembering: **the addresses in the IP header stay essentially the same from start to finish (except for address translation at the home router), while the link layer's "shipping label" is torn off and replaced at every hop.** This is the division of labor between the "end-to-end" network layer and the "segment-by-segment" link layer.

Also, a single web request doesn't produce just one packet. On the Ethernet links common across the Internet, a single packet typically carries data on the order of 1,500 bytes (this is the most common MTU, or Maximum Transmission Unit, meaning the most a single packet can hold). An ordinary web page, along with its images and scripts, is often anywhere from a few hundred KB to several MB, requiring hundreds or thousands of packets to deliver.

## 5.1.2 The Outbound Trip: From Your Door to the Server's Door

Let's trace, in order, the first packet that actually carries the request (assuming domain name resolution has already finished; that part is covered in detail in Section 5.2):

1. **Computer → home router**. The packet first reaches the router via Wi-Fi or an Ethernet cable. The router does something special: it replaces the packet's private source address (such as 192.168.x.x) with the public address the carrier assigned to your home, and records the mapping. This is Network Address Translation (NAT).
2. **Router → optical modem → access network**. The optical modem converts electrical signals into optical signals and sends them into the fiber running into your home, where they are aggregated at the carrier's central-office equipment and then enter the metropolitan area network.
3. **Metro network → backbone network**. The carrier's routers look up their forwarding tables hop by hop to decide the next stop. At each router, the "time to live" (TTL) in the IP header is decremented by one, preventing the packet from circling endlessly in a loop.
4. **Crossing between networks**. If the website isn't on your carrier's network, the packet must be handed off to another network at an Internet exchange point or a private interconnection point. This step is determined jointly by the business relationships between autonomous systems and BGP routing, as discussed in Chapter 4.
5. **Arriving at the server's door**. Today many websites place their content on a content delivery network (CDN), so the packet often actually arrives at a cache server in a city relatively close to you, rather than at the website's headquarters data center.

The whole outbound trip may pass through anywhere from a dozen to more than twenty hops; this is a common range in public measurements, depending on your region and the destination. You can see these hops for yourself with the traceroute tool introduced in Chapter 7.

## 5.1.3 The Return Trip: How the Web Page Is "Unpacked" and Sent Back

After receiving the request, the server cuts the page content into many segments, encapsulates each into its own packet, and sends them back. Three facts here are easy to overlook:

- **The return path isn't necessarily the same as the outbound path.** Each carrier selects routes according to its own policies, so traveling different routes in each direction is normal; this is called path asymmetry.
- **Packets may arrive out of order or be lost.** The Internet uses "best-effort" delivery, and when a router's queue is full, it drops packets. TCP uses sequence numbers and acknowledgments to put out-of-order packets back in order and to request retransmission of lost ones; in HTTP/3, which uses QUIC, this work is done by the QUIC protocol running on top of UDP.
- **Back at your door, there's one more "translation."** The home router looks up the NAT mapping table it recorded earlier, changes the destination address back to your computer's private address, and delivers the packet to your computer. The computer unpacks it layer by layer and finally hands the HTML to the browser.

## 5.1.4 One Page Is Many "Flows" Stacked Together

Once the browser has the home page HTML, it finds references to stylesheets, scripts, images, and fonts, which may come from different domains and different servers. So the browser issues multiple requests at once, creating multiple parallel packet flows. Within the second after you press Enter, your computer may be maintaining connections with several servers simultaneously.

An analogy helps: the home page is like a menu. After reading it, the browser places orders with several kitchens at once, and each kitchen's dishes are delivered by different couriers along different routes, finally coming together on your table as a full banquet. If one kitchen is slow, a piece of the page will be slow to appear—this is a common cause of "the page getting stuck halfway through loading."

| Stage | Packet direction | Typical sources of delay |
|---|---|---|
| Establishing connections | Computer ⇄ server | Round-trip time, number of handshakes |
| Requesting the home page | Computer → server | Uplink, queuing |
| Returning content | Server → computer | Bandwidth, packet loss and retransmission |
| Loading sub-resources | Multiple parallel flows | Number of domains, server distance |

## 5.1.5 Summary: Seeing "Going Online" as Flow

By following a single packet along its way, we have seen the whole picture assembled from the earlier chapters of this book: end devices handle encapsulation and unpacking, the home router handles address translation, the access and backbone networks forward hop by hop, autonomous systems handle handoffs between one another, CDNs supply content from nearby, and TCP or QUIC reassembles scattered packets into complete content. Each part does only its own small segment well, yet together they deliver a web page from thousands of miles away right to your screen.

However, for a packet to take the right path, it must "know" its destination address, and routers must "know" where to forward it. These two kinds of "knowing" are themselves information flowing through the network.
