# 5.2 Address and Name Flows: DHCP, NAT, and DNS Queries

You open your laptop in the morning, it connects to your home Wi-Fi automatically, and a few seconds later the browser can load web pages. At no point did you type in an address. Yet as the previous section explained, every packet must state "where it came from and where it is going." Before the laptop powered on, it had no address, and it had no idea where the web server was. That information was obtained on the fly, before you ever pressed Enter, through a set of invisible "address and name flows." Three jobs do most of the work: DHCP hands out addresses, NAT translates addresses, and DNS looks up addresses.

## 5.2.1 DHCP: Issuing a "Room Number" to Newcomers

The Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP; the current IPv4 version is based on RFC 2131) works much like a hotel front desk assigning rooms to arriving guests. When a device joins a network, it typically exchanges four messages with the front desk, known by their initials as "DORA":

| Step | Message | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Discover | The guest calls out in the lobby: "Is there a front desk?" |
| 2 | Offer | The front desk replies: "You can have room 308." |
| 3 | Request | The guest says: "Great, I'll take 308." |
| 4 | Acknowledge | The front desk records it: "308 is yours for 24 hours." |

The first message is sent as a broadcast. The new device has no address yet and doesn't know where the front desk is, so all it can do is "shout" to the entire local network. On a home network, the front desk that answers is usually the wireless router.

The front desk gives the device more than just an address. A typical DHCP reply includes four items:

- **The device's IP address**, for example 192.168.1.23;
- **The subnet mask**, which determines which addresses count as "on the same local network";
- **The default gateway**, i.e., who receives packets bound for the outside world first—usually the router itself;
- **The DNS server address**, i.e., where to go to look up names later.

Addresses also come with a "lease." As the lease nears expiration, the device renews it on its own. Once a device leaves the network, it stops renewing, and the expired address is reclaimed for someone else. Thanks to leases, even a coffee shop with a constant stream of customers won't run its address pool dry.

In your computer's network settings, or on your phone's Wi-Fi details page, you can usually see these four items. They are the outcome of this "check-in."

## 5.2.2 NAT: Converting Between Private and Public Room Numbers

You may notice that most of the devices in your home have addresses beginning with 192.168 or 10. These are **private addresses** defined by RFC 1918 and are valid only inside a local network. Countless households around the world reuse the same set of private addresses, so these addresses cannot appear directly on the public internet.

The solution is Network Address Translation (NAT), which is also usually handled by your home router. Think of it as the mailroom of an apartment building:

1. Your phone (192.168.1.23) sends a request, with a source port of, say, 50000;
2. The router rewrites the source address to the public address assigned to it by the ISP, and swaps in a port of its own choosing, such as 62001;
3. The router records the mapping in its "translation table": 62001 corresponds to port 50000 on 192.168.1.23;
4. When the server's reply arrives at port 62001, the router looks up the table, rewrites the packet back, and delivers it to the phone.

By distinguishing connections by port, a single public address can serve dozens of connections for the whole household at once. This approach is often called NAPT or PAT. During the era of IPv4 address scarcity, it eased the problem of "not enough room numbers," but at a cost: outside hosts find it hard to initiate connections into the internal network, which complicates peer-to-peer communication, as noted in Section 4.3. Some ISPs add yet another layer of NAT inside their own networks, known in the industry as carrier-grade NAT (CGN). In that case, the "public address" your router receives is actually an internal address within the ISP's network. Whether CGN is used varies by ISP and region. As Section 7.1 will explain, IPv6 has an address space large enough that, by design, it no longer depends on NAT.

## 5.2.3 DNS Queries: Turning Names into Addresses

Now the device has an address and knows where its exit is. But what the browser has is only a name, such as www.example.com. So it must use the Domain Name System (DNS) to turn that name into an IP address. The query itself is a small data flow:

- The browser and operating system first check their **local cache**; if the name was looked up recently, the cached answer is used directly;
- If it isn't in the cache, the query goes to the DNS server provided by DHCP. On a home network, this is often the router forwarding to the ISP's **recursive resolver**. Users can also manually switch to a public DNS service;
- If the recursive resolver has no cached answer either, it queries the root, top-level domain, and authoritative servers in turn. This hierarchical process is covered in detail in Section 6.4;
- Each answer carries a "time to live" (TTL), during which caches at every level may reuse it directly.

Traditional DNS queries mostly use UDP port 53 and travel in plaintext. In recent years, encrypted methods such as DNS over HTTPS (DoH, RFC 8484) and DNS over TLS (DoT, RFC 7858) have emerged, and at the time of writing, major browsers and operating systems all offer related options. The purpose of encryption is to prevent queries from being snooped on or tampered with in transit.

## 5.2.4 Tying the Three Flows Together

Arranged in chronological order, the "preparation flow" before your first web page loads looks roughly like this:

| Stage | Direction of Flow | What You Get |
|---|---|---|
| DHCP | Device ↔ router (within the LAN) | Device address, gateway, DNS server |
| DNS | Device → router → resolver → servers at each level | IP address of the target server |
| NAT | Every packet entering or leaving the home passes through the router | Translation between private and public addresses |

Note that each operates over a different scope. DHCP flows only within "one building." NAT stands guard at the "building entrance," and every packet must pass through it. DNS, meanwhile, may reach all the way to the other side of the globe.

When troubleshooting, these three steps are also the most common points of failure:

- If a device shows "Connected, no internet" and its address begins with 169.254, DHCP most likely failed to provide an address;
- If you can reach a web page by IP address but not by domain name, the problem is often DNS;
- If certain online games or video calls warn of a "strict NAT type," the issue relates to address translation.

Only once addresses and names are in place do packets know which direction to head. How routers exchange "maps" with one another and decide on a specific path is the subject of the next section, on routing information flows.
