# 5.4 Content Flows: How Video and Web Pages Reach You from Cache Nodes

At nine in the evening, you tap on an episode of a popular show on your phone, and within a few seconds it starts playing. You drag the progress bar to jump to the second half, and the picture picks up again almost immediately. At that very moment, a great many people across the city may be watching the same episode. If every one of them had to fetch a complete copy of the video from an origin server a thousand miles away, the intercity and international links would quickly be saturated. The previous sections covered how packets, addresses, and routing information flow. This section looks at a different kind of flow: how **the content itself** is copied, stored, and divided up, and then delivered to your screen from the closest possible place.

## 5.4.1 One Piece of Content, Many Copies: From Origin to Edge Node

The server where a website or video platform stores its original content is called the **origin server**. If content lived only on the origin server, every request would have to travel a long network path. A **Content Delivery Network** (CDN) instead caches copies of the content on servers distributed across many locations close to users. These servers are usually called **edge nodes**.

Content flows through roughly three tiers:

| Tier | Location | What It Stores |
|---|---|---|
| Origin server | The platform's own data center or the cloud | All original content |
| Mid-tier cache | Large regional nodes | Content that is requested fairly often |
| Edge node | City data centers, inside ISP networks, near exchange points | Whatever is most popular locally right now |

Think of it as a logistics system: the origin server is the central warehouse, and edge nodes are the forward warehouses at the entrance to your neighborhood. Popular items are stocked in the forward warehouse ahead of time, so when you place an order, it comes from just downstairs; only unpopular items have to be shipped from the central warehouse. Some large content platforms go further and place cache servers directly inside the facilities of access ISPs, so that traffic stays within the ISP's network as much as possible. This is a specific arrangement between particular platforms and ISPs, and not every network works this way.

## 5.4.2 Hits and Misses: How a Cache Decides Whether to Serve You

Once your request reaches an edge node, one of two things happens:

- **Cache hit**: The node already has the content and returns it directly. The path is short and the response is fast.
- **Cache miss**: The node doesn't have the content, or its copy has expired. The node fetches it from the tier above or from the origin, passing it to you while keeping a copy for whoever comes next.

So for the same episode, the first person in the area to open it may wait slightly longer, while those who follow get it faster. Which content is worth keeping, and for how long, depends mainly on two things. The first is the caching directives the origin server writes into its HTTP response. For example, `Cache-Control: max-age=3600` means the content may be cached for one hour; HTTP caching rules are defined in RFC 9111. The second is that each node has limited capacity, so when space runs out, it must evict content according to some policy. A common approach is to evict first whatever has gone unaccessed the longest.

Caching isn't limited to CDNs, either. Your browser has its own cache: the second time you open the same website, the site logo, stylesheets, and scripts are often read straight from your device without going online at all. The first stop in the content flow is often right inside your own device.

## 5.4.3 Why Videos Are Cut into Small Pieces

A web page is made up of many small files, whereas a video is one large file. Mainstream online video is not downloaded as a whole. Instead, it is cut into **segments** a few seconds long, and the same content is pre-encoded into multiple quality versions. The player first downloads a **manifest** file that lists the address of each segment and the available bitrates, then requests the segments one by one.

The two common standard approaches today are **HLS** (HTTP Live Streaming, proposed by Apple and specified in RFC 8216) and the international standard **MPEG-DASH** (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP). Both deliver segments over ordinary HTTP, so they can take direct advantage of the web caches and CDNs already spread everywhere, with no need to build a dedicated video network.

Segmentation brings three benefits:

1. **Easy to cache**: Small segments are ordinary files, which edge nodes can readily store and serve as hits.
2. **Easy to seek**: To jump to minute 40, the player only needs to request the segment for that portion.
3. **Easy to adjust**: The quality level can be chosen afresh for every segment.

## 5.4.4 Adaptive Bitrate: The Player Is Watching the Road

The third point is what's known as **Adaptive Bitrate** (ABR). The player continuously estimates how fast recent segments have downloaded, and keeps an eye on how many seconds of playable content remain in its local buffer. When the network is smooth and the buffer is ample, it switches the next segment to a higher-definition version. When the signal drops in an elevator and the buffer is about to run dry, it falls back to a lower quality, accepting a blurrier picture in order to avoid stalling.

When you notice a video starting out blurry and then sharpening, that usually isn't a malfunction. The player is conservatively choosing a low bitrate at the start, then stepping it up once it has gauged network conditions. This reveals a division of labor: the network simply does its best to deliver segments, while the job of deciding how large a segment to send is handed to the player on the end device. This follows directly from the end-to-end principle discussed in Chapter 8.

## 5.4.5 Summary: Content Moves Closer to Users

To tie this section together, the path of content flow is as follows. The origin server produces content, which is replicated to mid-tier and edge nodes according to rules. Your request is directed to a nearby node; on a hit, the content is returned directly, and on a miss, the node fetches it from upstream. Video flows in the form of segments, with the player choosing the quality level based on real-time network conditions. Meanwhile, the browser cache means that some content never has to leave your device at all.

Packet flow is concerned with "which way to go"; content flow is concerned with "where to fetch from." The internet can withstand the enormous volume of viewing at evening peak hours largely not by widening every link indefinitely, but by moving content closer to people in advance.
