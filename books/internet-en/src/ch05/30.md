# 5.5 The Scale and Tides of Traffic: Peaks, Troughs, and Bursts

At nine in the evening, you open a video site to watch an episode of a show, and the picture stays blurry for a few seconds before it sharpens. At seven the next morning, you watch the same episode and it is crisp almost the moment you click. Your home broadband hasn't changed, and neither has your phone. What has changed is "everyone else." Around nine at night, people in your neighborhood, your city, and the whole country are streaming videos, playing games, and broadcasting live. Traffic on the network rises and falls like the sea. This section takes a clear look at these tides: how large traffic is, when it rises, when it ebbs, where sudden big waves come from, and how the network copes with them.

## 5.5.1 How Traffic Is Measured: Rate and Volume

When we talk about traffic, we first need to distinguish between two quantities:

| Concept | Meaning | Common Units | Everyday Analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate (bandwidth usage) | Amount of data passing through per second | bit/s, often written as Mbps, Gbps, Tbps | How much water flows through a pipe each second |
| Volume | Total data transferred over a period of time | Bytes, often written as GB, TB, PB, EB | How many tons of water you used in a month |

Note that rates are usually expressed in "bits" (bit, b), while file sizes are usually expressed in "bytes" (Byte, B); 1 byte equals 8 bits. A broadband plan labeled "1000M" means roughly 1000 Mbps, which works out to a maximum download speed of a little over a hundred megabytes per second.

Now scale up. A household watching HD video typically uses anywhere from a few Mbps to twenty or thirty Mbps. A city's broadband egress can reach the Tbps range (trillions of bits per second) at peak times. According to figures commonly cited in statistical bulletins from national telecom regulators and in public materials from organizations such as the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), global internet traffic is now measured in EB (exabytes, roughly one billion GB) per month, on the order of several hundred EB. The exact numbers change every year; consult these organizations' latest annual reports for current figures. What is truly worth remembering is the structure: the vast majority of traffic is video, and much of that video is delivered from the nearby cache nodes discussed in the previous section, so it doesn't all have to cross the backbone.

## 5.5.2 The Daily Tide: Why Evenings Are the Most Congested

If you plot a region's broadband egress traffic over a full day, you will usually see a very regular wave:

- **Early-morning trough**: From 2 a.m. to 6 a.m., most people are asleep, and traffic drops to its lowest point of the day, perhaps only a fraction of the peak.
- **Daytime climb**: Once people start work and school, office and mobile network traffic rises, while home broadband stays relatively flat.
- **Evening peak**: From around 8 p.m. to 11 p.m., people back home are all watching videos, gaming, and making video calls at once, pushing home broadband traffic to its daily high. Carriers often call this period the "busy hour."

Mobile network curves look a bit different, with smaller peaks during commuting hours and lunch breaks as well. There are also weekly rhythms (higher during weekend daytime), yearly rhythms (holidays, winter and summer school breaks), and "festival surges" from major sporting events, televised galas, and big updates to popular games.

This curve dictates a basic principle of network construction: **networks must be built for the peak, not the average.** Just as city roads must be designed with enough lanes for rush hour, a link that is packed at night will sit largely idle in the early morning. When planning capacity expansion, carriers generally look at busy-hour utilization; if it stays close to a certain threshold (standards vary by carrier, and figures commonly cited in public sources range from 50–60% to 70–80%), they consider adding bandwidth. Once traffic approaches full load at peak, the queuing and packet loss analyzed in Section 8.2 begin to appear—and this is one source of those few seconds of blurry video at the start: the player detects a drop in speed and automatically switches to a lower resolution.

## 5.5.3 Bursts: Where the Big Waves Come From

Tides are predictable; bursts arrive suddenly. Common sources of bursts include:

1. **Breaking events**: Major news, celebrity announcements, ticket sales opening—within minutes, huge numbers of users visit the same website at once, putting instant strain on its servers and the links they sit on.
2. **Mass downloads**: When a phone operating system update or a large patch for a popular game is released, thousands upon thousands of devices start downloading almost simultaneously.
3. **Live-streaming peaks**: Live broadcasts of major sporting events and galas, where viewers pour in at the same moment and are sensitive to delay.
4. **Abnormal traffic**: Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks launched from devices controlled by malicious software, which flood a target with massive volumes of useless requests. This book covers only the principles and defenses: protection usually relies on upstream carriers and specialized scrubbing services to identify and filter abnormal traffic, while spreading a service across multiple nodes so that no single point is easily overwhelmed.

Even on very short timescales, traffic comes "in spurts." A web page consists of dozens of files; the browser requests them all concurrently in an instant, then goes quiet while you read. Network researchers discovered long ago that internet traffic is bursty across timescales of milliseconds, seconds, and minutes alike, and cannot simply be treated as a steady flow of water. This is also why routers need buffers—to queue and temporarily hold packets that arrive in sudden surges.

## 5.5.4 How the Network Copes with Ebb and Flow

In the face of tides and bursts, the internet has no central commander; instead, multiple layers of mechanisms each do their part:

| Layer | Methods | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Endpoints and applications | Adaptive bitrate, off-peak downloading (e.g., automatic updates at night) | When speed drops, proactively "ask for a little less," and shift big tasks to the trough |
| Transport layer | Congestion control in protocols such as TCP (see Section 6.2) | Slow down sending upon sensing congestion, so everyone shares the bandwidth |
| Content delivery | Pushing popular content to edge caches in advance | Intercept traffic close to users, easing pressure on the backbone |
| Carriers | Expanding capacity for the busy hour, load sharing across multiple paths, traffic engineering | Keep headroom, and steer traffic toward less congested lines when needed |
| Service providers | Elastic scaling, rate limiting and queuing, degrading to static pages | The "You're in line" page during ticket sales is one such protective measure |

As you can see, each layer here reflects content from earlier chapters: cache nodes handle the flow of content, routing protocols decide which path traffic takes, and the restraint of endpoints themselves is part of overall stability.

## 5.5.5 Summary: Reading Your Home Network's "Heartbeat Chart"

Traffic has both regular tides and unpredictable waves. Networks are built for the peak and rely on multiple layers of mechanisms to stay usable through the rise and fall. The next time a video turns blurry in the evening, try looking at it from another angle: it may not be that your home network is broken, but that the entire network is at high tide for the day. You can also log in to your home router's admin page and look at the traffic curve it records, or run a speed test at different times of day and sketch your household's own "tide line" by hand.
