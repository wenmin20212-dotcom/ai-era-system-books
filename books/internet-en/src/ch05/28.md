# 5.3 Routing Information Flows: How Routing Protocols Exchange "Maps"

When you open a map app on your phone and start navigation, the blue route on the screen doesn't come out of nowhere. Behind it is a constantly updated road network: which roads are open, which are closed, where traffic is jammed. All of that has to be gathered first before the app can work out a route. The internet works the same way. The data packets covered in the previous section, along with DHCP, NAT, and DNS, deal with "who am I" and "where are you." But a packet leaving your home has to pass through a dozen or even several dozen routers. How does each router know which exit to send it through next? The answer is that before packets start moving, and all the while they move, another invisible flow is running through the network. It is called the routing information flow, and routers use it to exchange "maps" with one another.

## 5.3.1 Two Flows: The Delivery Trucks and the Messengers

Let's start by separating two things that are often lumped together.

| Aspect | Data flow (forwarding) | Routing information flow (route selection) |
|---|---|---|
| What flows | User data packets such as web pages and video | Messages between routers about "what is reachable and how to get there" |
| Who handles it | The router's forwarding part, which must be extremely fast | The router's control part, computed in software |
| Output | Each packet sent out of a particular interface | A routing table, and the forwarding table generated from it |
| Analogy | Trucks driving on the road | A traffic radio station constantly reporting road conditions |

In engineering terms, these two parts are called the Data Plane and the Control Plane. A routing protocol is the set of "messaging rules" on the control plane. Through it, a router tells its neighbors which networks it can reach. The neighbors then organize what they hear into their own routing table. Each row of that table says roughly this: to reach a given address prefix, go out through this interface and hand the packet to this next hop. When a packet arrives, the router only has to look up the table. It doesn't have to think the route through all over again each time.

Your home router actually has a routing table too, though its contents are very simple: the local subnet stays inside your home, and every other address goes to your ISP. This "send everything else outside" rule is called the default route. At home, a single default route is enough. In carrier networks and at the internet's core, however, routers must keep exchanging information through routing protocols.

## 5.3.2 Inside a Network: The Detailed Map an Operator Draws for Itself

As Chapter 4 explained, the internet is pieced together from many Autonomous Systems (AS). Each autonomous system is usually the network of one carrier, one large company, or one university. Inside an autonomous system, the routing information flow uses an Interior Gateway Protocol (IGP). There are two common approaches.

**Distance Vector**: Each router only tells its neighbors "how far I am from a given network." A neighbor adds its own distance to that router onto the number and passes the result further along. It's like asking for directions and only being told "head east, about three stops." You never see the whole picture yourself. The early RIP (Routing Information Protocol, first specified in RFC 1058, with version 2 in RFC 2453) belongs to this category. It measures distance in "hops" and recognizes at most 15 hops, so it suits only small networks.

**Link State**: Each router broadcasts "who I'm connected to and what each link costs" to every router in its area. As a result, everyone holds a complete topology map, and each router computes the shortest paths on its own. OSPF (Open Shortest Path First, RFC 2328) and IS-IS (Intermediate System to Intermediate System) belong to this category and are the mainstream choices inside large carrier and enterprise networks.

One intuition is all you need here: distance vector is "hearing it secondhand from your neighbors," while link state is "everyone holds the full map." The shortest-path algorithms behind them will be covered in detail from a graph-theory perspective in Section 8.3 of Chapter 8.

## 5.3.3 Between Networks: Exchanging Only "Reachability," Not Details

Between autonomous systems, things are different. Carriers won't reveal the lines, equipment, and costs inside their networks to competitors. Besides, the details of every network in the world add up to far more than any router could hold. So autonomous systems talk to each other using the Border Gateway Protocol, BGP (the current version is BGP-4, RFC 4271).

The information BGP carries is more like a series of "announcements": "Address prefix X can be reached through me, passing through autonomous systems A, B, and C in that order." This sequence of autonomous system numbers is called the AS path (AS_PATH). The side receiving an announcement puts it to three uses:

- It looks at the path length as one reference when choosing a route;
- If it finds its own number in the path, a loop has formed, so it discards the announcement;
- It weighs options in light of business relationships. In common carrier practice, for example, when several routes reach the same destination, a carrier usually prefers a customer's link first, then a peering partner's, and only last a paid upstream transit provider.

So BGP chooses routes based first on policy, and only then on "closeness." This corresponds to the peering and transit relationships discussed in Section 4.2. At this layer, the routing information flow is really carrying business contracts.

The announcements from all the world's autonomous systems add up to a list often called the "global routing table." According to commonly cited public measurements, at the time of writing it holds IPv4 prefixes on the order of a million, plus several hundred thousand IPv6 prefixes, and both numbers keep growing year by year. Much of the memory and lookup hardware in internet core routers exists to hold this table.

## 5.3.4 The Map Changes: Convergence and "Passing Along the Wrong Message"

Road conditions change, and so do networks: fiber cables get dug up, equipment restarts, new websites go online. That is why the routing information flow never stops. When a link goes down, the affected routers send out update or withdrawal messages. The messages spread hop by hop until every router has once again reached a consistent conclusion. This process is called convergence. Until convergence completes, some packets may take detours, bounce back and forth between two routers, or simply be lost. When a web page occasionally "freezes for a few seconds and then comes back," the cause is sometimes a route converging somewhere.

A bigger concern is "passing along the wrong message." BGP was designed on the assumption that every party tells the truth. If some network mistakenly announces someone else's address prefix as its own, its neighbors may believe it and keep passing it on, and a large swath of traffic ends up pulled to the wrong place. Such incidents are called route leaks or route hijacks, and they have repeatedly made well-known websites unreachable in parts of the world. One of the industry's defenses is RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure). It uses digital certificates to prove "who is authorized to announce which prefix," so a router receiving an announcement can validate its origin. The detailed mechanism is left for Section 6.3.

## 5.3.5 Summary: First the Map, Then the Road

Placing this section back into the book's overall framework: for a packet to find its way, routers must already have a map in hand. That map is assembled bit by bit, and constantly repaired, by the routing information flow. Inside an autonomous system, IGPs aim to find the shortest path quickly and accurately. Between autonomous systems, BGP conveys reachability and business policy. Only with the two working together can your request cross unfamiliar networks and reach its destination when you press Enter.
