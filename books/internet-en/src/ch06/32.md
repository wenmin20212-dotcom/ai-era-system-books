# 6.1 Packet Switching and Best Effort: The Internet's Fundamental Design

In the evening you're at home watching a video while someone else in the family browses the web, and the router's lights keep blinking. The two of you share the same fiber line and the same router, yet neither has to wait for the line to free up. Go back a few decades to the landline telephone and things were different: if someone at home was on a call, anyone who picked up an extension heard only a busy signal. The difference between these two experiences comes from two different approaches to network design: circuit switching and packet switching. The Internet chose the latter and added a modest promise on top of it: "best effort." This section starts there and looks at the foundation that every mechanism in Chapter 6 relies on.

## 6.1.1 Two Kinds of Switching: Reserve the Road First, or Split Up and Go

**Circuit switching** is how the traditional telephone network works. Before a call begins, the network reserves an end-to-end path between the caller and the person being called. For the whole call, that path belongs to you alone, and the resources stay tied up whether you are talking or silent. The advantage is stable delay and predictable sound quality. The disadvantage is waste: much of a call is pauses, and the reserved capacity simply sits idle.

**Packet switching** instead cuts the data to be sent into small pieces and puts an "envelope" on each one (a header stating the source and destination addresses). Each piece is called a **packet**. Packets travel independently. When a router receives one, it glances at the address and forwards it, then moves to the next. Links are not reserved for anyone. Whoever has data uses them.

A traffic analogy helps. Circuit switching is like closing a road in advance for a convoy, so that no one else may use it even when it is empty. Packet switching is like an open highway, where all vehicles share the lanes and each follows the road signs.

| Comparison | Circuit Switching | Packet Switching |
|---|---|---|
| Path setup | Established and reserved before communication | No reservation; packets forwarded as they arrive |
| Resource use | Exclusive, held even when idle | Shared, used on demand |
| Delay | Stable, predictable | Varies with congestion |
| Behavior under congestion | New calls refused (busy signal) | Packets queue and slow down, or are dropped |
| Typical example | Traditional landline telephone network | The Internet |

## 6.1.2 Why the Internet Chose Packet Switching

Computer communication has a feature that sets it apart from phone calls: it is **bursty**. You open a web page, a large amount of data must download within a few hundred milliseconds, and then you read for a minute while almost nothing is transmitted. Reserving a fixed path for traffic like this would leave it idle most of the time. Packet switching lets the bursts of many users interleave on the same link, filling each other's gaps. In networking this is called **statistical multiplexing**. Because users rarely all send at full speed at the same moment, a link can serve far more users than an even split of its capacity would suggest. That is why you can watch a video while your family member browses the web.

The second reason is **resilience**. The idea of packet switching was proposed independently in the 1960s by Paul Baran, Donald Davies, and others, and the ARPANET adopted it soon after. The network has no fixed line that must stay open. If a link or router fails, later packets can take a different route. Chapter 1 described the Internet as a "network of networks," and packet switching is what makes that possible. Different networks can pass traffic to one another as long as they all handle packets in the same format. Nobody has to arrange, in advance, a dedicated line running through several carriers for every pair of communicating parties.

## 6.1.3 What "Best Effort" Actually Promises

The **Internet Protocol (IP)** at the core of the Internet provides **best-effort** service. RFC 791 describes IP plainly: it does not guarantee reliable delivery, and it provides no end-to-end acknowledgments, flow control, or sequencing. In everyday terms, the network promises each packet only "I'll try to get you there." It makes none of the following promises:

- **No guaranteed delivery**: if a router's buffer is full, a newly arriving packet may simply be dropped;
- **No guaranteed order**: a packet sent first may take a slower route and arrive after one sent later;
- **No guarantee against duplicates**: under certain abnormal conditions, two copies of the same packet may appear;
- **No guaranteed delay**: during congestion, queuing time can be short or long.

This sounds like a rather irresponsible service contract, but it is a deliberate design. The routers in the middle need to do only one simple job: look up a table and forward, and drop packets when they can't keep up. Routers do not track the state of each connection and are not responsible for recovering losses. That makes them fast and cheap to build, and it lets the network scale to a global size.

## 6.1.4 Where Did Reliability Go? To the Two Ends

If the network doesn't guarantee reliability, why aren't characters missing from the files you download, and why don't web pages arrive out of order? The answer is that reliability has been pushed out to the two **ends** of the communication: your phone or computer, and the server far away.

| Responsibility | Handled By |
|---|---|
| Forwarding packets hop by hop based on address | Routers in the middle of the network (IP layer) |
| Detecting lost packets and retransmitting | The two end hosts (e.g., TCP) |
| Putting out-of-order packets back in order | The two end hosts (e.g., TCP) |
| Slowing down or speeding up sending based on congestion | The two end hosts (congestion control) |
| Encryption and identity verification | The two end hosts (e.g., TLS) |

This division of labor lets each application make its own trade-offs. A file download must arrive exactly, character for character, so it uses the **Transmission Control Protocol (TCP)** to guarantee reliable, in-order delivery. A video call cares more about timeliness. It would rather skip a lost packet or two than wait for retransmission, so it can build on the **User Datagram Protocol (UDP)** and handle things itself. The network stays simple, and the edges add complex functions as needed. Chapter 8 returns to this idea under the name "end-to-end principle" and explains its theoretical basis.

"Best effort" does have a cost. The network treats everyone the same, so delay and packet loss at peak times are hard to guarantee in advance. Engineers have added various remedies, such as quality-of-service (QoS) marking and priority scheduling within carrier or enterprise networks, or content delivery networks that place content closer to users. All of these are local enhancements built on top of best effort, and none of them changes the basic model of the public Internet.

## 6.1.5 Summary: A Simple Foundation

Packet switching answers the question of how to share lines efficiently, and best effort sets the limit on what the network is responsible for. Together they produce a system that is simple in the middle and smart at the edges: routers forward quickly and are allowed to drop packets, while end hosts recover losses and adjust their sending. With this foundation in place, the mechanisms in the rest of this chapter share a common starting point. TCP has to build reliable connections on top of a network that loses and reorders packets. BGP has to decide where these packets go between autonomous systems. DNS and TLS, working over the same best-effort network, solve the problems of finding the other party and talking to it securely.
