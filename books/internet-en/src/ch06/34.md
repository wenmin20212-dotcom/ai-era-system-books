# 6.3 BGP: The Internet's Routing Protocol and Its Weak Points

When you open an overseas video site at home, your data packets leave your home optical modem and must pass through your ISP, and perhaps one or two international carriers, before reaching the video site's data center. Each of these carriers manages its own network, and there is no central dispatcher coordinating them. So how does your ISP's router know "which direction" the video site's address lies in? The answer is a protocol through which carriers constantly exchange messages with one another: the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP). Ordinary users rarely notice it, but when it goes wrong, a major website can "vanish" from the internet entirely.

## 6.3.1 What BGP Does: Exchanging "Where I Can Reach" Between Autonomous Systems

As Chapter 4 explained, the internet is made up of tens of thousands of Autonomous Systems (AS), each with a number (ASN). An AS might be a carrier, a university, a cloud provider, or a large website. How traffic moves inside an AS is decided by the AS itself, using interior routing protocols such as OSPF; how traffic moves between ASes is BGP's job. The version in use today is BGP-4, specified in RFC 4271.

The core information BGP exchanges can be boiled down to one sentence: "A certain address range is reachable through me, and here is the path." For example, the AS hosting the video site announces to its neighbors, "Address range X is here with me." A neighbor that receives this prepends its own number to the path and passes it on to its own neighbors. Relayed hop by hop, the announcement eventually lets carrier routers around the world know where address range X is.

A few key concepts:

| Concept | Meaning | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Prefix | A block of IP addresses, such as a /24 address block | The range of house numbers in a neighborhood |
| Announcement | Telling neighbors "this prefix is reachable through me" | Putting up a signpost at an intersection |
| Withdrawal | Telling neighbors "this prefix is no longer reachable through me" | Taking down the signpost |
| AS_PATH | The sequence of AS numbers traversed to reach the prefix | The list of cities along the route |
| Session | A long-lived connection between two BGP routers, running over TCP port 179 | A dedicated phone line between two neighbors |

BGP running between ASes is called external BGP (eBGP); BGP used to synchronize this information among border routers within the same AS is called internal BGP (iBGP).

## 6.3.2 Path Vectors and Policy: BGP Doesn't Just Look for the "Shortest"

Chapter 8 will cover distance-vector algorithms. BGP is a variant of them, known as a "path-vector" protocol: instead of passing along a single distance number, it passes along the complete AS path. This has two advantages. First, it prevents loops: if a router sees its own number already in the path, it discards the route. Second, it enables policy: a carrier can decide whether to use a route based on who appears in its path.

This is the key to understanding BGP: **BGP route selection looks first at business relationships and policy, and only then at path length.** The "peering" and "transit" relationships introduced in Chapter 4 come into play here—a carrier typically prefers routes learned from customers (who pay it), then routes from peers, and only last routes from upstream transit providers it has to pay. Routers compare a series of attributes in order, such as local preference and AS_PATH length, to choose the best path. As a result, your packets often travel not the geographically shortest route, but the "commercially appropriate" one.

Another characteristic of BGP is incremental updates: once a session is established, the two sides first exchange their full routing tables, and afterward send updates only when something changes. Data from public route-monitoring projects (such as RouteViews and RIPE RIS) show that, at the time of writing, the global IPv4 routing table is on the order of a million prefixes.

## 6.3.3 Weak Points: A System Built on "Trusting Your Neighbors"

BGP was born in the late 1980s (its first specification, RFC 1105, was published in 1989), when the internet had few participants who all knew one another. The protocol itself trusts neighbors' announcements by default—it does not verify "you say this prefix is with you, but is that true?" This leads to several typical kinds of problems:

- **Route hijacking**: An AS announces a prefix that doesn't belong to it. Because BGP prefers more specific (longer) prefixes, such bogus announcements can draw away large amounts of traffic. One of the most famous cases occurred in 2008, when Pakistan Telecom, aiming to block YouTube domestically, announced a more specific /24 block of YouTube's addresses. The announcement accidentally propagated worldwide, knocking YouTube offline globally for about two hours.
- **Route leaks**: An AS passes routes that were meant for limited use to neighbors that shouldn't receive them—for example, announcing routes learned from one upstream provider to another upstream provider—causing large volumes of traffic to detour through a small network that lacks the capacity to carry it. RFC 7908 classifies these problems.
- **Withdrawals caused by misconfiguration**: In October 2021, Facebook (now Meta) suffered an outage lasting several hours. According to the company's public post-incident explanation, an error during a backbone maintenance operation led to the withdrawal of the BGP announcements for the prefixes hosting its DNS servers, and the outside world could no longer find those servers.

Most of these incidents were not malicious; rather, human errors were rapidly amplified in a system built on "default mutual trust." BGP's fragility is fundamentally a trade-off between open interconnection and security verification, a topic Section 8.5 will explore further.

## 6.3.4 Reinforcements: Adding "Proof of Ownership" to Route Announcements

Over the past decade or so, the industry has gradually hardened BGP without overturning it:

1. **RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure)**: Address holders obtain Route Origin Authorizations (ROAs) through Regional Internet Registries, declaring "this prefix may only be announced by a certain AS." Carriers' routers use these to perform Route Origin Validation (ROV), discarding announcements whose origin doesn't match. The relevant specifications are RFC 6480 and RFC 6811. RPKI effectively blocks many erroneous announcements, but it validates only the "origin," not the entire path.
2. **Filtering and best practices**: Carriers set filtering rules based on the addresses their customers actually hold, accepting only reasonable announcements. The industry initiative MANRS (Mutually Agreed Norms for Routing Security) encourages its members to adopt such practices.
3. **BGPsec (RFC 8205)**: Signs the entire path hop by hop. It is more powerful, but costly to compute and deploy, and at the time of writing its deployment remains very limited.

As an ordinary user, you can't directly influence BGP; but readers who run websites or operations can check whether their address ranges have registered ROAs, and choose upstream carriers that support Route Origin Validation.

## 6.3.5 Summary

BGP is the "diplomatic protocol" that glues tens of thousands of autonomous systems into a single internet: it propagates paths, enforces business policy, and trusts neighbors by default. That trust allowed the internet to expand rapidly—and it also allows a single bad announcement to ripple across the globe within minutes. Routing tells packets which direction to go, but before they even set out, the browser must first turn a domain name into an IP address.
