# 6.4 DNS Hierarchy, Caching, and the Resolution Process

You type a web address into the browser's address bar and press Enter. The first time you open a particular site, there is sometimes a brief "hitch" before it starts loading; open the same site again, and the response is almost instant. During that short pause, one thing is often happening: your computer is asking the network which IP address corresponds to that name. Chapter 3 explained that the Domain Name System (DNS) is responsible for turning names into addresses. This section takes its inner workings apart further: why names are managed in layers, how a single query works its way down level by level, how caching makes the second lookup faster, and what this system relies on to guard against impersonation.

## 6.4.1 An Upside-Down Tree: The Hierarchical Structure of DNS

The DNS namespace is an inverted tree. Take `www.example.com.` as an example and read it from right to left:

| Level | Example | Who Is Responsible |
|---|---|---|
| Root | The usually omitted "." at the far right | The root server system, operated jointly by multiple organizations |
| Top-Level Domain (TLD) | `.com`, `.cn`, `.org` | The registry operator for each top-level domain |
| Second-level domain | `example.com` | The domain holder, registered through a registrar |
| Names further down | `www.example.com` | Configured by the holder |

Each level manages only its own segment and hands off authority over the level below through **delegation**. The root knows only which servers handle `.com`; it does not know where `www.example.com` is. The `.com` servers know which servers are responsible for `example.com`, but they don't know the addresses of specific hosts either. The final answer is held by `example.com`'s own **authoritative name server**.

This design resembles a postal address: country, province, city, and street are divided level by level, and no post office needs to remember the location of every household in the world. Hierarchical delegation keeps DNS manageable and frees it from dependence on any single central server. The basic DNS specifications are RFC 1034 and RFC 1035, both published in 1987, and they remain the foundation of the system today.

There is a common misconception about root servers: that "there are only 13 root servers in the world." More accurately, there are 13 root server names (a through m), and behind each name, **anycast** technology deploys a large number of instances around the world; publicly available figures put the total number of instances in the thousands. The instance closest to you answers your query.

## 6.4.2 A Complete Resolution: Recursion and Iteration

An ordinary user's device does not query the root servers itself; that work is handed to a **recursive resolver**. It is usually provided by your broadband ISP, and your home router hands its address out to phones and computers via DHCP; some people switch to a public DNS service instead. The whole process has two stages:

1. **Device → recursive resolver (recursive query)**: Your computer says, "Please just tell me the address of `www.example.com`," and then waits for the result. The program on your computer that sends this request is called the "stub resolver."
2. **Recursive resolver → authoritative servers at each level (iterative queries)**:
   - Ask the root: "Where is `www.example.com`?" The root replies: "I don't know—go ask the `.com` servers; here are their addresses."
   - Ask `.com`: it replies, "Go ask the authoritative servers for `example.com`."
   - Ask `example.com`'s authoritative server: it replies, "The IPv4 address of `www.example.com` is…"
3. The recursive resolver passes the result back to your computer, and only then does the browser begin establishing a connection.

Think of it as a front-desk concierge: you say one sentence to the front desk, and the front desk runs around to several departments on your behalf. Common record types in the answers include: A records (IPv4 addresses), AAAA records (IPv6 addresses), CNAME (an alias pointing to another name, a method content delivery networks often use to take over traffic), NS (indicating which server is responsible), and MX (mail servers).

## 6.4.3 Caching and TTL: The Secret to Speed, and a Source of Delay

If every query started from the root, the root and top-level domain servers would be overwhelmed, and users would wait a long time. DNS gets most of its efficiency from **caching**: browsers, operating systems, home routers, and recursive resolvers may all temporarily store the results they look up.

How long a cached entry can be kept is determined by the record's own **Time To Live (TTL)**, measured in seconds and set by the domain holder on the authoritative server. Typical values range from tens of seconds to a full day: sites that frequently change their routing set short values, while stable records get long ones. Recursive resolvers also cache intermediate information such as "who handles `.com`," so most queries never need to ask the root servers again. Results that could not be found are also cached for a while; this is called negative caching (RFC 2308).

Caching has two consequences:

- **Fast**: On a second visit, the answer is often found on your own machine or inside the resolver, cutting the time from tens of milliseconds to nearly zero.
- **Slow**: After a website changes its server address, caches everywhere must wait for the TTL to expire before updating. This is what operations staff mean when they say "DNS changes take time to propagate." DNS has no unified "broadcast update" action, so old and new answers coexist for a period of time.

## 6.4.4 Weak Points and Defenses: DNSSEC and Encrypted DNS

Early DNS queries typically used UDP, were transmitted in plaintext, and carried no signatures, which created two categories of risk:

| Risk | How It Works | Defenses |
|---|---|---|
| Forged responses, cache poisoning | An attacker impersonates the authoritative server and races to return a fake answer; once cached by the resolver, it affects large numbers of users | Randomization of source ports and query IDs; **DNSSEC** (DNS Security Extensions, RFC 4033 and related) uses digital signatures to verify answers level by level |
| Eavesdropping or tampering | Devices along the path can see which sites you are looking up | **DoT** (DNS over TLS, RFC 7858) and **DoH** (DNS over HTTPS, RFC 8484) encrypt the segment between the device and the resolver |

Note the division of labor between the two technologies: DNSSEC ensures the answer "is genuine" but does not provide confidentiality; DoT/DoH ensure the transmission "isn't snooped on" but cannot prove that the content provided by the authoritative server hasn't been forged. They solve different problems. As of this writing, DNSSEC deployment remains uneven across top-level domains and websites; many mainstream browsers and operating systems already support encrypted DNS, though default settings vary by product and version.

For ordinary users, the most practical precautions are: use a trustworthy resolution service, keep your router firmware up to date, and change the router's default admin password. Once someone alters your home router's DNS settings, every device in the house using default settings could be directed to the wrong addresses.

## 6.4.5 Summary

DNS uses hierarchical delegation to stay "manageable," a combination of recursive and iterative queries to make sure answers "can be found," and caching with TTL to make lookups "fast," then relies on DNSSEC and encrypted transport to make up for the security shortcomings of its early design. That brief pause before a web page opens is this globally distributed database at work on your behalf. Once it has the IP address, the browser needs to establish an encrypted connection with the server, which is the subject of the next section: TLS.
