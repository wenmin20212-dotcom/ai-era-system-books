# 6.5 HTTPS and TLS: How Encrypted Connections Are Established

In your browser's address bar, most web addresses begin with "https://", and next to them sits a small padlock icon (at the time of writing, browsers style this differently; some have replaced it with a "tune" icon, but clicking it still shows a message such as "Connection is secure"). Click it and you'll see the site's certificate information: who it was issued to, who issued it, and when it expires. Behind this little icon lies a negotiation process that completes within a few dozen milliseconds of your pressing Enter. In the previous section we saw how DNS turns names into addresses; in Section 6.2, TCP established a reliable connection. But reliability alone isn't enough. Packets must pass through your home router, your ISP's equipment, internet exchange points, and other parties' backbone networks, and any link along the way could potentially "see" or "alter" the content. What HTTPS sets out to solve is exactly this: how to "whisper secrets safely on a road you don't control."

## 6.5.1 Three Hazards of the Plaintext Era

HTTPS stands for "Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure" (HTTP Secure), and is essentially "HTTP running on top of TLS." TLS stands for Transport Layer Security, and its predecessor was SSL (Secure Sockets Layer). Today every version of SSL has been formally deprecated by the standards documents, yet the term "SSL certificate" lives on in everyday speech.

If only plain HTTP is used, web content travels across the network in plaintext and faces three kinds of problems:

| Hazard | Everyday analogy | Corresponding protection TLS provides |
|---|---|---|
| Eavesdropping | Anyone can read a postcard | Confidentiality: content is encrypted |
| Tampering | Someone changes a few words in a letter in transit | Integrity: any modification can be detected |
| Impersonation | The sender is actually a con artist | Authentication: a certificate proves "I really am this website" |

All three are indispensable. Encrypting without authenticating identity means you may end up with a "highly secure" encrypted channel to an impostor, which is like locking your secrets in a safe and then handing it to a thief.

## 6.5.2 Two Kinds of Keys: Symmetric and Asymmetric Encryption

To understand TLS, you first need to distinguish two kinds of cryptographic tools.

**Symmetric encryption**: Both parties use the same key to encrypt and decrypt. It is fast and suited to large volumes of data; a common algorithm is AES. The difficulty is this: how can two strangers agree on the same key over a line that someone may be listening to?

**Asymmetric encryption** (public-key cryptography): Each party has a pair of keys. The public key can be shared openly, while the private key is kept secret. Anything "locked" with the public key can only be opened with the private key; a "signature" made with the private key can be verified by anyone using the public key. It solves the problem of "how strangers establish trust," but it is computationally expensive.

TLS's approach combines the strengths of both: it first uses asymmetric techniques to authenticate identity and negotiate keys, and then uses the resulting symmetric key to encrypt the actual web data. The common method for negotiating keys is called "key exchange," for example Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman Ephemeral (ECDHE). Its cleverness lies in this: each side publishes part of its values, and each can then compute the same secret, while an onlooker who sees all the public values still cannot compute it. As a loose analogy: two people each add their own secret color to a bucket of shared paint and swap buckets; in the end both sides mix the same color, but no outsider can separate the colors back out.

## 6.5.3 Certificates and the Chain of Trust: Who Proves "You Are You"

How does a server prove that it really is example.com? With a **digital certificate**. A certificate contains the domain name, the server's public key, the validity period, and the issuer's signature. The issuing organization is called a **Certificate Authority** (CA).

Browsers and operating systems come preinstalled with a set of "root certificates," which serve as the starting point of trust. Verification forms a **chain of trust**:

1. The website's certificate is signed by an "intermediate CA";
2. The intermediate CA's certificate is signed by a "root CA";
3. The root CA's certificate is preinstalled on your device and trusted directly.

The browser checks the signatures level by level, verifies that the domain name matches, and confirms the certificate hasn't expired or been revoked. If any link fails, a warning such as "Your connection is not private" pops up. This warning deserves to be taken seriously: if you encounter it on public Wi-Fi, the safest course is not to proceed, and certainly not to enter any passwords.

There is also the "Certificate Transparency" mechanism (RFC 6962 and others): certificates issued by CAs must be recorded in publicly auditable logs, making misissued or abusively issued certificates easier to detect. According to public sources, the emergence of free CAs such as Let's Encrypt and the spread of automated issuance tools are among the main reasons HTTPS has become so widespread over the past decade.

## 6.5.4 A TLS 1.3 Handshake

At the time of writing, the mainstream version is TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446, published in 2018), while the older TLS 1.2 is still used on some systems. After the TCP three-way handshake completes, a full TLS 1.3 handshake goes roughly as follows:

1. **ClientHello**: The browser states which cipher algorithms it supports, attaches its key exchange parameters, and writes the domain it wants to reach in the "Server Name Indication" (SNI) field—a single server often hosts many websites and needs this to pick the matching certificate.
2. **ServerHello and what follows**: The server selects the algorithms and sends back its own key exchange parameters. At this point both sides can compute the shared key, so everything the server sends afterward—the certificate, the signature proving it holds the private key, and the handshake-finished message—is already encrypted.
3. **Finished**: After verifying that the certificate and signature are valid, the browser sends its own finished message and can immediately send an encrypted HTTP request.

Whereas TLS 1.2 needs two round trips, TLS 1.3 needs only one; for recently visited websites, "session resumption" and even "0-RTT" allow data to be sent earlier (0-RTT carries replay risks, and the standard restricts its use). TLS 1.3 also removed a batch of outdated, insecure algorithms and dropped static RSA key exchange; full handshakes uniformly use (EC)DHE key exchange with "forward secrecy": even if the server's private key leaks later, previously recorded communications cannot be decrypted.

Be aware of the limits of what HTTPS protects: it encrypts the content, but the IP address you connect to, the timing of communication, and the volume of data remain visible. The domain name in SNI is still plaintext by default in TLS 1.3, and the related encryption scheme (ECH) was still being gradually deployed at the time of writing. The icon also indicates only that "the channel is secure and the other party genuinely holds a certificate for this domain"; it does not guarantee that the website itself is trustworthy—phishing sites can obtain valid certificates too.

## 6.5.5 From the Handshake to the Next Chapter

Looking back at a single web visit: DNS finds the address, TCP establishes the connection, TLS performs another handshake, and only then does the HTTP request actually set off. Each additional round trip adds latency, which is especially noticeable on transoceanic links. This is precisely the starting point of the next chapter on QUIC and HTTP/3: merging the transport and encryption handshakes so that "secure" and "fast" no longer hold each other back.
