# 7.4 Software-Defined Networking and Network Virtualization

Open the admin page of your home router, and you can rename your Wi-Fi network, set up a guest network, or limit how long your child's tablet can stay online. Click "Save," and the router starts working under the new rules. Hidden inside this small action is a big question. With just one router at home, changes are easy. But a carrier or a data center may have thousands upon thousands of switches and routers. If engineers have to log in to each one individually and type configurations line by line, how long would a single policy change take? And if something goes wrong, how would anyone track it down? Software-defined networking and network virtualization emerged to answer exactly this question.

## 7.4.1 Traditional Networks: Every Device "Makes Its Own Decisions"

As earlier sections explained, a router does two things. First, it exchanges routing information with its neighbors and works out where traffic should go. Second, it uses those results to send packets out through the right port. Engineers call the first job the **control plane**, responsible for "making decisions," and the second the **data plane** (also called the forwarding plane), responsible for "doing the work."

In traditional networks, both parts live in the same device. Each device runs its own routing protocols and computes its own paths. The advantage is that there is no single point of failure: if one device breaks, the rest keep working, which is one source of the Internet's resilience. The cost is decentralized management:

- Policies must be broken into individual commands and written into each device separately;
- Devices from different vendors use different command formats, so engineers must speak several "dialects";
- Trying out a new forwarding method often means waiting for vendors to update their device software.

To use an analogy, a traditional network is like a city without a traffic control center, where the officer at each intersection directs traffic based only on what they can see. Most of the time this works, but adjusting routes across the whole city means notifying one intersection at a time.

## 7.4.2 Software-Defined Networking: Centralizing the "Brain"

The core idea of **Software-Defined Networking** (SDN) is to pull the control plane out of individual devices and hand it to a centralized software system called the **controller**. Switches keep only the data plane and forward packets according to rules sent down by the controller.

This approach is usually described in three layers:

| Layer | Role | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Application layer | Expresses intent, such as "guest traffic may not enter the finance department" | City government setting traffic policy |
| Control layer (controller) | Maintains the network-wide topology and translates intent into specific rules for each device | Traffic control center |
| Infrastructure layer (switches) | Looks up tables and forwards according to rules | Traffic lights at intersections |

The controller and switches need a common "language" to communicate. One of the earliest protocols promoted by academia and industry was **OpenFlow**. It originated in research at Stanford University and other institutions, and its standardization was later driven by the Open Networking Foundation (ONF). OpenFlow abstracts forwarding rules into "match–action" flow tables: if fields such as a packet's addresses and ports satisfy a match condition, the corresponding action is executed—forwarding out a certain port, dropping the packet, rewriting fields, or handing it to the controller.

It's worth noting that SDN is a design philosophy, not a synonym for OpenFlow. In systems deployed at the time of writing, controllers and devices also commonly communicate through management protocols such as NETCONF and gNMI, or through vendor-specific interfaces. Some solutions don't fully remove the control plane from devices at all; they simply use centralized software to orchestrate configuration and policy. Whether a system counts as SDN depends on whether it has the defining traits—**a centralized view, programmability, and separation of control from forwarding**—not on which protocol it uses.

## 7.4.3 Network Virtualization: Carving Many Logical Networks from One Physical Network

SDN addresses "how to manage"; network virtualization addresses "how to divide."

Start with a familiar example. On the same switch in an office, finance department computers and guest computers are placed in different **Virtual LANs** (VLANs) and cannot see each other—like partitioning a building into several separate rooms. VLAN is one of the earliest widely adopted network virtualization technologies. Defined by the IEEE 802.1Q standard, it distinguishes networks with a 12-bit identifier, allowing a theoretical maximum of about four thousand.

In the cloud computing era, four thousand is not enough. A cloud data center may host a large number of tenants, each wanting its own "private network" with its own address plan—even if those addresses overlap with other tenants'. This gave rise to the **overlay network** approach: on top of the existing physical network (called the underlay), each tenant packet is "sealed in an envelope" with an extra outer header, then unwrapped when it reaches the destination server. A common encapsulation format is **VXLAN** (Virtual Extensible LAN, see RFC 7348), which uses a 24-bit identifier to distinguish about sixteen million virtual networks; the later Geneve (RFC 8926) was designed to be more flexible and extensible.

This is much like shipping a package: you put a gift in your own box (the tenant's original packet), and the courier puts that box into a standard shipping container (the outer header). Along the way, only the address on the container is read; at the destination, your box is taken out and delivered to the recipient. Devices along the path don't need to understand each tenant's internal arrangements.

A companion idea is **Network Functions Virtualization** (NFV): firewalls, load balancers, and the various network elements in a carrier's core network used to be dedicated hardware boxes, but can now run as software on general-purpose servers—spun up when needed, shut down when not. This concept was driven mainly by telecom operators through the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI).

## 7.4.4 Where They're Used and What They Cost

Based on public information at the time of writing, SDN and network virtualization are most heavily applied in roughly three scenarios:

1. **Cloud data centers**: Tenants create private networks, subnets, and security rules with a few clicks in a web console, while behind the scenes the controller translates those intentions into overlay network configurations. Data centers and cloud computing are covered in detail in a separate volume of this series.
2. **Wide area networks**: Some large Internet companies have publicly described using centralized control to schedule traffic between their own data centers—Google, for example, has published papers on its B4 network. This is how these companies run their private networks; it does not mean carriers on the public Internet do the same. Between carriers, each still makes its own decisions using BGP, covered in the previous chapter.
3. **Enterprise branch connectivity**: So-called SD-WAN manages the multiple links between headquarters and branch offices (leased lines, broadband, mobile networks) in a unified way, choosing paths by application type.

Centralization brings convenience, but also new risks:

- **The controller becomes a critical point**: If it fails or is compromised, the whole network is affected. In practice, engineers typically deploy clusters of controllers as mutual backups and tightly restrict access.
- **Configuration errors get amplified**: A mistyped command used to affect one device; now a single faulty policy can be pushed to every device in an instant. Mature teams therefore validate changes in simulated environments first, roll them out in staged batches, and keep a way to roll back quickly.
- **Troubleshooting gains an extra layer**: In an overlay network, one packet is wrapped inside another, so a successful ping on the underlay doesn't mean the tenant network works. When troubleshooting, you must identify which layer the problem lies in.

## 7.4.5 Summary: Networks Are Starting to Be Written Like Software

Think back to that "Save" button on the router admin page. What SDN does is turn the network of an entire city or data center into one "settings page" that can be modified uniformly, version-controlled, and executed automatically. Network virtualization, meanwhile, lets a single physical network be sliced into many logical networks that don't interfere with each other—much as one server can be split into many virtual machines.

Yet neither changes the Internet's basic rules: packets are still forwarded hop by hop on a best-effort basis, and across carriers, BGP still connects the autonomous systems. What SDN changes is "who sets the rules and how they are delivered." No matter how well the rules are designed, you still need to see how the network is actually behaving to know whether they work.
