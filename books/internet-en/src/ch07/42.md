# 7.5 Network Measurement and Troubleshooting: ping, traceroute, and Monitoring

It's nine o'clock at night, and the video you're watching suddenly stutters like a slideshow while web pages spin endlessly without loading. Most people's first instinct is to restart the router. Sometimes that works, sometimes it doesn't. The problem might lie between the phone and the router, or on the carrier's lines, or the website on the other end might itself be down. A network engineer facing the same situation measures first and draws conclusions second. This section introduces a few of the most commonly used measurement tools and the principles behind them. These tools are all read-only diagnostic aids, meant for seeing your own network clearly, and have nothing to do with attacks.

## 7.5.1 ping: Shout Into the Distance and Listen for the Echo

The name ping is borrowed from the "echo" of sonar. When you type `ping` followed by a web address at your computer's command line, the computer sends a special small packet to the target called an "Echo Request," and the target replies with an "Echo Reply" upon receiving it. Both messages belong to the Internet Control Message Protocol (ICMP), the "accompanying messenger" of the IP protocol, dedicated to carrying diagnostic and error information. The specification for the IPv4 version is RFC 792.

Two numbers in ping's output deserve the most attention:

- **Round-Trip Time (RTT)**: how many milliseconds the trip there and back took. Pinging your home router from a home computer usually takes no more than a few milliseconds; pinging a server in the same city commonly takes a dozen to several dozen milliseconds; transoceanic links commonly run over a hundred milliseconds or even higher. Light travels through optical fiber at roughly two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum, so distance alone sets a lower bound. No network, however good, can outrun physics.
- **Packet loss rate**: send ten packets, and see how many come back. Losing one or two occasionally isn't necessarily a problem, but persistent packet loss indicates that some segment of the link is congested or of poor quality.

A word of caution: a failed ping does not necessarily mean the website is broken. For security reasons, many servers and firewalls choose not to respond to ICMP requests. In that case the web page may open normally while ping keeps reporting "Request timed out." Ping offers a clue, not a verdict.

## 7.5.2 traceroute: Calling the Roll of Every Stop Along the Way

ping only tells you whether the path between two ends is "up, and how fast," but not who lies in between. traceroute (called `tracert` on Windows) solves exactly this problem: it lists every router a packet passes through along the way.

It makes use of a field in the IP header: Time To Live (TTL). As Chapter 3 explained, each time a packet passes through a router, its TTL is decremented by 1; when it reaches 0, the router discards the packet and sends an ICMP "Time Exceeded" message back to the sender. This design was originally meant to keep packets from circling endlessly in routing loops, and traceroute cleverly borrows it:

1. First, send a packet with TTL=1. The first router discards it and reports back, so you learn who the first hop is;
2. Then send a packet with TTL=2 to discover the second hop;
3. Keep incrementing until the packet reaches its destination.

Each line of output is one hop, usually showing the router's address and three latency measurements. A few rules of thumb for reading the results:

| Observation | Common meaning |
|---|---|
| A line shows `* * *` | That router doesn't respond or rate-limits its responses; if later hops return to normal, it's most likely not a fault |
| Latency jumps sharply at a certain hop and stays high | Possibly entering a long-distance link (such as a transoceanic one), or congestion begins here |
| Only one middle hop shows high latency, and later hops drop back down | Usually that router gives diagnostic messages low priority; it doesn't mean forwarding is slow |
| No responses from a certain hop onward | The fault or filtering is likely somewhere near here |

Also remember that on the Internet, the outbound and return paths are often different (this relates to how each network chooses its own routes via BGP, discussed in Chapter 6), and traceroute can only see the outbound half.

## 7.5.3 From Near to Far: A Troubleshooting Approach

Tools are only means; troubleshooting depends on method. The most practical approach follows the structure of this book, "from near to far, eliminating segment by segment":

- **Start with the device**: Do other websites open? Does another device work fine? If only one device has trouble, check that device first.
- **Then the home network**: Ping the router's address (commonly a private address like 192.168.x.1). If there's packet loss or latency swings up and down, the problem is most likely the Wi-Fi signal or the router itself.
- **Then name resolution**: If a web address won't open but connecting directly by IP address works, the problem may lie with DNS. You can use `nslookup` to look up the address for a domain name, and if necessary switch DNS servers for comparison.
- **Then the carrier and the far end**: Run traceroute to the target and see at which hop things start to go wrong. If problems appear in the first hop or two just outside your home, contact your broadband provider; if the problem is near the target website, often all you can do is wait.

The value of this sequence is that each step cuts the possibilities in half, making it faster and more reliable than "restarting everything" on a hunch.

## 7.5.4 From a Single Measurement to Continuous Monitoring

For individual users, an occasional ping is enough, but websites and carriers need to watch nonstop. Network monitoring falls roughly into two categories:

- **Active measurement**: actively sending probe traffic, such as pinging on a schedule or periodically loading web pages and recording load times. The speed-test websites mentioned earlier also fall into this category. Some research institutions and organizations deploy large numbers of probes around the world to continuously measure Internet connectivity, such as the RIPE Atlas project operated by RIPE NCC, the European Regional Internet Registry.
- **Passive measurement**: sending no extra packets, but instead tallying the real traffic already present on devices. Routers can summarize records by "flow"—who communicated with whom and how many bytes were used; devices can also report port traffic and error counts via mechanisms such as the Simple Network Management Protocol (SNMP).

Operations teams aggregate this data into graphs and alert rules, for example, "notify the on-call staff if a link's utilization stays above a certain threshold." The traffic tides discussed in Chapter 5 were drawn using exactly this kind of monitoring.

From a tiny ping sent with a press of the Enter key to probe networks spanning the globe, measurement makes the invisible Internet observable. It is also the common foundation on which the key technologies in this chapter are put into practice: how far IPv6 deployment has come, whether QUIC has delivered speedups, whether CDN nodes are healthy—all of these must ultimately be answered by measurement.
