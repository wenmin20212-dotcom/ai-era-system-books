# 7.1 IPv6: When Addresses Run Out

Open your phone's Settings, go to the details page for your current Wi-Fi or mobile network, and scroll down a bit. Many people will see two kinds of addresses. One is the familiar four-part number like "192.168.1.23." The other looks strange: something like "240e:3a1:xxxx:xxxx::1," separated by colons and sprinkled with letters. The latter is an IPv6 address. The reason it exists is simple: there were no longer enough of the original addresses to go around.

## 7.1.1 Why Four Billion Isn't Enough

As discussed in Chapter 3, the most common IP addresses today are defined by Internet Protocol version 4 (IPv4). They are 32 bits long, which gives a theoretical total of about 4.3 billion. When the protocol was designed in the 1970s and 1980s, that number seemed more than ample, since the machines on the network were mostly large computers at universities and research institutions.

What happened next is well known: personal computers, phones, tablets, smart TVs, cameras, fitness bands… Each person may have several connected devices nearby, and the number of connected devices worldwide has long since passed several billion. Worse still, addresses can never be used at 100 percent efficiency, and early allocation was fairly generous, with large address blocks going to a small number of organizations. In 2011, the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA), which is responsible for global address allocation, handed out the last large blocks of IPv4 addresses to the five Regional Internet Registries (RIRs). After that, the regions one by one entered a phase in which only small amounts of the remaining addresses could be allocated, under strict policies.

Faced with exhaustion, engineers had two paths:

| Approach | Method | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Conserve | Network Address Translation (NAT), letting all the devices in a home, or even an entire residential complex, share one public address | Hard for outside parties to initiate connections inward; peer-to-peer applications become more complicated; carrier-grade NAT adds equipment and troubleshooting burden |
| Expand capacity | Switch to IPv6, a new protocol with longer addresses | Requires upgrades across the whole chain: end devices, routers, servers, and applications |

NAT, introduced in Chapter 5, belongs to the first path. It really did help the internet hold on for many years, but at heart it is a patch. IPv6 takes the second path.

## 7.1.2 What an IPv6 Address Looks Like

The core specification of Internet Protocol version 6 (IPv6) is RFC 8200, and the address architecture is described in RFC 4291. The most obvious change is that addresses grow from 32 bits to 128 bits, giving roughly 3.4×10³⁸ possible addresses. That number is almost unimaginably large; popular science writing often says there would be enough to give every grain of sand on Earth its own address and still have plenty left over.

Written in decimal, 128 bits would be far too long, so IPv6 uses hexadecimal: eight groups of 16 bits each, separated by colons, for example:

`2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0000:0001`

To make addresses easier to write, there are two shortening rules: leading zeros in each group can be dropped, and a run of consecutive all-zero groups can be replaced by a single "::", though this may only be done once per address. So the address above can be shortened to `2001:db8::1`. (The address range beginning with 2001:db8 is reserved by the standard specifically for documentation and examples and should not be used on real networks.)

An IPv6 address is usually split into two halves. The first 64 bits are the "network prefix," assigned by the service provider, like the name of a residential complex and building number. The last 64 bits are the "interface identifier," which identifies the specific device, like an apartment number. The modem or router in your home often receives an entire prefix from the provider and then hands addresses out to each device in the house, so every device can have its own public address.

## 7.1.3 More Than Just Longer: Several Design Improvements

If IPv6 merely made addresses longer, it would not be worth so much effort to upgrade. The designers took the opportunity to make several structural adjustments as well:

- **A simpler, fixed-length header.** The IPv4 header has a variable length and many fields; the IPv6 base header is a fixed 40 bytes, with optional features moved into "extension headers," making processing more regular for routers.
- **Routers no longer fragment.** In IPv4, when a packet is too large, a router along the way may cut it into pieces. IPv6 hands this job to the sender: a router that finds a packet too large drops it and sends back a message, and the sender adjusts the size. This reflects the idea of "keeping complexity at the endpoints," which Chapter 8 will discuss.
- **Addresses can configure themselves.** Stateless Address Autoconfiguration (SLAAC, RFC 4862) lets a device hear the prefix advertised by a router and assemble a complete address on its own, without necessarily needing a DHCP server. For privacy reasons, mainstream operating systems now usually generate random temporary addresses that change periodically (RFC 8981), so that a fixed second half cannot be used to track the device online over long periods.
- **Neighbor Discovery replaces ARP.** To find a neighbor's hardware address on the same local network, IPv6 uses the Neighbor Discovery Protocol (NDP), which is based on ICMPv6.

It should be made clear that IPv6 does not make networks "inherently more secure" or "inherently faster." Encryption still relies on TLS, covered in Chapter 6; speed depends on the path, congestion, and server location. In fact, having public addresses makes it all the more important to rely on the router's firewall to block externally initiated connections by default. Most mainstream home routers are already set up this way, and readers can check this on their router's admin page.

## 7.1.4 How Old and New Coexist

IPv4 and IPv6 header formats are incompatible with each other: a device that only understands IPv4 cannot talk directly to one that only understands IPv6. The entire internet cannot shut down one day and switch over all at once, so the transition period will be long. There are three common approaches:

1. **Dual stack**: Devices have both IPv4 and IPv6 addresses, running the two in parallel. Most phones and computers today work this way. When a browser queries DNS, it requests both the A record (IPv4 address) and the AAAA record (IPv6 address), and uses a mechanism called "Happy Eyeballs" (RFC 8305) to race the two connections, using whichever succeeds first, so the user isn't left waiting if one side is broken.
2. **Tunneling**: IPv6 packets are wrapped inside IPv4 packets to cross a stretch of network that only supports IPv4, like packing a small box inside a big box for shipping.
3. **Translation**: For example, NAT64 combined with DNS64 lets IPv6-only devices reach IPv4-only websites. Some mobile carriers use this kind of approach in their networks.

As for deployment progress, it varies greatly across countries and providers. According to public measurement statistics (such as IPv6 access ratios published by large websites or research institutions), at the time of writing a substantial share of user traffic worldwide already travels over IPv6, and in some countries the share exceeds half. China has also deployed IPv6 at large scale in recent years under policy encouragement, and mobile networks and home broadband generally support it. The exact figures change quickly; readers can search for the latest statistics published by the relevant organizations.

## 7.1.5 Back to Your Phone

Look again at that colon-filled address on your settings page, and you can now read it: the first half comes from your provider, and the second half may be a temporary identifier randomly generated by the system that will change after a while. When you visit a website that supports both protocols, your phone has quite possibly already gone over IPv6 without your noticing at all, which is exactly what a successful transition should look like.

IPv6 solves the problem of whether there are enough addresses; it is an upgrade at the network layer. Above it, the transport layer is changing too: TCP handshakes are too slow and head-of-line blocking drags down page loading, which gave rise to QUIC and HTTP/3.
