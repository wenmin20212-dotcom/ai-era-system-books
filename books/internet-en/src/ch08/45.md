# 8.2 Queueing Theory and Network Congestion: Why "Traffic Jams" Happen

It's nine in the evening, and you're streaming videos at home when the picture suddenly freezes on a spinning wheel. At the same moment, a family member in another room is on a video call, and the other side says their voice is "choppy." Yet your broadband plan promises hundreds of megabits per second, and speed tests really do hit that number. If the connection is fast enough, why does it still lag? Answering this question requires a branch of mathematics that seems unrelated to networking: queueing theory. It studies the cycle of "arrive—wait—get served—leave," and bank tellers, highway toll booths, and data packets inside routers all follow the same set of rules.

## 8.2.1 A "Line" Hidden Inside the Router

As Chapter 6 explained, the Internet uses packet switching: data is cut into individual packets, and when a router receives one, it looks up its table and forwards it. Forwarding takes time, and an outgoing link can send only one packet at a time. If several incoming ports simultaneously receive packets headed for the same outgoing link, the later arrivals must first be placed in a buffer to wait their turn.

So the time it takes a packet to travel from your phone to a server can be broken down into several parts:

| Delay component | Meaning | Does it vary with congestion? |
|---|---|---|
| Processing delay | The router looks up its table and checks the packet header | Essentially fixed, very small |
| Transmission delay | "Pushing" the entire packet onto the link; depends on packet length and link rate | Fixed |
| Propagation delay | The signal traveling the distance through fiber or cable | Fixed, determined by distance |
| Queueing delay | Waiting in the buffer for packets ahead to be sent | **Varies dramatically** |

The first three are like the distance of a drive and the traffic lights along the way—largely predictable. The fourth is like a traffic jam: sometimes present, sometimes not, sometimes long, sometimes short. Most of the time, the "lag" we feel is queueing delay at work. And when the buffer fills up, newly arriving packets can only be discarded—this is packet loss.

## 8.2.2 The Closer to Full Load, the Steeper the Wait

One of the most basic quantities in queueing theory is utilization, usually written as ρ (pronounced "rho"), which equals "arrival rate ÷ service rate." For example, if an outgoing link can send 1,000 packets per second and an average of 800 arrive per second, the utilization is 80%.

Intuitively, 80% utilization leaves 20% headroom, which should be plenty. But queueing theory says otherwise. In the most classic and most frequently cited simplified textbook model (random arrivals, random service times, a single server, denoted M/M/1), the average queueing wait is proportional to ρ/(1−ρ). Plugging in a few numbers reveals its temperament:

| Utilization ρ | ρ/(1−ρ) (relative waiting) |
|---|---|
| 50% | 1 |
| 80% | 4 |
| 90% | 9 |
| 99% | 99 |

Going from 50% to 90% utilization, the load increases by less than double, yet the wait grows roughly ninefold; as it approaches 100%, the wait practically "explodes." Real network traffic doesn't perfectly fit this model, but the general shape—"the closer to full load, the more sharply waiting rises"—holds universally. Highways are the same: at 70–80% capacity, traffic still flows smoothly, but add just a little more and it abruptly goes from slow-moving to standstill.

This explains the puzzle from the opening: during a speed test, you have the line to yourself, so even running at full speed, you aren't queueing behind anyone. But during evening peak hours, many people are using the neighborhood uplink and the carrier's links at the same time, pushing up utilization on some segment, and queueing delay and packet loss emerge.

## 8.2.3 Randomness and Bursts: Enough on Average Doesn't Mean Enough at Every Moment

There's an even subtler reason: traffic doesn't arrive evenly. Loading a webpage often requests dozens of files in an instant, and a video player buffers a large chunk at once, then goes quiet for a while. This "in fits and starts" pattern of arrivals is called a burst.

Suppose a link can handle 1,000 packets per second and the average arrival in a given second is 600 packets—seemingly plenty of room. But if half of those 600 packets crowd in during the first 0.1 second, that instant far exceeds the processing capacity, and a queue forms immediately. Queueing theory tells us that for the same average load, the more irregular the arrivals, the longer the average wait. This is also why carriers planning their networks generally avoid running links near full load for extended periods and instead reserve headroom—exactly how much varies with each operator's engineering experience, and there's no single standard value.

## 8.2.4 Buffers: Too Small Means Packet Loss, Too Large Means "Bloat"

Since queueing is unavoidable, wouldn't it be better to make buffers larger, letting packets wait a bit longer instead of being dropped? Many early devices did exactly that, which led to a problem known as bufferbloat, widely discussed in network engineering circles around 2010.

Here's the reasoning: as Chapter 6 explained, TCP congestion control relies mainly on "packet loss" to detect network congestion and then slows down its sending. When buffers are too large, packets aren't dropped; instead, they pile up in an ever-growing queue. The sender never receives the "time to slow down" signal and keeps stuffing more in, making the queue even longer. In the end, large file downloads may seem reasonably fast, but games, voice calls, and video conferences on the same line see latency soar to hundreds of milliseconds or more. Home routers, with their limited upstream bandwidth and potentially oversized buffers, are a common place for this phenomenon to occur.

The engineering community's countermeasure is called Active Queue Management (AQM): rather than waiting for the buffer to fill, when it detects the queue persistently growing, it drops or marks a small number of packets early to prompt senders to slow down. The IETF has published recommendations on this (such as RFC 7567) and standardized algorithms such as CoDel (RFC 8289), FQ-CoDel (RFC 8290), and PIE (RFC 8033). There is also Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN, RFC 3168), which can signal congestion by "marking" packets instead of "dropping" them. Some home router firmware offers "Smart Queue Management" or similar options; names vary by vendor, and support differed across manufacturers at the time of writing.

| Buffer strategy | Result |
|---|---|
| Too small | Packets drop as soon as a burst arrives; throughput can't climb |
| Too large and unmanaged | No packet loss but high latency; real-time applications stutter |
| Moderate + active queue management | Balances throughput and low latency |

## 8.2.5 Summary: Congestion Is the Price of Sharing

Looking back, a "traffic jam" isn't a network malfunction but an inevitable cost of packet switching: everyone shares the same links, which yields extremely high utilization efficiency but also means queueing must be endured. Queueing theory reveals three rules: waiting rises steeply as utilization approaches full load; the burstier the traffic, the more likely queues form; and buffers should be "sufficient but not excessive," paired with active management. TCP congestion control, AQM, and carriers' capacity planning are all, at heart, homework done around this invisible queue.

But the queue is only half the problem. Before a packet enters any queue, it must first decide which path to take—choose the route well, and it can bypass congested intersections.
