# 8.4 Metcalfe's Law and Network Effects

Think about the messaging app on your phone: why did you choose it in the first place? Probably not because its interface is the prettiest, but because your family, classmates, and coworkers all use it. If you were the only person in the world who had installed it, it would be completely useless; each friend who joins makes it a little more valuable. Email works the same way: you can write to any email address, no matter which company provides the recipient's mailbox. The previous sections looked at how packets queue and how paths are computed. This section takes a different angle: why "being connected" creates value in itself, and why that value grows so sharply with scale.

## 8.4.1 From Telephones to "Number of Connections": Metcalfe's Observation

The first person to express this intuition as a simple relationship was Robert Metcalfe, one of the inventors of Ethernet. According to the account commonly found in public sources, he used the following argument while selling Ethernet equipment in the 1980s: the cost of a network grows roughly linearly with the number of devices, while the value of a network grows roughly with the number of "possible pairs of connections." Later this was summarized as **Metcalfe's Law**: the value of a network is roughly proportional to the square of the number of its users.

You don't need a math background to understand the "square" here. Just count the lines:

| Number of terminals in the network | Possible pairwise connections |
|---|---|
| 2 | 1 |
| 5 | 10 |
| 10 | 45 |
| 100 | 4,950 |

The pattern is this: n devices can have at most n×(n−1)/2 pairwise connections. Increase the number of devices tenfold, and the number of possible connections increases roughly a hundredfold. The early telephone network is the most intuitive example: a single, isolated telephone is useless, but once a city has ten thousand of them, each phone becomes far more useful.

It is worth stressing that the word "law" can be misleading. This is not a rigorously verified physical law like the conservation of energy. It is an empirical estimating model, a way of framing the problem that helps us think.

## 8.4.2 Challenges to the "Square" and Proposed Revisions

Metcalfe's Law has been controversial ever since it was proposed. There are three main objections:

1. **Not all connections are equal.** Your connection with your family is clearly worth more than a "theoretically possible" connection with a stranger on the other side of the planet. Most possible connections are never used.
2. **As a network grows, the marginal value of each new user may decline.** Some scholars have argued that network value grows more like n×log(n): slower than the square, but still faster than linear. In public discussion, this revision is often cited as the leading counterargument to the "square" view.
3. **There is an even stronger effect.** If a network lets people form groups on their own (class chats, interest communities), the number of possible "groups" grows even faster than the number of pairwise connections. This idea is sometimes called "Reed's Law."

These views predict different rates of growth, but they all point in the same direction: **the value of a network grows faster than its number of users**. This is what economists call a **network effect**, also known as a network externality: when one person joins a network, they benefit, and so does everyone who was already in it. For readers of this book, remembering that direction is enough. There is no need to settle which formula is more accurate.

## 8.4.3 Why the Internet "Snowballed"

Placing network effects back into the history of the Internet helps make sense of the evolution described in Chapter 1. In the 1980s and 1990s, the world had several competing computer networking technologies and a number of separate, independent networks. In the end, the TCP/IP-based Internet joined them together. This was not because it was best on every technical measure. A key reason was that it **could connect to other networks**.

Three design features amplified this effect:

- **Open standards.** The TCP/IP protocol specifications are published openly as RFC documents. Any vendor can implement them without seeking permission from any single company. Because the barrier to entry was low, many networks joined.
- **A network of networks.** The interconnection of autonomous systems, discussed in Chapter 4, means that each operator needs to interconnect with only a few neighbors to reach the entire world indirectly. A newly connected network can immediately reach every existing user, which is the most direct expression of the network effect.
- **The end-to-end principle.** As discussed in Section 8.1, the network core only forwards packets on a best-effort basis, so new applications can be developed at the endpoints. The World Wide Web, email, and video conferencing did not need prior approval from network operators. This allowed new uses for "connection" to keep emerging, further increasing the network's value.

The result was a positive feedback loop: the more people who were connected, the more worthwhile it became to build applications on the network; the more applications there were, the more people wanted to connect. This cycle drove the Internet's transformation from a research network into today's infrastructure, which connects billions of devices.

## 8.4.4 The Other Side of Network Effects: Lock-in and Concentration

Network effects don't only bring benefits. They also help explain some troubling phenomena.

**First, lock-in.** Even when a new messaging app comes along with better features, it is hard for you to switch on your own, because your friends are all still on the old one. These services usually don't interoperate with one another; each is a small closed network. Email offers a clear contrast: it is based on open protocols (such as SMTP, the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol), so users of different providers can exchange messages. The network effect belongs to the email system as a whole, not to any single company.

**Second, a winner-take-all tendency.** Among platform services that rely on network effects, the largest one tends to keep getting larger. Exactly how much market share any particular platform holds varies widely over time and by region, so no claims are made here. But the structural tendency for "scale to bring more scale" is widely recognized.

**Third, lessons for infrastructure.** The Internet's underlying layers have remained relatively open largely because of public protocol standards and multi-stakeholder governance (see Section 4.4). When the underlying layers are open, all participants share in the network effect. If those layers were carved up by a few closed systems, the value of the Internet as a whole would be carved up too.

| Comparison | Open-protocol networks (e.g., email, the Web) | Closed-platform networks (most instant messaging apps) |
|---|---|---|
| Who captures the network effect | The system as a whole | A single platform |
| User migration | Still interoperable after switching providers | Switching platforms often means losing contacts |
| New entrants | Can join simply by following the protocol | Must build a user base from scratch |

## 8.4.5 Summary: Connection Itself Is Value

The formula in Metcalfe's Law is not precise, but it captures the most fundamental economic logic of the Internet: **the more connections there are, the more valuable each one becomes**. This explains why the Internet won out in competition with other networking technologies, and it also explains today's platform concentration and user lock-in. With this in mind, if you look back at open standards, autonomous system interconnection, and the end-to-end principle, you will see that they are not just engineering choices. They are also institutional arrangements that let everyone share in the benefits of network effects.

However, "more openness and more connections" also means that risks can spread more easily. The next section discusses how the Internet balances openness, resilience, and security.
