# 1.3 The Boundaries of the Internet: The Internet, the World Wide Web, Local Area Networks, and Mobile Networks

You get home in the evening, and your phone automatically connects to the home Wi-Fi. You "cast" a document from your computer to the printer in the living room, open a news page in your phone's browser, then switch to WeChat to send a friend a voice message. When you head out and go downstairs, the Wi-Fi signal drops, and your phone quietly switches over to 4G or 5G—and the video keeps playing.

In everyday speech, we loosely call all of these things "going online." In fact, each one uses a different "network": some only make a round trip inside your home, while others cross oceans; some belong to the World Wide Web, while others have nothing to do with web pages at all. If you can't tell these boundaries apart, it's easy to get confused later when we discuss routing and domain names. This section puts these frequently conflated terms side by side and draws a clear line between each of them.

## 1.3.1 The Internet and the Web: The Highway and One Kind of Vehicle on It

The most common confusion is treating the "Internet" and the "World Wide Web" (abbreviated as the Web or WWW) as the same thing.

As discussed in the previous section, the Internet is a "network of networks": thousands upon thousands of independently operated networks interconnected according to the common IP protocol (Internet Protocol), so that any two connected devices can deliver data packets to each other. It is a piece of **infrastructure**, like a highway system covering the globe.

The World Wide Web, by contrast, is **one application** that runs on this highway system. It consists of three things:

- **How web pages are written**: HTML (HyperText Markup Language), which specifies what text, images, and links a page contains;
- **The addresses of web pages**: URLs (Uniform Resource Locators), such as the string beginning with `https://` in your browser's address bar;
- **The rules for fetching web pages**: HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) and its encrypted version, HTTPS.

The World Wide Web was proposed by Tim Berners-Lee around 1989 at the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN), whereas ARPANET, the predecessor of the Internet, was already running back in 1969. In other words, the highway came first, and only twenty years later did the "vehicle" known as the World Wide Web appear.

The Web isn't the only kind of vehicle on the highway. Sending and receiving email, playing online games, making video calls with instant messaging apps, having your phone sync its clock—all of these use the Internet, but they don't necessarily involve web pages. Many mobile apps don't use web pages for their interfaces; they may still use HTTP behind the scenes to exchange data with servers, but that is merely borrowing the Web's "pickup rules." The table below offers a comparison:

| Aspect | The Internet | The Web |
|---|---|---|
| Essence | Globally interconnected network infrastructure | An information service running on the Internet |
| Core rules | IP, TCP, etc. | HTML, URL, HTTP |
| When it appeared | Predecessor ARPANET, 1969 | Proposed around 1989 |
| Analogy | The highway system | One type of vehicle on the highway |
| What happens without it | No networked application could exist at all | Web pages won't load, but email, games, etc. still work |

This book uses "clicking open a web page" as its anchor because web pages are the most common and the easiest to observe; but always keep in mind that web pages are only one of the Internet's many uses.

## 1.3.2 Local Area Networks: Your Own Little "Backyard Plot" at Home

Back to the opening scenario of sending a document to the printer. That document very likely never left your home: the computer handed the data to the wireless router, the router passed it on to the printer, and the whole process took place inside the house. A small-scale network like this, covering a single household, office, or building, is called a **Local Area Network** (LAN).

LANs have several characteristics:

- **Small scope, clear ownership**: It belongs to you, your company, or your school, and you (or the network administrator) are in charge;
- **Commonly connected via Ethernet and Wi-Fi**: Wired connections use Ethernet, and wireless connections use Wi-Fi;
- **Uses "private addresses"**: The addresses your home devices receive mostly look like `192.168.x.x` or `10.x.x.x`. Addresses of this kind are designated in RFC 1918 for private networks only; they are valid only internally and cannot be reached directly from the wider Internet.

A LAN can operate entirely on its own without connecting to the Internet. Unplug the incoming fiber from your optical modem, and your computer can still send documents to the printer—it just can't open web pages from outside. Your home router sits right on the boundary: on one side is your LAN, and on the other it connects to the Internet through your service provider. It is responsible for "translating" internal private addresses into external ones, a technique called NAT (Network Address Translation), which Chapter 5 will cover in detail.

So the relationship between a LAN and the Internet is like that between your own yard and the highway outside: the paths in the yard form a self-contained system, and once the gate opens, they connect to the highway.

## 1.3.3 Mobile Networks: Another Entrance to the Internet

After you go downstairs and your phone switches to 4G/5G, you are using a **mobile communication network**, also called a cellular network. It consists of the carrier's base stations and core network. It was originally designed for phone calls and text messages, and only gradually came to carry large volumes of Internet traffic.

Two distinct ideas need to be separated here:

1. **The mobile network itself is a carrier's dedicated network.** Base stations handle the wireless signals, while the core network authenticates your SIM card, handles billing, and manages your handoffs between different base stations. Ordinary phone calls and text messages can use this network alone.
2. **The mobile network is also one way of accessing the Internet.** When you browse the web using mobile data, the data first passes through the base station and core network, then enters the Internet through the carrier's gateway.

In other words, a mobile network, like your home broadband, is an entrance that "connects" you to the Internet—one uses radio waves, the other uses optical fiber. From the Internet's point of view, a mobile carrier's network is also one member of the "network of networks." As for how base stations allocate spectrum and how phones hand off seamlessly between base stations, the *Mobile Communications* volume of this series will go into detail; the principles of Wi-Fi are left to the dedicated *Wi-Fi* volume.

## 1.3.4 Putting the Different "Networks" into One Picture

Now we can reclassify the activities from the opening:

| Everyday action | Network used | Does it enter the public Internet? |
|---|---|---|
| Computer sends a document to the home printer | LAN (Wi-Fi or Ethernet cable) | Usually not |
| Opening a news page in a browser | LAN → broadband access → Internet, using the Web service | Yes |
| Sending a WeChat voice message | Same as above, but using an instant messaging app rather than a web page | Yes |
| Continuing to watch video after going downstairs | Mobile network → Internet | Yes |
| Making an ordinary phone call on a mobile phone | Voice service of the mobile communication network | Traditionally does not pass through the public Internet |

(Note: With the spread of technologies such as VoLTE, at the time of writing many carriers already carry voice as IP packets within their own networks, but this is still the carrier's internal network and does not amount to traveling over the public Internet.)

Drawn as a diagram, it would look roughly like three circles: innermost is your home LAN, the next layer out is the carrier's access network (broadband or mobile), and the outermost is the Internet, formed by the interconnection of many networks. Applications such as the World Wide Web, email, video, and instant messaging are like different vehicles traveling along the roads formed by connecting these circles.

## 1.3.5 Summary: Distinguishing the "Road" from the "Vehicle," and the "Yard" from the "Entrance"

This section has drawn four boundaries: the Internet is globally interconnected infrastructure, and the World Wide Web is one application on top of it; a LAN is a small-scale network under your control, and the router is its gate to the Internet; a mobile network is a carrier's dedicated network, and at the same time a wireless entrance to the Internet. With these boundaries in mind, when the next section looks back at the evolution of the Internet, you'll be able to see clearly whether each change was a change in the "road," in the "vehicles" on it, or in the "entrances."
