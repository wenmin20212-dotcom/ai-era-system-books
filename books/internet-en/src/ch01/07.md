# 1.4 The Evolution of the Internet: From ARPANET to Mobile Internet and the Cloud

When you tap open a web page on your phone today, you almost never stop to think about the history behind it. Wind the clock back more than half a century, though, and a "network" was nothing more than a few dedicated lines between a handful of universities and research institutions, linking a few mainframe computers the size of wardrobes. Getting from those few nodes to today's billions of devices did not happen according to a single blueprint. The Internet "grew," layer by layer, driven by a series of concrete problems. Understanding this evolution will help you see why many of the designs in later chapters look the way they do.

## 1.4.1 The Starting Point: ARPANET and the Bold Idea of "Packet Switching"

In the 1960s, computers were expensive and scarce, and researchers wanted computers at different institutions to be able to share resources. The U.S. Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) funded the construction of ARPANET. In the fall of 1969, the first message was sent over this network between the University of California, Los Angeles and the Stanford Research Institute. Public accounts often mention one detail: the operator meant to type "LOGIN," but only the two letters "LO" got through before the system crashed.

What truly mattered about ARPANET was not how many machines it connected, but its use of **packet switching**: data is cut into small packets, each carrying its own address, forwarded independently across the network, and reassembled on arrival. This differs from the traditional telephone network. When you make a phone call, the system must first reserve a complete circuit between the two ends and releases it only when the call ends. The idea was proposed independently in the 1960s by Paul Baran in the United States and Donald Davies in the United Kingdom, among others. Chapter 6 will cover how it works in detail.

## 1.4.2 The Key Leap: TCP/IP Makes a "Network of Networks" Possible

After ARPANET, other networks such as satellite networks and packet radio networks appeared one after another, but they could not communicate with each other. In 1974, Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published a paper proposing a set of protocols that would let different networks interconnect; this became what we now know as **TCP/IP**. Within it, the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) handles reliable delivery, while the Internet Protocol (IP) handles addressing and forwarding.

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET officially switched over entirely to TCP/IP. Many sources regard this date as the "birthday" of the modern Internet. From that day on, "internet" no longer referred only to one particular network, but to a method for connecting any networks together. The "network of networks" described in Section 1.2 gained its technical foundation at this point.

## 1.4.3 Leaving the Lab: Academic Networks, Commercialization, and the World Wide Web

In the mid-1980s, the U.S. National Science Foundation built NSFNET as a backbone network connecting universities and supercomputing centers. It gradually took over ARPANET's role, and ARPANET was decommissioned in 1990. In the early 1990s, commercial networks began to connect, and the NSFNET backbone was retired in 1995. From then on, the Internet backbone was carried jointly by multiple commercial carriers, laying the groundwork for the "carrier interconnection" landscape discussed in Chapter 4.

At almost the same time, another development changed the relationship between ordinary people and the network. In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee at the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN) proposed the concept of the **World Wide Web**, using web addresses (URLs), the Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP), and the HyperText Markup Language (HTML) to link scattered documents together. In 1993, CERN announced that Web technology would be made freely available to the public. That same year saw the arrival of the Mosaic browser with a graphical interface, and from then on going online was no longer just for experts. Note: the World Wide Web is an application that runs on the Internet, not the Internet itself, a distinction already drawn in Section 1.3.

In 1994, China achieved full-function connectivity to the global Internet, after which the country's access and backbone networks developed rapidly.

## 1.4.4 From the Desktop to the Pocket: Mobile Internet and the Cloud

In the late 2000s, the spread of devices led by touchscreen smartphones, combined with improved data capabilities in cellular networks, turned going online from "sitting in front of a computer" into "anytime, anywhere." Around the same time, **cloud computing** took off: instead of building their own server rooms, companies could rent computing and storage resources on demand in large data centers. To get videos and web pages to users faster, **content delivery networks (CDNs)** also began replicating content to nodes closer to users.

The combination of these two trends changed the "shape" of the Internet. Early networks looked more like peer nodes connected to one another; today, a large share of traffic flows from a small number of large data centers and widely distributed cache nodes out to a vast number of mobile devices. Addresses also became scarce: the IPv4 address space of the original design gradually proved insufficient, driving the deployment of IPv6 (see Chapter 7). Mobile communications, data centers, and cloud computing each have their own volume in this series; here we simply point out their place in the evolution.

| Stage | Approximate Period | Defining Changes | Impact Today |
|---|---|---|---|
| Experimental networks | From 1969 | ARPANET, packet switching | Data is sent in "packets" |
| Internetworking | 1974–1983 | TCP/IP proposed and fully adopted | Different networks can interoperate |
| Academic and commercial | Mid-1980s–1995 | NSFNET, commercial carriers take over | Multi-carrier interconnection |
| Rise of the Web | 1990s | Web pages, browsers | Ordinary people go online |
| Mobile and cloud | From the late 2000s | Smartphones, cloud, CDN | Concentrated traffic, always online |

## 1.4.5 Summary: An Unchanging Skeleton, Changing Clothes

Looking back over this history, devices have gone from mainframes to phones, access from dedicated lines to fiber and 5G, and applications from file transfer to short videos. Yet a few underlying principles have endured: data is sent in packets, networks remain independent yet interconnect through common protocols, and intelligence is kept at the edges of the network as much as possible. It is precisely because the skeleton is stable that the Internet can keep putting on new clothes.
