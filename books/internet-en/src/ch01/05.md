# 1.2 What the Internet Is: A Network of Networks

Open your phone's Settings and look at the Wi-Fi details, and you'll see an address like 192.168.1.5. Turn off Wi-Fi and switch to mobile data, then visit a website that shows "What is my IP address," and you'll see a different string of numbers. It's the same phone, but changing how it connects is like changing its "street address," and web pages still load just fine. This tells you that what you're connected to is not a single cable or a single company. It is a whole made up of many independent networks pieced together. The word "Internet" itself says as much: the prefix inter- means "between," and "Internet" originally meant "interconnection between networks."

## 1.2.1 Not One Network, but Tens of Thousands

Many people imagine the Internet as one giant net spanning the globe, built and managed by a single organization. The reality is quite the opposite. The Internet has no master builder and no central control room. It is composed of a large number of independently operated networks, such as:

- Your home network: a router plus a few phones and computers;
- Campus networks at schools and businesses;
- Telecom carriers' broadband and mobile networks;
- Data center networks run by video platforms and cloud providers;
- International backbone networks run by multinational carriers.

Each network has its own owner, budget, and management rules. Its equipment may come from different vendors, and its internal structure varies. These networks agree to exchange data in a common "language" and connect to one another at many interconnection points. Together they form a "network of networks."

Here's an analogy. A country's road system is made up of national highways, provincial roads, city streets, and roads inside residential compounds, each built by different authorities. But because vehicles follow the same traffic rules and intersections link one road to the next, a car can drive from a residential compound to another province. On the Internet, the "vehicles" are data packets, and the "traffic rules" are a set of open protocols.

## 1.2.2 Three Things That Glue Networks Together

Three things allow independent networks to fit together into a single whole:

| Element | Function | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Common protocols | Define how data is packaged and how addresses are labeled; the core is the Internet Protocol (IP) | A standard envelope format and a standard way of writing addresses |
| Globally unique addresses | Every device that communicates externally (or the exit point of the network it sits on) has an IP address recognizable across the whole Internet | Postal addresses that never repeat |
| Interconnection and routing between networks | Different networks exchange data at connection points and tell each other "which addresses can be reached through me" | Transfer agreements between post offices |

The most important of these is IP. It does just one thing: it splits data into packets, each carrying a source address and a destination address, and makes a best effort to deliver them. It doesn't care whether the layer below is fiber, copper, or radio, or whether the application above is a web page or a video. Because IP asks for so little, almost any physical network can connect to it, and almost any application can run on top of it. This "narrow in the middle, wide at both ends" structure is often called the "hourglass model," and we'll explore it in detail in Chapter 4 when we discuss layered models.

## 1.2.3 How Networks Connect to Each Other: Autonomous Systems

At Internet scale, a collection of networks managed by a single organization and presenting a unified routing policy to the outside world is called an Autonomous System (AS). A large carrier, a university, or a major content company may each operate one or more autonomous systems, each with an AS number assigned by a Regional Internet Registry. According to public statistics, the number of active autonomous systems worldwide is currently in the tens of thousands, approaching a hundred thousand.

Autonomous systems exchange "reachability information" using the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP). A tells B, "These address blocks are with me," and B then decides, based on its own business relationships and policies, whether to pass that on to others. There are roughly two kinds of connections. In one, a network pays another to carry its traffic to the rest of the Internet; this is called transit. In the other, two networks exchange traffic between their respective users as equals, usually without paying each other; this is called peering. Much peering takes place at Internet Exchange Points (IXPs). We'll cover these topics in Chapters 2 and 4. For now, just remember this: the Internet's overall connectivity is woven together, bit by bit, from countless bilateral connections and agreements. Nobody planned it centrally.

## 1.2.4 How Many Networks Does a Single Visit Cross?

Let's return to that web page you opened. A data packet leaves your phone and first passes through your home Wi-Fi and router: that's the first network. The router hands it to your broadband provider's access network and metropolitan network: that's the second. If the website's server sits with a different carrier or a cloud provider, the packet must also cross an interconnection point between carriers to enter the other network. If the server is overseas, the packet will also pass through an international gateway and undersea cables. Run `tracert` in the Windows command prompt, or `traceroute` in the macOS Terminal, followed by a web address, and you'll see the router addresses hop by hop along the way. From these you can roughly identify where the packet "left home," "entered the carrier's network," and "switched to another network." Chapter 7 will teach you how to read these results carefully.

On this journey, no single network knows the full route. Each router simply looks up the destination address in its own table and hands the packet to the "next hop." The end-to-end path is completed as a relay of many local decisions.

## 1.2.5 Summary: No Center, Yet Connected

Putting these points together, we can give the Internet a working definition: **The Internet is a global computer network system formed by many independently managed networks, connected through mutually negotiated interconnection arrangements and following a set of open protocols centered on IP.**

This definition has three key phrases. "Independently managed" means it has no one in overall charge. "Open protocols" means its rules are available for anyone to read, with standards documents published mainly as RFCs (Requests for Comments). "Mutually negotiated" means its connectivity depends on agreements and cooperation between networks. Once you understand the "network of networks," you can see why, when an undersea cable fails, some websites slow down while others are unaffected, and why the same website can run at different speeds when you switch carriers.

But are everyday terms like "going online," "the World Wide Web," "local area network," and "5G network" the same thing as the Internet? Where are the boundaries between them? That is exactly what the next section sets out to clarify.
