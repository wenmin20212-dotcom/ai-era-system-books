# 8.2 How Electrodes Enable Measurement: Potential Difference, Impedance, and Contact Conditions

Put on a watch that supports ECG measurement and lightly touch the designated electrode with a finger of your other hand, and a waveform may appear on the screen. Optical heart rate measurement requires a light source, but this measurement relies primarily on contact between metal and skin. Some watches can also estimate body composition through contact with the body. Both functions use electrodes, but what the electrodes receive, what the instrument applies, and how the results are produced differ.

## 8.2.1 Potential Difference: Measuring the Difference Between Two Locations

To understand electrodes, start with a voltmeter. A voltmeter needs two probes because voltage describes the electric potential difference between two locations. Asking only “how much electricity” there is at one point, without specifying a reference location, does not constitute a complete voltage measurement. A watch’s ECG function likewise requires different contact locations; a single metal surface alone cannot provide a complete recording.

When heart muscle cells are active, the distribution of ions across their cell membranes changes. The coordinated excitation and recovery of many cells produce electrical activity within the body that varies over time. Body tissues contain water and electrolytes and can conduct ionic currents, so small potential differences also appear between different locations on the body’s surface. An ECG records how these differences change over time; it does not directly measure the force of the heart’s contractions.

In a common watch-based measurement, an electrode on the back of the watch contacts one wrist while a finger of the other hand touches the crown or another designated electrode. The device can then observe the voltage between these two locations. The actual number and functions of the electrodes vary by product, and some devices also have auxiliary electrodes. You cannot determine how many ECG leads a device provides simply by counting the visible metal pieces.

## 8.2.2 One Lead Provides Only One Viewing Direction

The heart’s electrical activity occurs in three-dimensional space. A lead formed by two contact locations provides information along only one direction. Think of observing an object’s shadow from a single angle: the shadow does come from the object, but it cannot reveal every side of it. This analogy helps explain the viewing direction; it does not mean that electrodes take pictures of the heart.

In engineering analysis, the heart’s complex electrical activity is sometimes approximated as an equivalent electric dipole whose direction and magnitude change over time. Its projection along the lead direction can then be considered. This model simplifies calculations. It does not mean that the heart actually contains only one pair of charges, nor can every waveform be explained by a single fixed angle.

For the same person, changing the wrist on which the watch is worn, the contact locations, or the device settings may therefore affect the recording’s direction and appearance. Follow the product instructions. A single-lead watch recording does not provide the same range of information as a multilead examination in a medical facility. The absence of a particular notification cannot rule out all heart conditions, and a device’s ability to record a waveform does not mean it can detect a heart attack.

## 8.2.3 Skin and Metal Do Not Form an Ideal Wire

To travel from the body to the measurement circuit, the signal must also pass through the electrode–skin interface. Conduction inside the body is primarily ionic, while conduction inside metal is primarily electronic. These two forms of conduction meet at the contact interface. The stratum corneum, the outermost layer of skin, forms an additional barrier, making this interface far more complex than two wires touching.

This is where the concept of impedance becomes necessary. Resistance primarily describes opposition to current, while impedance also includes capacitive effects and therefore varies with signal frequency. Skin hydration, contact area, pressure, and surface contamination can all change contact impedance. The electrode interface can also develop its own potential, whose magnitude and variation depend on the material, surface condition, and contact environment.

Not all these interface effects originate in the heart. If a finger slides or the back of the watch lifts away from the skin, changing contact conditions may cause baseline drift or brief spikes. Muscle tension also introduces electrical activity of its own. A fluctuation on the screen cannot automatically be interpreted as a heartbeat, much less as a sign of disease.

## 8.2.4 How Circuits Preserve Signals Within Tiny Differences

ECG acquisition circuits typically have high input impedance. The aim is to draw as little current as possible from the measurement sites, minimizing the measurement’s own effect on the signal. An amplifier then amplifies the difference between the two inputs. The circuit can suppress some interference that enters both inputs simultaneously, but its ability to do so is limited.

If the contact impedances on the two sides differ substantially, external interference that was initially similar at both inputs may become a difference that is difficult to eliminate. The device must also handle electrode offsets, slow drift, and high-frequency noise before converting the signal into digital samples. Different products may use different approaches to managing offsets and filtering; there is no single fixed circuit suitable for every watch.

Nor is filtering better simply because it removes more “messiness.” Excessive processing may alter meaningful waveforms, while insufficient processing leaves interference behind. Reliable measurement therefore depends not only on circuitry, but also on contact conditions and quality assessment. Sometimes, prompting the user to measure again is more appropriate than forcing the device to draw a smooth curve.

## 8.2.5 BIA: Applying an Excitation and Observing the Body’s Response

BIA also uses electrodes, but it does not simply receive potential differences generated by the heart. A device with this function applies a weak alternating-current excitation according to its design and measures the corresponding voltage–current relationship to estimate impedance along a particular measurement path through the body. This is not a different algorithm applied to an ECG waveform; it is a different measurement task.

Different body tissues have different water content, electrolyte distributions, and cell membrane structures, so they respond differently to alternating current. The device combines the measured impedance with inputs such as height and population data used to develop its model to estimate body composition. Electrodes therefore do not directly “weigh” body fat. Fluid distribution and the model’s applicability both affect the result. Not every watch supports BIA; consult the specific product instructions for relevant limitations.

You can make an observation without changing your physical conditions: provided that your device supports the function and you meet its requirements for use, clean the contact sites and keep them dry as instructed, sit steadily, relax your arms, and complete a normal measurement. Note whether the device reports poor contact. Do not apply liquids, increase pressure, or connect an external power source to create differences. Record “the prompts observed,” “possible contact-related causes,” and “causes still unknown” separately. This helps make clear that electrodes provide access to the body’s electrical information, but reliable conclusions also require suitable measurement conditions, appropriate processing methods, and clearly defined limits on intended use.
