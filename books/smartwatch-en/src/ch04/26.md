# 5.2 From Skin to Digital Samples: How Measurement Signals Flow

## 5.2.1 Before the Screen Shows a Number, There Is a Measurement Chain

Sitting at your desk, you turn on your watch’s heart rate measurement function. The light source on the back activates, and a reading appears on the screen a little later. That wait involves more than just “calculating heart rate”: the watch must first send light into tissue, receive the returning light, and convert a weak electrical signal into digital samples that can be processed. The displayed result sits at the end of this chain; it cannot directly reveal the state of every preceding step.

This section traces the measurement path of wrist-based photoplethysmography, or PPG. We need to distinguish three things: changing light in tissue, changing electrical signals in circuits, and digital samples in memory. They carry related information, but they are not the same thing. Understanding where information changes form helps explain where errors can enter—and why “collecting data” is not the same as “obtaining a reliable reading.”

## 5.2.2 Light Travels Through Tissue, and Changes Enter the Circuit

Light-emitting diodes on the back of the watch shine light onto the skin. Some of the light entering the tissue is absorbed; some returns to the watch after scattering multiple times and is received by a photodetector. Changes in blood volume with each pulse affect the received light intensity. However, the detector receives more than pulse information: it also receives a background shaped by the skin, other tissues, and the optical arrangement.

PPG therefore does not film blood flowing or count red blood cells one by one. It observes how the intensity of returning light changes over time, then looks for components associated with the pulse. Wavelength, emitter position, detector position, and contact conditions all affect this path, and the specific arrangements differ between watches.

The photodetector converts the received light into an electrical signal. In a commonly used photodiode, for example, changes in illumination can cause changes in photocurrent. At this point, body-related information has moved from variations in light intensity to variations in current, but it is not yet digital. If the strap is loose, changes in the relative position of the watch back and skin can also cause the current to fluctuate. The circuit cannot identify a pulse merely because “something changed.”

## 5.2.3 The Analog Front End Brings Weak Changes into a Suitable Range

The circuitry immediately following the detector is usually called the analog front end. Its job is not to deliver a health conclusion directly, but to condition a weak signal mixed with background components so that it falls within a range the subsequent conversion circuitry can accept. Here, “analog” means that the signal varies continuously within a range—not that it is simulated or unreal.

For photocurrent, a common approach is to use a transimpedance amplifier to convert it into a voltage, followed by any necessary gain adjustment and filtering. Think of this as choosing the right scale for observing small fluctuations: too little amplification makes the changes hard to see; too much can allow a strong background or sudden interference to drive the output beyond its range, causing saturation. A saturated waveform resembles a mountain with its peak clipped flat. Later stages cannot recover lost details out of nothing.

Some designs also take measurements with the light source switched off to estimate the ambient-light background, then process these alongside measurements taken with the source on. This helps reduce the influence of ambient light, but it cannot guarantee that all interference is eliminated. Changes in bright light, unstable contact, and circuit noise may still leave traces. TI’s AFE4404 is one example of a component that combines LED driving and optical signal acquisition, but this does not establish that any particular watch uses that model.

## 5.2.4 Analog-to-Digital Conversion Captures Samples of Continuous Change

The conditioned analog signal enters an analog-to-digital converter, or ADC. At scheduled times, it observes the input and represents the signal’s magnitude as a number. Continuous change thus becomes a sequence of discrete samples: each sample describes information from only one sampling instant or acquisition window, rather than providing a complete copy of the entire changing signal.

To understand this step, we need to distinguish sampling rate from quantization bit depth. Sampling rate describes how many samples are acquired per unit of time; quantization bit depth determines how finely the numerical scale can be divided. One concerns the selection of points in time, while the other concerns the division of amplitude into levels. More levels do not necessarily mean greater measurement accuracy. If motion interference has already overwhelmed the input, adding more bits will not automatically restore the true pulse signal.

Sampling arrangements must also be considered in relation to their purpose. Resting measurements, exercise recording, and different sensor channels may use different strategies, with adjustments made according to the device’s state. Sampling too sparsely can miss changes, and some faster variations may appear as slower, false variations after sampling. Front-end filtering and sampling design therefore need to work together. No fixed frequency or ADC bit depth should be presented as a configuration shared by all smartwatches.

## 5.2.5 Samples Need Timing Information Before Moving On

Once digital samples have been generated, they still need to travel through internal chip pathways or board-level interfaces into buffers and the processor. A buffer acts like a temporary inbox: it can accumulate a batch of samples before passing them to subsequent software together, reducing how often the processor must intervene. Some components use a first-in, first-out queue, or FIFO, so that earlier data is read out first. The precise buffer location and data-transfer method depend on the design.

Timing information is equally important. If a watch uses accelerometer data to assess whether arm movement has affected PPG, it needs to know the times corresponding to samples from both streams. The sampling time is not the same as the time when software reads the data: a batch of samples may have been generated earlier and retrieved from the buffer only later. The system can reconstruct their sequence using timestamps, sample counts, and relationships between clocks, but timing precision cannot be inferred from a clock’s name alone.

The following teaching diagram shows only changes in the form of information, not the specific wiring of any product:

Skin and blood volume changes → Changes in returning light intensity → Photocurrent → Conditioned analog signal → Digital samples and timing information

Viewed along this chain, limitations at one stage carry forward into the next. You can observe the wait for a measurement while wearing the watch normally and sitting quietly, then consult the device instructions for its wearing requirements. Do not assess your physical condition using readings taken with a deliberately loosened watch, or infer the sampling rate from the screen’s refresh interval. The screen may update only periodically even though many samples have already been collected internally. What the measurement chain delivers is data subject to particular conditions and errors. Turning that material into readable results requires further processing.
