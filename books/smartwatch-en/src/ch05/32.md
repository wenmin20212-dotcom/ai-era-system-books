# 6.2 How Much to Measure, and When: Sampling Strategies and Power Trade-offs

Wear a watch to bed and open its app in the morning, and you may find some parts of the heart-rate chart more densely populated than others. Start recording a walk, and the numbers on the screen update more often. Is the watch “slacking off” during everyday use and only working hard during exercise? A more accurate explanation is that it schedules its measurements. Its limited battery must support not only sensors but also the display, communication, and computation, so the system must decide when to collect data, for how long, and which data warrant further processing.

## 6.2.1 First, Distinguish Three Kinds of “Speed”

To understand sampling, imagine a continuously changing bodily signal as a line extending endlessly. A sensor cannot store that entire line exactly as it is; it can only take readings at individual moments. These readings are called samples, and the number taken per unit of time is the sampling rate. For PPG, samples typically come from the photodetection signal chain. They are not separate heart-rate results, but the raw material used to trace changes in light intensity.

Sampling rate, result update frequency, and measurement interval are three different things. During a measurement, a sensor may collect samples at short intervals, while the algorithm waits until it has accumulated a stretch of data before producing a result. The screen may refresh at yet another pace. Background measurements, meanwhile, may start only periodically, collecting data for a short period each time.

Thus, a number updating every second on the screen does not mean the sensor takes only one sample per second. Nor does a point appearing every few minutes on a historical chart mean that no data were collected in between. Charts may also show summary values rather than every record. The interface alone usually cannot reveal the internal sampling rate; you still need to consult documentation for the specific model and mode.

## 6.2.2 More Frequent Sampling Does Not Necessarily Mean Greater Accuracy

Why not simply collect as many samples as possible? Because some changes of interest are fast and others are slow. To identify waveform details, sampling too sparsely may miss important fluctuations or even make fast changes appear slow. This phenomenon is called aliasing. It is like a wheel that sometimes appears to turn backward in a video: the wheel is not actually reversing, but the recorded moments are insufficient to represent its motion correctly.

However, a higher sampling rate is not always better. If motion has already severely contaminated an optical signal, additional samples may merely record the interference in greater detail. The bandwidth of the photodetector and analog circuitry, along with filtering before sampling, also limits what information can ultimately be retained. Sampling settings must be designed together with the measurement objective, hardware capabilities, and algorithms.

It is also important to distinguish “sampling more densely” from “observing for longer.” Taking more samples within a given period helps capture details; extending the observation period may help identify recurring patterns. The two cannot fully substitute for each other. With too short an observation window, patterns may be unstable; a longer window may make results respond more slowly to changes. A slight delay in readings at the start of exercise may relate to this trade-off, but the delay alone cannot reveal the internal algorithm.

## 6.2.3 Power Is Consumed Beyond the Instant of Sampling

An optical measurement usually involves more than simply “reading the sensor.” The system must drive a light source, operate the acquisition circuitry, convert the signal into digital form, and then move, process, and store the data. If results must also be displayed or transmitted in real time, those modules consume power too. Increasing the sampling rate may place greater demands on some of these stages, but power consumption does not always increase proportionally.

Another important concept is duty cycle: the proportion of time a component is actually operating. Brief periods of operation followed by rest consume a different amount of energy over a day than continuous operation, even when the sampling rate during measurement is identical. The duration and intensity of light pulses, as well as the number of active channels, also affect consumption. Two features both labeled “continuous heart rate” may therefore follow different internal operating schedules.

The system can also temporarily store samples in a buffer near the sensor, then pass them to the processor together once enough have accumulated. This is like collecting several letters before making a delivery, reducing the overhead of frequent wake-ups and data transfers. However, batch processing can add delays; tasks requiring prompt responses cannot wait indefinitely. Saving power therefore depends not only on measuring less, but also on organizing the work that follows measurement efficiently.

## 6.2.4 Schedule Measurements According to the Task

During everyday wear, a watch may measure intermittently or adjust its strategy based on activity. In exercise mode, it may extend measurement periods, shorten intervals, and activate more relevant sensors to track changes. The word “may” matters here: different products, features, and software versions do not follow a single set of rules.

```text
Task and mode -> Choose measurement interval, sampling rate and duration
                                      |
Body and movement -> Acquire signal -> Check quality -> Usable result
                                      |      ^
                                      +-- Adjust or remeasure
Leave missing data when reliability remains inadequate.
```

This diagram illustrates a design approach, not the internal program of any particular watch. A system can first identify the task, then check whether the collected data are usable and adjust measurements if necessary. When quality is insufficient, it may withhold a result rather than continually intensify measurement.

For example, when the wearer is still and the signal is relatively stable, a limited stretch of data may be enough to achieve the objective. During walking, changing contact conditions may require more observation. But such adjustments have limits: if the strap is noticeably loose or the sensor window is obstructed, increasing sampling alone may not solve the problem. Low battery levels or unsuitable temperatures may also restrict features. Measurement strategies are always constrained by both bodily conditions and device status.

## 6.2.5 Understand the Trade-offs Through Safe Observation

You can make a simple observation during ordinary daily life: consult the watch’s instructions to check the descriptions of background and exercise heart-rate features, then examine records from sitting quietly and walking normally. Note the mode, how the watch was worn, the activity periods, and how closely spaced the chart’s data points are. There is no need to swing your arms vigorously, and certainly no reason to use dangerous exercise or improper wear to force the device to change its strategy.

If comparing battery consumption, keep settings such as the always-on display, location services, network connections, and notifications as consistent as possible, and record the observation duration. Even then, coarse battery-percentage readings and differences in background tasks can affect the results. A small difference in battery use over a short period cannot be attributed directly to sampling; likewise, a denser chart does not prove a higher internal sampling rate.

The most valuable outcome of this observation is not guessing a hidden parameter, but learning to distinguish visible results from internal mechanisms. A watch needs to collect enough information, but it does not need every module running at full capacity all the time. An appropriate strategy matches sampling density and duration to the task, and acknowledges missing data when evidence is insufficient, rather than using more numbers to create an illusion of certainty.
