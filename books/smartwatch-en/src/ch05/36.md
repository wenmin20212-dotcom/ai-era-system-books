# 6.6 Comparing Usage States: Charting Operating Timelines and Battery Drain

The same watch may lose battery charge at different rates when resting on a desk, worn during a walk, or providing continuous navigation. The difference is not simply “how long the screen was on.” It may also involve positioning, optical measurements, wireless transmission, and background computation. Understanding these changes does not require taking the watch apart or draining its battery. An everyday observation with its conditions recorded can connect the wake-up, sampling, scheduling, and quality assessment processes discussed earlier.

## 6.6.1 Define the Usage States Clearly First

“The battery drained quickly today” is an impression, not yet evidence that lends itself to comparison. Start by giving each usage state a specific name, such as “worn, screen off, no workout recording” or “outdoor walking, workout recording enabled, occasional screen checks.” Add the conditions that could affect the results after each name.

At a minimum, record the device model, operating system version, start and end times, battery percentages, and the status of always-on display, positioning, phone connectivity, and power-saving mode. Weather, how the watch was worn, and whether audio was playing are also worth noting briefly. There is no need to fill a table with every possible parameter. The point is to let another reader understand exactly what you are comparing.

For your first attempt, choose just two states: ordinary quiet wear and a normal recorded walk. Conduct both observations within similar battery charge ranges and in similar environments, keeping their durations equal. Do not start one just after a full charge and the other near automatic shutdown. Nor should you attribute every difference between quiet indoor wear and outdoor navigation in cold weather to the workout feature.

## 6.6.2 Connect Actions and Responses with a Timeline

On the timeline, time runs horizontally, with separate rows for user actions, visible responses, and possible internal activity. First chart what you actually saw, then mark inferences with dashed lines or explanatory text. The following is a teaching illustration, not measured results from a particular watch.

```text
Time ->          Quiet wear     Raise wrist       Start walk recording     Finish and sync
User action      Arm lowered    Raise/touch       Walk normally            Tap finish
Visible response Off/always-on  Interface wakes  Duration/distance update Summary appears
Internal work    Low-power work Wake and render  Acquire, compute, save   Organize and
    transmit
Evidence         Observable     Observable       Partly observable        Partly observable
Internal scheduling details still require documentary verification.
```

The most important aspect of this chart is not how much detail it provides about internal activity, but how it separates “what was seen” from “how it is explained.” The screen lighting up is an observed fact; the processor increasing its operating frequency is a possible explanation. Without reliable documentation, you cannot conclude that the watch switched between two chips, much less estimate its wake-up time from visual impressions.

Data synchronization may also continue for a while after a workout ends. Stopping the workout therefore does not necessarily mean the device immediately returns to its previous state. Including this post-workout processing stage in the chart helps prevent its battery consumption from being mistakenly assigned to the next period of quiet wear.

## 6.6.3 Turn Battery Changes into Comparable Records

Record the battery level at the start and end of each period, along with its actual duration. For example, if the level drops from 72 percent to 69 percent during an observation, the change is three battery percentage points—not “a three-percent reduction in battery capacity.” What you are comparing is the remaining charge percentage displayed by the system, not directly measured electrical energy.

If the two periods differ in length, divide the change in battery percentage points by the number of hours to obtain the average decline per hour. This figure is useful for summarizing that period, but it cannot directly predict all-day battery life. The watch switches between operating states, and its battery display is subject to rounding, estimation, and update delays.

Avoid rushing to rank the results, especially when the displayed level drops by only one percentage point or does not change at all. An unchanged display does not mean no energy was consumed; consumption may simply not yet have crossed a display increment. You can extend the everyday observation moderately or repeat it under the same conditions on different days, rather than repeatedly waking the screen while waiting for the number to change.

## 6.6.4 Change Only One Main Condition at a Time

After completing your initial records, choose one setting for further comparison. For example, if the device supports it and doing so is convenient, compare always-on display enabled versus disabled. Keep other conditions as similar as possible, including the watch face, brightness settings, number of notifications, and number of screen checks. If you disable positioning, lower brightness, and enable power-saving mode all at once, even a reduction in battery drain will tell you little about each change’s contribution.

“Keeping conditions similar” does not mean that home observations must achieve laboratory precision. A walking route may have different obstructions, the phone connection may briefly drop, or an update may run in the background. Noting these events alongside the record is more reliable than explaining every difference afterward with a single definitive story.

The comparison should also include the user experience, not just battery level. Power-saving settings may alter positioning, background measurements, or display behavior; consult the device documentation for specifics. If records become less frequent, do not simply write “battery life improved.” Note that the functional conditions changed as well. Energy savings and the services provided should be assessed together.

## 6.6.5 Keep Conclusions Within What the Evidence Supports

After making several records, summarize the results in three statements: what you observed, which mechanisms might explain it, and what remains unclear. For example: “The average battery decline was faster during periods with walking recording enabled; positioning, sensor data collection, and interface updates may all have contributed; the power consumption of individual modules cannot currently be separated.” Restrained as this conclusion is, it can still guide the next observation.

Battery records also cannot verify the accuracy of health readings. A smoother curve may result from averaging, update timing, or the handling of missing data; it does not prove that motion artifacts have been eliminated. Likewise, the absence of a fall alert does not prove that detection failed, and you must not test it by striking or dropping the device, or by pretending to lose consciousness. For assistance procedures, only read the official instructions and check the normal settings; do not trigger a real alarm.

The entire exercise should take place during comfortable, safe everyday activities. Do not record numbers while crossing a road, use extreme temperatures to create differences, or repeatedly deep-discharge the battery. If the device becomes unusually hot or shows signs of battery damage, stop the observation and follow the manufacturer’s guidance. The resulting timeline and battery-drain record are not a chip power-consumption report, but a map of operation that contains facts, inferences, and acknowledged unknowns.
