# 3.3 How Wrist Movements Become Records: IMUs and Activity Recognition

When you go for a walk, the step count on your watch keeps rising. Yet when you sit down to fold clothes, it may add a few steps too. Conversely, after walking while pushing a shopping cart, you may find that it has counted too few. Your watch cannot see your feet. Instead, it collects motion clues from its vantage point on your wrist, then judges whether those clues resemble walking. Understanding this conversion helps distinguish “what the sensors measured” from “what the algorithm thinks is happening.”

## 3.3.1 Two Types of Sensors, Each Detecting a Different Kind of Change

An inertial measurement unit, or IMU, typically combines inertial sensors such as accelerometers and gyroscopes. The exact components and number of measurement axes depend on the device’s design; “six-axis” should not be assumed to describe every watch. A typical three-axis accelerometer measures along three mutually perpendicular directions, while a three-axis gyroscope provides information about angular velocity around three axes.

An accelerometer helps detect changes such as arm swings, sudden stops, and impacts. One common misconception needs clearing up here: when a watch rests on a table, its accelerometer does not necessarily output zeros, because it also responds to the effect of gravity while supported. As the wrist turns, the components of gravity along the measurement axes change. A change in the reading along one axis therefore does not necessarily mean that the hand is accelerating in that direction.

A gyroscope adds rotational clues, such as how quickly the wrist is turning and in which direction. Combining these two types of information helps distinguish changes in orientation from changes in motion, but still cannot reconstruct the wrist’s trajectory without error. Small errors accumulate through repeated calculations, so a watch cannot rely on inertial measurements alone to track its position accurately indefinitely.

## 3.3.2 Finding a “Walking-Like” Rhythm in a Stream of Numbers

During walking, the body’s rise and fall and the swinging of the arms often follow a repetitive rhythm. At the wrist, these changes produce signals that fluctuate over time. Algorithms generally begin by reducing some of the noise, then look for candidate step events within a segment of the signal, checking their spacing, strength, and continuity rather than simply “counting every peak as a step.”

For example, picking up a cup may produce a pronounced peak, but it usually lacks a sustained walking rhythm. By contrast, signals from continuous walking may retain a similar timing pattern even when individual steps differ in amplitude. Algorithms use these differences to decide whether to count steps. Some devices also wait for further evidence before adding earlier steps to the record. As a result, the number on the screen may remain unchanged briefly, then jump by several steps.

People differ in how they swing their arms, how quickly they walk, and where they wear their watches, and their gait is not always regular. Pushing a cart reduces wrist movement, carrying heavy objects restricts motion, and folding clothes may create a repetitive rhythm. These situations illustrate an important point: wrist motion is only indirect evidence of footsteps. False or missed steps do not necessarily mean that a sensor is faulty.

## 3.3.3 Activity Recognition Asks More Questions Than Step Counting

Beyond determining “how many steps were taken,” a watch may also distinguish walking, running, cycling, and other activities. This requires combining more clues: how quickly the signal repeats, how large its changes are, whether rotation is pronounced, and how long the pattern lasts. Some devices also incorporate positioning-derived speed, changes in heart rate, or a workout mode selected by the user to reduce the ambiguity of any single clue.

The following is an educational illustration of activity recognition, not the internal program of any particular product:

```text
Wrist movement and rotation
            |
Acceleration and angular-velocity time series
            |
Organize signals; extract rhythm and change features
            |
Combine duration and other available evidence
            |
Activity class, step count, or insufficient evidence
```

The two middle stages are the most important: numerical samples do not turn directly into “running.” They must first undergo feature extraction and interpretation. The same wrist movements can have different meanings in different contexts. Bumps during a vehicle ride also cause changes in acceleration, but combining movement rhythms with information such as positioning-derived speed may help the system avoid mistaking them for walking.

Automatic recognition usually requires a period of evidence gathering, so it may not issue a notification from the very first second of an activity. Manually starting a workout mode effectively tells the system in advance to “focus on this type of activity,” but it does not guarantee that every metric will be accurate.

## 3.3.4 On-Screen Exercise Metrics Vary in How Direct Their Evidence Is

Step count and cadence mainly depend on identifying repetitive movement events. Metrics such as stride length and distance may additionally incorporate personal information, positioning results, or calibrated models. Thus, even when two watches report similar step counts, their distance estimates may differ. A displayed decimal does not mean that the corresponding length was measured directly.

Running metrics such as ground contact time and vertical oscillation are further removed from the wrist’s raw signals. They require estimation using specific models, and some also depend on compatible external sensors. Consult the device instructions to check support and the applicable activities and wearing positions. Signals from a single wrist also cannot, by themselves, establish that the forces on the left and right feet or their ground contact patterns are perfectly symmetrical.

Some watches also include a barometer, which provides clues for counting floors and estimating total ascent. It measures ambient air pressure and uses it to estimate changes in elevation; the IMU is not directly “counting stairs.” Weather, indoor ventilation, and pressure changes can all affect the results, so algorithms often need to incorporate motion information when interpreting them. Fine resolution in pressure readings does not mean that every individual stair can be reliably identified.

## 3.3.5 Checking Your Understanding with an Ordinary Walk

Try a simple observation on a flat, safe walking path: note the starting step count, walk a short distance at your usual pace while counting your steps yourself, then stop fully before checking the record. On another occasion, choose a comparable stretch and carry a lightweight item naturally, as you normally would, then compare the results. Do not deliberately stiffen your arm swing to create a difference, and do not stare at the screen while walking.

Record which wrist you wore the watch on, whether the strap fit snugly, whether a workout mode was enabled, and how long you waited after stopping. A single comparison only shows performance under those conditions; it cannot establish that one brand is generally more accurate. If you find a discrepancy, first suggest possible explanations, then check them through safe, repeated observations.

What a watch records is “activity inferred from wrist-based evidence,” not a direct video of the whole body’s movements. Keeping this distinction in mind when viewing step counts, activity categories, and running metrics helps you appreciate their practical value while identifying conditions worth checking when the numbers do not match your expectations.
