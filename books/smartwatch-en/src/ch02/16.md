# 3.4 How Routes Appear on a Map: GNSS and Positioning Assistance

Head out for a walk, tap “Start Workout,” and when you get home, a route appears on the screen. It rounds street corners and crosses the park—or perhaps suddenly veers into a river. The watch has not seen the roads beneath your feet. Instead, it receives satellite signals, estimates a series of positions, and connects them in time order. To understand this line, we first need to distinguish three stages: receiving signals, calculating a position, and plotting that position on a map. Completing one stage does not guarantee that the results of the next will be reliable.

## 3.4.1 Satellites Provide Ranging Clues, Not Maps

GNSS is the collective term for global navigation satellite systems, including GPS and BeiDou. The systems, frequency bands, and operating combinations supported depend on the device. A watch typically only receives signals broadcast by navigation satellites; it does not need to send its own position to them. Navigation signals contain information such as time and satellite orbit data, which the receiver uses to determine where the satellites are and how long their signals took to arrive.

Electromagnetic waves travel so quickly that even a tiny timing error can produce a substantial distance error. The receiver converts travel time into a distance estimate, but because the watch’s internal clock is not perfectly synchronized with the satellite clocks, this distance is called a “pseudorange.” Here, “pseudo” does not mean fabricated. It reminds us that the value also contains clock offsets and other errors.

Think of the distance information from each satellite as a constraint in space. Multiple constraints work together to narrow down the receiver’s possible location. Ordinary three-dimensional positioning also requires solving for the receiver’s clock offset, so usable observations from at least four satellites are generally needed. In practice, devices often use more satellites and continually update their results based on observation quality. More satellites do not necessarily mean greater accuracy; their distribution across the sky matters too.

## 3.4.2 Why You Need to Wait for a Position Fix When Heading Out

After you tap the workout button, the watch needs to search for signals, track them, and obtain enough satellite information. If it has not determined its position for a long time, or has moved a considerable distance, obtaining the first usable position may take longer. Standing under an open sky is generally better than standing beneath an overhang, between tall buildings, or under dense tree cover, but the actual waiting time also depends on the device’s state and its surroundings.

A phone or network can supply assistance data, such as approximate time, a rough location, and satellite orbit information, to help the receiver narrow its search. This assistance does not strengthen satellite signals or guarantee an immediate fix every time. Some watches have no independent GNSS capability and rely mainly on a phone to record routes. Others can determine their position independently but still use a phone to update assistance data.

A route in an activity record therefore does not prove that the watch calculated the positions independently. Check the product documentation and current settings to distinguish between “independent watch positioning,” “using the phone’s location,” and “receiving network assistance.” Even if independent positioning is available, power-saving settings may affect whether a particular workout mode uses it.

## 3.4.3 Why Tall Buildings Can Push a Route Off the Road

Satellite signals are already very weak by the time they reach the ground. Buildings, trees, the human body, and even wrist orientation can affect reception. Reflections create a more difficult problem: a signal from the same satellite may arrive directly, or it may first bounce off a wall or glass surface before reaching the antenna by a longer route. This phenomenon is called multipath. The detour increases travel time, and if the receiver does not adequately account for it, the distance estimate may be wrong.

On city streets, a receiver may be unable to receive a satellite’s direct signal at all, picking up only reflected signals. A route that drifts inside a building does not mean the person actually entered it; the environment has affected the position estimate. Road boundaries on the map, basemap alignment, and display processing may also contribute to the apparent offset. A screenshot alone cannot establish a single cause.

Dual-frequency reception provides additional observations that can help estimate ionospheric delay. The structure of certain signals can also help distinguish some reflected interference. But dual-frequency reception does not guarantee that multipath will be eliminated. Antenna design, algorithms, how the watch is worn, and the extent of obstructions all influence performance. Likewise, support for multiple satellite systems provides access to more satellites, but does not mean accuracy will be the same on every street.

## 3.4.4 Continuous Tracks Also Need Help from Inertial Information

GNSS produces position estimates updated over time, not an inherently continuous line. Software must organize these points into a route while also calculating distance, pace, and other results. When positions fluctuate, simply adding the distances between adjacent points may count drift while stationary as movement. Smoothing can reduce this jitter, but it may also make sharp turns look too rounded.

Some devices combine accelerometer, gyroscope, cadence, or other information to help determine whether the wearer is moving and maintain an estimate during brief losses of satellite signals. This is rather like relying on your previous direction and footsteps to estimate your position when landmarks briefly disappear from view. However, inertial measurements and stride-length models both contain errors, so they cannot replace satellite positioning indefinitely. A long tunnel may result in a straight connecting line, a gap in the track, or a sudden position jump at the exit.

Map matching also deserves scrutiny: an algorithm may match estimated positions to nearby roads, producing a neater route without necessarily preserving the details of the actual walk. A smooth curve on the screen does not, by itself, prove that the underlying positioning was more accurate.

## 3.4.5 Making Sense of a Route on an Everyday Walk

Choose a familiar, safe walking route that passes through both open areas and ordinary neighborhood streets. Before starting, wait for a position fix as instructed by the device, wear it normally, and note whether you are carrying your phone, which workout mode you are using, and whether power saving is enabled. There is no need to cross roads just to make comparisons, and do not stare at the screen while walking.

Afterward, check three things: whether the beginning of the route is missing, whether corners have been cut diagonally, and whether sections with more obstructions show offsets. If you repeat the observation, try to keep the route and settings similar, while recognizing that satellite distribution, foliage, nearby vehicles, and other conditions may change. A single recording shows performance only under those particular conditions; it cannot justify a permanent verdict on the device.

A route offset is an observable fact. Attributing that offset to a reflection from a particular wall is an inference that requires more evidence. A sound understanding does not mean expecting every line to hug the road perfectly. It means knowing which signals, assistance data, and processing steps combine to produce that line—and recognizing what remains uncertain.
