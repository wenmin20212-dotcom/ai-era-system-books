# 3.2 How Electrodes Contact the Body: Uses and Limits of ECG and BIA

## 3.2.1 Similar Metal Contact Surfaces, Different Tasks

Open the ECG app on some watches, and the screen will ask you to touch the crown or a side electrode lightly with a finger from your other hand. Open the body composition feature, and it may ask you to place two fingers on designated locations. Both may look like simply “touching metal,” but the measurements serve different purposes: an ECG records electrical potential changes already present in the body, while bioelectrical impedance measurement applies a controlled, weak alternating electrical signal and observes the body’s response.

These two features are abbreviated ECG and BIA, respectively, and not every watch has them. A metal bezel is not necessarily an electrode; it may simply be a structural or decorative component. Check the manual for the specific model rather than judging by appearance alone. Even with similar hardware, available features may differ by region, software version, intended user population, and authorization requirements.

## 3.2.2 ECG Records the Potential Difference Between Two Skin Locations

Heart muscle cells generate changes in electrical potential when active. The combined activity of many cells creates an electric field that varies over time. Because body tissues conduct electricity, small potential differences associated with the heart’s electrical activity can be measured between different locations on the body’s surface. An electrocardiogram records how these differences change over time; it does not directly observe the heart contracting.

In a common watch-based measurement, the electrode on the back of the watch contacts the wrist wearing it, while a finger from the other hand touches the crown or a side electrode, connecting the measurement circuit to two locations on the body. A high-input-impedance acquisition circuit minimizes disturbance to the original signal, which is then amplified, filtered, and digitized to produce the trace on the screen. Unlike BIA, it does not need to actively apply an alternating current to measure tissue impedance.

“Single-lead” can be understood as observing the heart’s electrical activity from one particular direction. A common contact arrangement involving both arms provides a view approximating standard Lead I, although the actual polarity depends on the wearing configuration. A hospital’s 12-lead ECG provides information from multiple directions; a single watch trace cannot replace those views. Nor does the presence of spikes in the trace mean that a disease assessment has been completed.

PPG and ECG are not simply two ways of plotting the same signal. The former observes changes in light intensity associated with changes in blood volume; the latter records electrical potential differences. The heart’s electrical activity is followed by mechanical contraction and pulse propagation, so corresponding features in the two waveforms do not inherently occur at the same time, and their interpretations cannot be freely interchanged.

## 3.2.3 Stable Contact Determines Whether the Trace Is Usable

The electrode–skin interface is not an ideal metal connection. Skin surface condition, contact area, and pressure all affect contact impedance—the opposition an electrical signal encounters as it passes through the contact interface. Excessively dry skin, loose contact, or body movement can all degrade a recording; sweating does not guarantee a better measurement either.

Pressing harder during measurement may not help, because tension in the fingers and forearm produces muscle electrical activity that can mix with the ECG signal. Holding the wrist unsupported, talking, or repeatedly adjusting posture can also make the trace fluctuate. Interference from nearby electrical equipment likewise needs to be handled by the acquisition circuitry and algorithms, but filtering cannot turn every poor signal into a reliable recording.

If the device supports the feature and you meet the conditions of use stated in its manual, sit quietly with your forearms naturally supported, touch the electrodes lightly as instructed on the screen, and observe how the app indicates contact quality. Do not repeatedly press harder in pursuit of a “good-looking waveform,” and do not apply conductive fluid or connect external wires on your own. If a result is “unclassified,” first save it along with the conditions at the time, rather than labeling it normal or abnormal yourself.

## 3.2.4 BIA Measures Impedance, Then Uses a Model to Estimate Body Composition

BIA stands for bioelectrical impedance analysis. The device applies a controlled, weak alternating current through a specified electrode arrangement and measures the resulting voltage response to determine impedance. Impedance includes not only opposition to current flow but may also involve a phase difference between changes in voltage and current. Measurement frequencies and electrode arrangements vary by product.

The human body is not a length of wire made from a uniform material. Tissues containing more water and electrolytes generally conduct electricity more readily, while cell membranes also affect how alternating electrical signals pass through them. The device uses these differences, together with inputs such as height and weight and statistical models, to estimate body fat percentage, body water, or other aspects of body composition. “Body fat percentage” is therefore not a raw quantity measured directly by the electrodes, much less a separate weighing of the body’s fat.

This process can be represented as “contact and electrical response—impedance—model—body composition estimate.” A change at an earlier stage can change the numbers produced later. Drinking, eating, recent exercise, skin temperature, and posture can all affect results; incorrect user-entered information can also skew the estimate.

This explains why a short-term change in a body fat reading does not necessarily mean that fat has actually been gained or lost. When comparing trends, follow the product instructions to keep measurement conditions as consistent as possible, and note any substantial differences. Different devices may use different current paths and models, so their readings should not be assumed to form a directly comparable series.

## 3.2.5 Understand the Results—and Their Limits

An ECG app may provide heart rate, recording classifications, or alerts for specific heart rhythm events. Its precise capabilities depend on the functions that particular product is authorized to provide. The scope of use stated in official ECG instructions applies only to the corresponding device and cannot be generalized to all watches. A single recording without an alert cannot rule out all heart conditions, and a watch must not be presented as a tool for detecting heart attacks.

Likewise, a single BIA reading cannot diagnose nutritional status or determine treatment. People with implanted electronic medical devices, and others for whom the feature may be unsuitable, should first check the contraindications and warnings and consult a professional if necessary, rather than trying it on their own. Nor should aggressive diet or exercise plans be based solely on a watch’s estimates.

When examining these two types of features, the most useful things to record are not isolated numbers but the device model, contact method, measurement conditions, and type of result. ECG provides a record of electrical activity from one direction; BIA provides an estimate generated jointly by electrical measurements and a model. Only by separating “what was measured” from “what was inferred from it” can we understand how much evidence a piece of metal touching the skin actually provides.
