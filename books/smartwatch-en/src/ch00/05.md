# 1.5 From Readings to Evidence: What Health Alerts Can Tell Us

## 1.5.1 The Numbers on the Screen Do Not Tell the Whole Story

After dinner, you are sitting on the sofa when your watch suddenly vibrates, alerting you that a heart rate reading differs from your usual pattern. You look down at the screen, then at your wrist: has something changed in your body, or is the strap loose? Does the alert mean you have a medical condition? If there is no alert, can you rest easy? To answer these questions, you first need to consider how the result on the screen was produced, rather than focusing on a single number.

A smartwatch touches only a small part of your body and observes certain signals that its sensors can capture. It cannot directly see your entire circulatory system or account for every aspect of your life. Its value lies in providing clues for everyday observation, building a record, and issuing alerts under specific conditions. Understanding this information requires recognizing the gaps between readings, estimates, notifications, and medical diagnoses.

## 1.5.2 From Signal to Alert: Several Stages of Conversion

Consider optical heart rate measurement. A light source on the back of the watch shines onto the skin, and a detector receives the returning light, which contains components related to changes in local blood volume. The system does not directly “count heartbeats.” Instead, it identifies a pulse rhythm in changes in light intensity and converts that rhythm into beats per minute. Wrist movement, the tightness of contact, and local blood perfusion can all affect the received signal. The displayed result therefore reflects both the body and the measurement conditions.

A “reading” usually presents a measurement result, but that reading may itself have undergone algorithmic processing. An “estimate” emphasizes inferring a target quantity or state from limited signals—for example, estimating sleep states from movement and physiological signals. A “notification” means that a result has met certain rules set by the device, prompting the system to alert the user. These are not mutually exclusive categories: the same number may be both a displayed reading and the result of a model-based estimate.

Medical diagnosis also requires consideration of symptoms, medical history, examinations, and professional judgment. Even if a particular watch feature has received authorization for a specific medical use in a particular region, it must be understood within its stated scope. Authorization for one feature does not give the entire watch comprehensive diagnostic capabilities. A single-lead ECG records the electrical potential difference across the body's surface along a particular direction. It is not equivalent to the multilead examinations commonly used in hospitals, nor does it justify claiming that a watch can detect a heart attack.

## 1.5.3 The Weight of a Record Depends on the Chain of Evidence

To assess whether a record deserves attention, start with four questions: What was measured? What were the conditions? Is the record complete? What conclusions can the result support? A brief rise in heart rate after a walk, for example, has a different context from repeated unusual readings while resting quietly. The point is not to encourage readers to diagnose themselves, but to show that numbers lose meaning when separated from the context of activity and time.

Trends over time generally provide more context than isolated readings, but having “lots of records” does not automatically mean they are accurate. If the strap is consistently too loose, the same interference may recur. If the system produces no result when signal quality is poor, gaps in the chart cannot be taken as evidence that the body remained stable throughout. Missing data means no usable information was obtained; it must not be arbitrarily filled in as normal.

When comparing records, also check whether the device, software version, and calculation methods are consistent. A sudden change in recorded sleep duration after switching watches may reflect a change in your sleep routine, or it may result from differences in how the algorithms define “falling asleep.” A chart alone usually cannot distinguish between these causes. A reliable approach is to separate observed facts from possible explanations and leave unresolved questions open.

## 1.5.4 An Alert Is Not a Diagnosis; No Alert Does Not Rule a Condition Out

An alert may reflect a real change, or it may be affected by interference. No alert may mean that the relevant condition did not occur, but it may also mean that the watch was not being worn, the feature was not enabled, no measurement was being taken at the time, or the signal did not meet the requirements for analysis. Some features apply only to particular age groups, conditions of use, or types of events. “Not detected” must therefore not be rewritten as “definitely absent.”

Two capabilities are easily confused here: minimizing missed cases of the target condition, and minimizing the misidentification of other conditions as the target. In practice, systems often need to balance the two. More sensitive notification rules may generate more unnecessary alerts; more conservative rules may miss some cases. Performance also depends on the population used for validation and the conditions of use. One or two personal experiences are not enough to judge overall reliability.

Features such as blood oxygen measurement require particular attention to their intended use. A consumer health feature on a watch is not automatically equivalent to a medical pulse oximeter, and measurements may be affected by movement, contact, and physiological conditions. The limitations in the device instructions are not fine print to skip; they are part of understanding the results. If you experience significant discomfort or emergency symptoms, seek medical help promptly. Do not wait for the watch to sound an alarm, and do not delay care because a single reading appears normal.

## 1.5.5 Review a Record Without Adding Risk

Choose an existing, ordinary daily record and note the device model, feature name, recording time, and whether you were walking, resting, or sleeping. Then check whether the app indicates signal quality, gaps in the data, or limitations on use. There is no need to deliberately create an abnormal condition. Do not “test” the watch by holding your breath, exercising intensely, or changing your medication.

Organize your notes into three statements: “What I observed,” “What might explain it,” and “What I still do not know.” For example, the observed fact is that there are no heart rate data points during a particular period; one possible explanation is poor contact with the skin; the unknown is whether the device initiated a measurement at that time. Do not present the second statement as a confirmed cause.

When consulting a healthcare professional, bringing the original records and the circumstances surrounding them is usually more helpful than simply saying, “My watch says something is wrong.” Health alerts are best used to connect everyday observations with further verification, not to replace diagnosis. Learning to retain context and distinguish stronger conclusions from weaker ones is the first step from readings to evidence.
