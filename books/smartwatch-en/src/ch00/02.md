# 1.2 Visible Surfaces: The Screen, Crown, Strap, and Rear Window

Take the watch off your wrist and place it on a dry, soft surface. Do not start any measurements yet. On the front is a screen; along the sides, there may be a crown, buttons, and small openings. Turn it over, and you may see windows, metal areas, or charging contacts. The strap may seem like a mere accessory, but it determines how the watch rests against your skin. These external features are not simply a collection of decorative elements. They form the boundary where the internal systems interact with people, air, water, and light. To understand them, start with a simple question: what needs to pass through here, and what needs to be kept out?

## 1.2.1 The Screen: Displaying Information and Receiving Input

When the screen is dark, what we usually see first is the transparent cover over the display assembly, rather than exposed light-emitting components. The cover provides protection, the display assembly beneath it forms images, and the touch-sensing structure detects finger input. Visually, they appear to be a single surface, but they perform different tasks. The ability to display information is therefore not the same as the ability to detect touch, and a defect in part of the display and a loss of touch response do not necessarily originate in the same component.

The most immediate difference between a round and a square screen is how information is arranged. A round screen lends itself to markings around the edge, while a square screen makes it easier to arrange text and lists. Shape alone, however, does not tell you how sharp the display is or how much power it consumes. Readability also depends on ambient light, brightness adjustment, font size, surface reflections, and viewing angle. The same watch may be easy to read indoors but require higher brightness or a different viewing angle to make out small text in sunlight.

Look at the time display under ordinary indoor lighting, then turn your wrist slightly to see how the reflections move. This can help distinguish between a screen that is not displaying anything and a display obscured by glare. There is no need to leave the brightness at its maximum for an extended comparison, and you should not scratch the cover with a sharp object to test its hardness.

## 1.2.2 The Crown and Buttons: Clear Points of Interaction for Your Fingers

The crown is usually a knob that protrudes from the side of the case. Some crowns can be rotated and pressed; others support additional forms of sensing. A crown lets you scroll through lists, adjust values, or return to a particular screen without always having to swipe on a small display. However, not every component that looks like a crown can rotate, and you should not assume that the mechanical-watch practice of pulling out the crown to set the time also applies to a smartwatch.

Buttons have the advantage of being relatively easy to locate by touch and often provide clear feedback when pressed. During exercise, when your fingers may be less steady, physical controls can be more convenient than tapping small icons. Gloves, obstructing cuffs, and the orientation in which the watch is worn can still affect their use. A short press, repeated presses, and a long press may trigger different functions, potentially including an emergency call. Before exploring them, consult the operating instructions for your specific model.

From the outside, you can establish a button’s location and how it feels to operate, but you cannot determine what kind of switch or sealing structure it uses internally. A button must move while maintaining appropriate separation from the external environment. Understanding exactly how this is achieved requires structural documentation.

## 1.2.3 The Strap: A Functional Connection to the Body

Changing the strap on the same watch can noticeably alter the wearing experience. The material’s flexibility and breathability, the fastening method, and the spacing of adjustment positions all influence how pressure is distributed. Comfort also depends on more than the watch’s total weight: a wider strap may spread pressure, a thicker watch body may be more easily pushed by a cuff, and the clasp’s position may cause it to press against your wrist while you work at a desk.

The strap also helps establish the conditions needed for measurement. Optical measurements generally require suitable contact between the back of the watch and the skin. If the strap is too loose, the watch may slide as you move, allowing ambient light to enter or causing contact conditions to change repeatedly. If it is too tight, it may cause pressure and discomfort; tightening it should not be treated as a universal way to improve accuracy. Manufacturers’ wearing instructions often distinguish between everyday wear and measurements during exercise. Follow the guidance for your particular product regarding placement and tightness.

While wearing the watch normally, gently turn your wrist and check whether the watch frequently slips or the strap’s edges press persistently into your skin. If you feel discomfort, adjust or remove it rather than enduring the discomfort to finish recording. After replacing a strap, also check that its connections are securely locked. A faint click alone is not proof that installation is complete.

## 1.2.4 The Rear Window: Separate Paths for Light and Electricity

On the back of the watch, dark or transparent areas are often associated with optical measurement. Some devices emit visible green or red light while operating, but the colors and number of bright points you can see do not directly reveal their full measurement capabilities. Some light may be invisible, and light-emitting components do not perform the same task as light-receiving components. The window’s shape may also serve several purposes, including protection, light shielding, and structural assembly.

On products with the relevant functions, metal areas may act as electrodes that contact the skin. Some measurements also require the other hand to touch the crown or another electrode to establish the necessary measurement path. However, a metallic appearance does not make something an electrode, nor is every circular feature a sensor. Charging contacts, decorative elements, and sensing components should be identified with reference to the manual, not by color alone.

Sweat, dirt, hair, and the quality of contact between the watch back and the skin can all affect the user experience or measurements. Follow the manufacturer’s cleaning instructions. Do not polish the window with abrasive materials or pour cleaning agents into gaps. There is no need to bring the back of the watch close to your eyes to observe its light sources, and you should not test component responses by improvising covers or removing the back.

## 1.2.5 Small Openings and Seams: Recognizing Boundaries and Leaving Room for Uncertainty

Small openings on the sides may lead to microphones, speakers, or channels associated with air-pressure sensing, or they may serve other structural purposes. Sound and pressure changes need a connection to the outside world, while liquid water and dust should be kept out. The presence of openings is therefore not inconsistent with a degree of water resistance. Membranes and winding channels may lie behind an external opening, but observation alone cannot establish their precise structure.

The same applies to seams. Fine lines between the screen and case, or between the back cover and midframe, indicate boundaries between materials or components. They do not prove that these parts are intended to be opened by the user. Do not probe openings with a needle or pry at seams. Doing so is unlikely to reveal the internal structure and may damage protective components.

After your inspection, you can sketch the front, sides, and back, labeling the screen, buttons, strap connections, and windows. Mark features verified against the manual as “Confirmed” and those inferred from appearance as “To be checked.” The value of this exterior map lies not in naming every small opening, but in separating direct observation from judgments about function: the surface offers clues, while the connection between structure and purpose still requires evidence.
