# 1.6 Create a Record for Your Watch: Draw Your First System Map

## 1.6.1 Start with a Real Watch

Place your watch on a table, with the phone it normally pairs with beside it. Before opening any fitness or health apps, ask three questions: Which parts can you observe directly? Which functions require the phone? Which results on the screen are estimates derived from measurements, and which are simply information received from elsewhere? Creating a record for your watch means turning these questions into notes you can verify step by step.

This record does not need to be complete on the first attempt, nor should it be a copy of the product manual. Its purpose is to help you distinguish between “what I observed,” “what the documentation says,” and “what I do not yet know.” Even two watches that look similar may differ in their internal components, software capabilities, and service requirements. Build the record around the specific device in your hands, rather than filling it out according to what “a smartwatch should generally have.”

All you need is paper and a pen. There is no need to take the watch apart, install unfamiliar software, or export health data. If you do not currently have a watch, you can choose a product with a publicly available manual and clearly mark anything you cannot observe yourself as “based on documentation.”

## 1.6.2 Record Its Identity, Then Describe Its Appearance

On the first page, write the device name, model, operating system version, and date of the record. Whenever possible, confirm the model through the settings page or official documentation, rather than identifying it solely by the appearance of the watch face. Health features, payment services, and communication capabilities may also depend on the sales region, account settings, and permissions or authorizations. Record these conditions alongside the relevant functions. There is no need to copy serial numbers or account names into your learning record, and they certainly should not appear in public photos.

Next, draw simple front, side, and back views. Label the screen, crown or buttons, strap attachment points, rear windows, and visible metal contact areas. If you notice a small hole, label it “side opening” first, then consult the manual to confirm its purpose. Do not assume that a tiny hole must be a microphone or a barometric pressure port. Do not probe it with a needle, blow into it, or remove protective structures.

Your description should also include how the watch feels during normal wear: whether the strap is easy to adjust, whether the watch slips when you raise your wrist, and whether the buttons are easy to reach. These are observations made by a particular wearer under particular conditions, not universal conclusions. If you weigh the watch, note whether the strap is included and the scale’s display resolution. If you do not have a scale, leave the field blank rather than guessing a number from how heavy it feels.

## 1.6.3 Place Functions Within the System

On the second page, choose three everyday functions, such as checking the time, receiving notifications, and recording walking activity. Rather than simply writing “supported” or “not supported,” ask what each function depends on. Showing the time requires a display and internal timekeeping; notifications involve connectivity and permissions; recording walking activity requires sensing movement, processing data, and storing results. This turns function names into relationships within a system.

Use the diagram below as a reference for your first system map. Boxes represent parts that perform different tasks, while arrows indicate the direction of information or effects. This is not an internal circuit diagram, nor does it imply that all devices use the same structure.

```text
Body and surroundings -- motion, light and electrical signals --> Sensing
Sensing -> Computing and storage -> Screen, sound and vibration
                    <-> Phone <-> Cloud services
Battery and power management --> Energy for the parts inside the watch
Wearer -- touch, buttons and other actions --> Watch
```

The phone and cloud services are placed outside the watch to remind us that content appearing on your wrist is not necessarily generated entirely there. Two-way arrows indicate only that information may be exchanged. They do not imply a continuous internet connection or that every function uploads data. When drawing your own map, you can remove unused sound output and use a dashed line for any cloud connection you have not yet confirmed.

Also note that the “sensing components” in the system map are not the same as all the windows on the back of the watch. Internal inertial sensors can detect wrist movement, while some health measurements require suitable contact between the body and rear windows or electrodes. At this stage, you do not need to identify every chip. You only need to establish the basic connections between where inputs come from and where results appear.

## 1.6.4 Label Each Judgment with Its Evidence

To avoid producing a tidy diagram with unreliable conclusions, use three labels: “observed,” “supported by documentation,” and “to be confirmed.” For example, seeing a menu appear after pressing the crown counts as observed. An official specification stating that a particular model has standalone satellite positioning is supported by documentation. But concluding from a route map alone that the watch’s own positioning system must have been used still requires confirmation, because the phone may also have been involved.

Ideally, attach conditions and sources to each important entry. You might write: “The watch received a message while connected to the phone with notification permissions enabled.” Do not shorten this to “The watch can always receive messages.” When citing official documentation, record the page title, applicable model, and date accessed, so that an old record is not mistaken for a current capability after a software update.

Wording matters especially on health pages. “A heart rate value appeared” is an observation. “Optical signals are used to estimate pulse information” explains a principle. “Therefore, the body must be healthy” is not a conclusion supported by either of those statements. Nor does your record need to contain complete health graphs. Recording the page name, feature description, and measurement conditions is usually enough for this exercise.

## 1.6.5 Revise Your System Map Through Safe Observation

Finally, sit securely indoors and choose low-risk actions, such as checking the time, browsing menus, or receiving ordinary notifications, to check the relationships on your map one by one. Change only one condition at a time. For example, after confirming that you do not need to rely on the watch for communication or help, temporarily disconnect it from the phone. Observe which pages still open and which content stops updating, then restore the connection. Do not perform tests while driving, crossing a road, or exercising.

Being able to see old messages after disconnecting proves only that the device can still display stored content. It does not establish that it is still receiving new messages. Likewise, observing no change does not mean that the relevant component is inactive. Recording these limitations is more valuable than rushing to a conclusion.

When you finish, summarize in a few sentences what this watch can do independently, what requires outside support, and what remains unsupported by evidence. Your first system map need not be complicated, but it should be open to revision. As you learn more about sensing, power, communication, and algorithms, you will add arrows and conditions, gradually turning this record of appearances into an evidence-based explanation of the system.

> Chapter 1 Relationship Quick Reference

| What to Observe | Role in the System | Limits of Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sensing | Turns changes in light, movement, or user actions into inputs | Input signals and their final interpretation are not the same thing |
| Computing | Processes signals and coordinates device tasks | A single reading cannot reveal every internal process |
| Interaction and communication | Presents results, receives user actions, and exchanges information | Where a result is displayed is not necessarily the only place it was generated |
| Power and wear | Supports operation and maintains contact conditions | Battery life, comfort, and stability must be balanced together |
