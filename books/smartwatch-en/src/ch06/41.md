# 7.5 Identifying Connectivity and Power Components: Antennas, Batteries, Coils, and Power Devices

Lift a watch off its charging dock and turn it over. Some watches have visible metal contacts; others have only an uninterrupted glass or ceramic surface. Look at publicly available teardown photos, and you may find a flat battery, a ring-shaped coil, metal strips against the frame, and densely packed small components. What does each one do? Identifying these parts takes more than a visual resemblance: shape, position, connections, and published information must all be considered together.

## 7.5.1 An Antenna Is Not Necessarily a Separate Wire

In teardown photos, an antenna may appear as a curved metal pattern on a flexible film or a conductive trace on a plastic support. It may also use part of the watch frame as a radiating structure. Some designs use spring contacts to connect the circuit board’s radio-frequency path to components associated with the case. Not seeing an antenna cable therefore does not mean there is no antenna.

Start by looking for nonmetallic areas, segmented sections of the frame, and conductive structures near the edges. Then check whether contacts or traces connect them to the main board. These are only clues, however: metal spring contacts may also serve grounding, shielding, or sensing functions, while gaps in the frame may simply be needed for assembly. Without clear connections or reliable documentation, avoid labeling a structure as a Bluetooth, cellular, or GNSS antenna.

Tiny capacitors, inductors, and other components are often located near an antenna to help match the radio-frequency path to the antenna. Together, these are generally called a matching network. Their appearance is unremarkable, and a photograph cannot reveal their operating frequency bands. A watch may also share parts of a structure among several wireless functions or use multiple antennas for one function. Counting metal strips will not tell you how many wireless functions it supports.

During wear, the body, watchband, and surrounding objects all affect the antenna’s operating environment. A structure located along the frame’s edge does not necessarily receive signals better in every position. Component identification addresses “what role might this play,” not how signal performance ranks based on location alone.

## 7.5.2 Identify the Battery First, Then Read Its Specifications in Context

The battery is usually one of the larger internal components. It may have a pouch or metal enclosure, with its shape adapted to the available space. A thin silver block in a photo is not necessarily a battery; it could be a shield. More reliable clues include a battery label, terminals, connectors, and its connection to the main board’s power input.

The label may list a model number, nominal voltage, capacity, and energy. Milliamp-hours express charge capacity, while watt-hours express energy. Comparing batteries with different voltages using milliamp-hours alone can be misleading. As a rough estimate, multiplying nominal voltage by capacity in amp-hours gives energy in watt-hours. This does not equal all the energy the complete device can ultimately use, much less translate directly into days of battery life.

It is also important to distinguish nominal voltage from the charging voltage limit. The former describes the battery’s typical operating voltage level; the latter defines a boundary for charging control. They are not interchangeable. Capacity and cycle-life figures in datasheets usually come with specified temperatures, charge and discharge rates, and cutoff conditions. Without those conditions, the numbers lose their basis for comparison.

Battery connections may include temperature sensing or other interfaces in addition to positive and negative terminals, but the number of wires alone cannot establish their purpose. Readers should identify components through published photos and documentation, without opening battery packaging, probing unknown terminals, or attempting to charge a bare battery. Stop using any device that is swollen, leaking, or unusually hot, and follow the manufacturer’s instructions for handling it.

## 7.5.3 Similar-Looking Coils May Perform Different Tasks

Some watches have a large, flat coil near the back, which may be a wireless charging receiver coil. The charging dock produces a changing magnetic field, and the receiver coil converts the coupled energy into an electrical signal. Downstream circuitry then rectifies and regulates it for power and charging. Magnetic material may also sit behind the coil to guide magnetic flux and reduce its effects on surrounding metal.

Seeing a coil does not, however, prove that it is used for charging. Near-field communication antennas can also use loop-shaped conductors, while speakers and vibration actuators may contain windings. Identification should take account of location, size, connected circuitry, and official descriptions of the device’s functions. For example, a large planar coil close to the back cover is a candidate for a charging receiver structure, but further evidence is still needed.

Magnets on a charging dock mainly help align the watch and hold it in place. The magnets themselves do not transfer electrical energy into the battery. Magnetic attachment does not establish protocol compatibility or guarantee safe charging. Some devices receive power through contacts, so magnetic charging and wireless charging should not be treated as equivalent.

## 7.5.4 Identify Power Devices by Their Connections

The battery does not supply the same voltage directly to every module. Processors, displays, and sensors have different power requirements, so the system needs circuits for charging management, voltage regulation, switching, and protection. These functions may be integrated into a power management chip or distributed across several components. Do not assume that every watch has a single, separate “main power chip.”

A small, relatively thick square component in a photo may be an inductor, often found near switching voltage-conversion circuits. Nearby small capacitors store small amounts of charge and reduce voltage fluctuations. Yet similar-looking components may perform different tasks. Some inductors belong to radio-frequency circuits, while some power functions require no external inductor. A nearby inductor is therefore only a supporting clue.

If package markings are clear, first save the original photo and transcribe the markings exactly. Then consult the manufacturer’s package-marking guidance and datasheets. A short code may correspond to several models or include batch information. A model identification becomes more credible only when the marking, package dimensions, pin arrangement, and surrounding connections agree. A datasheet describes what a component can do; by itself, it does not prove that the watch uses that component.

## 7.5.5 Label Identification Results by Evidence Level

When examining a publicly available teardown photo, you can record findings using three labels: “confirmed” for components supported by clear labels or reliable documentation; “candidate” for components whose shape and location fit but whose connections remain unclear; and “unknown” for parts that cannot yet be identified. There is no need to name every black square just to make the image look complete.

For example, a clearly labeled battery can be confirmed first. A ring-shaped conductor near the back cover can provisionally be listed as a charging-coil candidate, while a small package at the edge of the main board can remain unknown. Cross-check these findings against other photos or documentation for the same model and version, taking care not to confuse structures from different sizes or connectivity variants.

The point of this method is not to guess the supplier, but to build a traceable assessment: what you observed, what evidence supports it, and what remains uncertain. Connectivity and power components are often tucked along case edges or housed in tiny packages. Learning to leave things unknown is a genuine step toward reliable identification.
