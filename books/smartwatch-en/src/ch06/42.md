# 7.6 Identifying Components Without Disassembly: Cross-Checking Photos, Markings, and Public Sources

Take off your watch, place it on a soft, dry cloth, and use your phone to photograph the front, back, and sides. The photos reveal windows, openings, and tiny inscriptions, but not the processor, battery wiring, or sensor circuitry. How can you determine what is inside? The reliable approach is not to guess a model from its appearance, but to connect photos of the actual device, device markings, and public sources so that every judgment has a traceable basis.

## 7.6.1 First Establish Which Watch You Are Studying

The first step in identification is not finding a chip, but confirming the device. Similar cases may belong to different generations, and products within the same generation may come in different sizes, connectivity variants, or regional versions. Their internal layouts and features are not necessarily identical. If you identify the device incorrectly, even the clearest teardown photos may be evidence about a different watch.

First, check the product name, model number, and software version on the “About” page in the watch settings or companion app. Then cross-check them against the inscriptions on the back, packaging labels, or purchase records. Note where each piece of information came from rather than recording only a marketing name. If the page shows a device model number while the case bears only a series name, retain both separately; do not assume they are equivalent identifiers.

Prepare a brief identification record: what the device is, when you consulted the sources, and which component you currently want to identify. Before sharing photos publicly, obscure serial numbers, account information, pairing QR codes, and other unique device identifiers. Studying a system’s components generally does not require this information, and identifying a chip does not require exposing the identity of the entire device.

## 7.6.2 Turn Photos into Evidence That Can Be Cross-Checked

Preserving orientation in photos is easily overlooked. You can establish a reference based on the crown’s position when the screen faces up and label the upper and lower strap attachment points. Do not change orientation without noting it when photographing the back; otherwise, the “left-hand window” in two images may refer to different locations. Keep an overall photo and add close-ups. This makes features easier to locate than retaining only an enlarged image of a window.

Under ordinary indoor lighting, changing the camera angle helps distinguish reflections and smudges from actual structures. Do not scrape windows, insert tools into openings, or hold a bright light directly against the watch. A dark-looking area does not prove that no components are present; tinted windows, light-blocking structures, and camera exposure can all affect its appearance.

Photos are best suited to answering questions about location, shape, and neighboring features. They generally cannot establish how many light sources sit beneath a window or which manufacturer made a detector. Even seeing green light establishes only that visible green light was being emitted at that moment. It does not reveal the full blood oxygen sensing configuration, much less identify the analog front end.

## 7.6.3 Let Each Source Answer the Questions It Is Suited to Answering

More public information is not necessarily better; what matters is what it can establish. Official device documentation is useful for checking which features a particular version claims to offer and under what conditions they can be used. Official repair documentation, if publicly available, can help clarify component names and locations. Teardown photos with clear provenance can show internal physical features, but you must verify that they depict the correct model.

Chip manufacturers’ product pages and datasheets serve to explain a candidate component that has already been proposed. For example, TI’s AFE4404 product documentation can help explain the role of an optical analog front end, but it cannot prove that your watch uses that chip. Two sources mentioning “heart rate detection” indicate similar functionality, not identical component models.

When you encounter reposted images, try to trace them to their original source and check the captions, model information, and what the images actually show. If three articles all cite the same photo, there is still only one image source. Likewise, an unsourced supplier list cannot establish who manufactured your watch’s battery, display, or chip packaging.

## 7.6.4 Use Markings to Propose Candidates, Then Check for Contradictions

If a public teardown image clearly shows markings on the top of a component, transcribe them exactly, retaining line breaks and logos rather than “correcting” them first. Characters on small packages may be abbreviations, lot codes, or traceability codes, not complete ordering part numbers. Mark unclear characters as illegible rather than filling them in based on expectations.

Next, compare the candidate component’s package shape, dimensions, pin configuration, and functional description. Assessing dimensions requires a scale reference; without one, comparisons are limited. A component’s proximity to an optical assembly may support the hypothesis that it participates in signal acquisition, but it does not directly identify it as a particular analog front end.

Actively look for contradictory evidence as well: Is the candidate’s package clearly incompatible? Does the documentation concern another product generation? Is the relevant location in the photo actually hidden by a shield? Any conflict involving a key condition should lower your confidence in the judgment. Not seeing a separate package does not mean a function is absent; it may be integrated into a larger module.

## 7.6.5 Complete an Identification Diagram That Includes Unknowns

Now choose one question for a practical exercise, such as “Which areas on the back might be associated with optical measurement?” First, circle the windows in your own photo, then check the official feature descriptions for the corresponding model. If you find teardown material that matches the model and has clear provenance, examine the positions of the components behind those windows. Throughout this process, consult sources only: do not open the case or try to verify functions by wearing the watch in unusual ways.

You can organize your notes using the following evidence chain:

```text
Physical-device photograph and model
        | Verify the object
Official documentation for that version
        | Propose candidates
Traceable teardown photographs and component markings
        | Check agreement of appearance and function
Official component documentation
        |
Confirmed / supported inference / still unknown
```

The arrows in this diagram indicate the order of cross-checking, not a guarantee that the next stage will provide an answer. If no matching teardown material is available, the evidence chain stops there. Do not use images of a similar model to fill the gap and present the result as “fact.” Your identification diagram might state: “These windows are confirmed to exist on the back; official documentation states that this model supports optical heart rate measurement; the specific models of the components beneath the windows remain unknown.”

Finally, review each conclusion: Which photo or passage supports it? Have you turned physical proximity into functional certainty, product capability into actual activation, or a candidate part number into a claim that the part is installed? Only when you can answer these questions have you completed the identification exercise. Leaving something unknown is not a failure; it leaves room for better evidence next time.
