# 7.4 Getting to Know the Parts Behind the Screen: Identifying Display and Interaction Components

## 7.4.1 Start with a Photo of a Lifted Screen

In publicly available teardown photos, a watch screen is often lifted to one side, still connected to the watch body by a narrow flex cable. The complete display area faces forward, while the back may be covered with black film, metal sheets, or flexible circuitry printed with codes. Nearby, there may also be small rectangular blocks, cavities with openings, and button connectors. All contribute to interaction, but proximity to the screen alone does not make them display components.

When identifying parts, first establish the photo’s orientation: which surface faces the user, which side corresponds to the crown, and where the screen connects to the mainboard. Then distinguish the screen assembly, its connections, and other interaction components. Here, “assembly” means a set of parts already combined, potentially including the cover glass, touch-sensing structure, display panel, and associated connectors. It does not mean every layer can be seen separately in the photo.

Also check whether the photo was taken before disassembly, after lifting the screen, or after further removal of covering layers. Disassembly changes the positions of parts. A component hanging beside the screen was not necessarily installed there originally. Photos showing the original assembly are generally better for judging spatial relationships than a single photo of loose parts laid out together.

## 7.4.2 A Screen Is Not Just a Glowing Sheet of Glass

The outermost transparent cover provides protection and also affects reflections and the touch experience; it is not the entire structure that generates images. Beneath it, the display panel presents content through controlled pixels. In OLED products, the pixels emit their own light; common transmissive liquid-crystal displays require a backlight. To determine the specific type, consult the product’s technical documentation first rather than relying on observations such as “the back is very black” or “the screen is very thin.”

An active-matrix OLED panel also contains a thin-film transistor backplane that controls the pixels. LTPO is one backplane technology, not a generic name for the entire screen. If public specifications state only OLED, you cannot further conclude that the panel uses LTPO. Even when LTPO is explicitly specified, that does not mean all such products have the same refresh-rate range.

Transparent adhesives, optical films, and encapsulation layers are often tightly bonded, making them difficult to distinguish layer by layer in ordinary teardown photos. Some screens include polarizing structures, while others use different antireflection approaches. When you see a dark film, first describe its location, color, and coverage rather than immediately declaring it a polarizer. The first step in component identification is accurate description, not naming every piece of material as quickly as possible.

## 7.4.3 Follow the Flex Cable for Clues About Display Driving and Touch Sensing

Screen flex cables typically carry densely packed traces, contacts, and connectors. Sometimes small chips or covered raised areas are also visible. Display driver circuitry converts image data from the system into the control signals required by the panel, while touch-sensing circuitry reads electrical changes caused by touch. These functions may be implemented separately or partly integrated; there is no rule that every screen must have two separate chips behind it.

A small black block on a flex cable is not necessarily a display driver chip. It could also be a protection device, reinforcement material at a connection, or encapsulation material covering a chip. If its markings are readable, cross-check them against component documentation, package dimensions, and trace routing. If the markings are unclear, retain the tentative description “suspected driver-related area.”

The lead-out portion of a flexible display sometimes bends toward the back, keeping the connection area away from the front display area. Some designs mount chips on a plastic substrate, an approach commonly called COP. However, a bent flex cable in a photo is not sufficient evidence of COP, nor can it be used to infer bezel width. The panel’s lead-out structure must be distinguished from external flexible circuitry, and the specific manufacturing process still requires documentary support.

Touch electrodes are often transparent and finely patterned, and may also be integrated into the display structure, so ordinary photos generally do not show them clearly. Thus, “no visible touch film” does not mean “no touch-sensing layer.”

## 7.4.4 Identifying Sound, Vibration, and Button Components

Looking inward from openings in the watch case may lead you to the speaker assembly. It typically requires a diaphragm, a magnetic circuit, and an acoustic path to the outside, but its shape varies with the available space. Nearby seals and acoustic membranes also form part of that path. A hole in the case suggests only that sound or pressure exchange may be involved; the number of holes alone does not establish the number of speakers.

Microphone assemblies are usually smaller. A miniature package on the circuit board may connect to an opening in the case through a dedicated channel. Reliable identification requires considering the package, acoustic port, and connection path together. Do not probe openings with a needle, blow into them, or pour water into them to test their purpose; these actions may damage protective structures.

A haptic actuator often appears as a separate component with a metal housing. Its role is to convert electrical signals into perceptible mechanical vibration. Some products use linear resonant actuators, but not every rectangular metal component is one. Mounting position, electrical contacts, and reliable teardown documentation are more persuasive than a similar appearance.

For the crown and side buttons, distinguish the external controls from the internal sensing components. A rotating crown does not imply that every design uses the same encoding method, and a button’s spring-back feel does not directly reveal how its electrical signal is generated. Brackets, seals, and flexible circuitry in photos provide structural clues without necessarily exposing the complete sensing mechanism.

## 7.4.5 Make an Identification Card with Evidence Levels

Choose a set of publicly available photos with a clear source and an identified model, and label the screen assembly, flex-cable connector, suspected driver area, speaker, and haptic actuator. Write just three sentences for each item: what you see, what supports your judgment, and what remains unknown. For example: “There is a bent connection area on the back of the screen. Based on the device’s orientation, it appears to connect the panel to the mainboard. The chip-packaging process cannot be confirmed from the photo alone.”

Next, use official product specifications to verify the display type, touch capability, and sound functions, and component documentation to check any clearly readable part numbers. Specifications can confirm that a product is claimed to have a function, but do not necessarily prove that a particular part in a photo implements it. Different sizes, versions, or production batches may also vary. When documentation cannot be found, leave a blank rather than filling the gap with information from another watch of the same brand.

Finally, during normal use, observe a touch interaction, a turn of the crown, and a vibration alert, and connect these observable actions to the components you have identified. This task requires neither disassembly nor peeling off films or bending flex cables. Reliable identification is not about covering the back of the screen with labels. It is about giving every label an evidential basis and allowing what cannot yet be seen to remain unknown.
