# 9.5 Boundaries in Health and Privacy: What Should Not Be Left to AI Judgment

Late at night, your watch displays a health notification. You take a screenshot, ready to send it to AI and ask, “Is something wrong with me?” But the screenshot also includes your name, the measurement time, and your activity route for the day. A seemingly simple question crosses two boundaries at once: turning a device alert into an individual medical assessment, and handing information originally stored within your watch-and-phone system to another service. The first decision is not how to phrase the question, but what AI should not judge and what does not need to be uploaded.

## 9.5.1 Being Able to Explain an Alert Does Not Mean Being Able to Assess Your Health

When you receive a health notification, AI can explain terminology, help distinguish “measurement results,” “algorithmic estimates,” and “event notifications,” and assist with reading the official documentation for your particular model. But it cannot establish a cause, rule out disease, or decide whether you need to adjust your medication based on a screenshot alone. Even a calm, thoroughly reasoned answer does not mean it has sufficient clinical evidence.

The watch itself has already processed the information through several stages. Signals from the skin undergo collection, quality screening, and algorithmic processing before becoming the readings on the screen; those readings may then be aggregated over time to produce trends or notifications. Sending that final result to AI does not restore the information missing from earlier stages. AI usually cannot see the raw waveforms, sensor contact conditions, complete medical history, or test results, nor can it verify whether the screenshot reflects your current condition.

For example, “no abnormality notification received” does not mean “all heart disease has been ruled out.” A watch ECG records electrical potential differences at the body’s surface under specific contact conditions. Single-lead features have defined limits on eligible users, detectable events, and regional authorization. Pulse information estimated through PPG is not the same as an ECG, either. If AI confuses the two, its subsequent assessment remains fundamentally flawed, however detailed it may be.

## 9.5.2 Do Not Leave Decisions About Action to a Chat Response

Decisions that may change what you do about your health most need to remain between you and professional care providers. Whether to stop or increase medication, ignore ongoing discomfort, or use one normal reading to dismiss persistent symptoms should not be decided by general-purpose AI based on watch data. It can help organize questions to discuss with a doctor, but it cannot replace a doctor’s individual assessment.

If you are experiencing significant or persistent physical discomfort, do not keep taking measurements or wait for chat replies in pursuit of a “more complete” AI analysis. Seek professional medical help promptly as appropriate, and contact local emergency services in an emergency. Neither the absence of a watch alert nor an AI assessment of low risk should be a reason to delay seeking help.

For questions that are not urgent, you can rephrase your request: “What does this notification mean according to this device’s documentation? What conclusions cannot be drawn from it?” This places AI in the role of explaining information rather than judging your health. If it still offers a diagnosis or medication advice directly, stop pursuing that line of questioning rather than supplying more private information in exchange for a more definite answer.

## 9.5.3 Health Screenshots Often Contain More Than Health Data

A workout record may reveal your residential area, frequently visited places, and activity times all at once. A continuous heart-rate record, combined with your schedule and location, may also reveal your daily routines. Hiding your name does not mean the remaining information can no longer be linked to you. Route starting and ending points, in particular, often reveal more about where you live than a profile picture does.

Before uploading, ask: “Does the recipient really need this information to explain the concept?” Explaining why motion affects PPG generally does not require your real name, date of birth, full medical history, or an entire year of records. To discuss why a recorded route drifts, you can use a partial illustration instead of a complete map showing your home. If a written summary will suffice, there is no need to provide the original file.

Account passwords, verification codes, recovery keys, and device-pairing credentials should not be shared with AI. Nor should you freely upload a family member’s or classmate’s health report merely because you have access to it. When other people’s information is involved, respect their right to know and choose. Even with consent, submit only what is necessary for the task, rather than sending everything at once.

## 9.5.4 Understand Where the Data Goes, and Do Not Assume Deletion Is Simple

Your watch, phone app, and AI service may represent different stages of data processing. Sending a health screenshot from your phone to a chat service adds a new recipient; it is not simply clicking an extra “explain” button within your existing health app. Permission previously granted to the watch app does not automatically mean you accept another service’s data practices.

Services may have different rules for content retention, human access, model improvement, third-party processing, and deletion. A padlock icon in the interface or a reference to encryption in promotional materials does not establish that every stage has the same protections. Protected transmission does not mean content will not be stored; disabling a model-improvement option does not necessarily mean all historical data is immediately erased.

Before use, review the service’s current privacy notice and settings to confirm how uploaded content will be used, who can access it, how it is stored, and where deletion controls are located. If you cannot understand or verify the terms, you can postpone uploading. Deleting chat history, deleting uploaded files, and requesting deletion of account data may be separate actions. Do not interpret an on-screen “deleted” message as a guarantee that every copy has instantly disappeared.

## 9.5.5 Check the Boundaries Without Uploading Real Records

You can do a safe exercise with pen and paper: imagine your watch reports that a measurement could not be interpreted. Write down what you originally wanted to ask AI, then divide it into three categories: “explaining concepts,” “assessing personal health,” and “providing private information.” Keep the first category, turn the second into questions to verify with a professional, and remove anything in the third that is irrelevant to the explanation.

Next, draft a question containing no real records: “Please explain the general meaning of an uninterpretable watch measurement, distinguish insufficient signal quality from conclusions about health, and indicate which official resources I should consult.” When reviewing the answer, check whether it acknowledges that the specific cause is unknown and whether it mistakenly presents general possibilities as things that have actually happened to you.

Uploading less and drawing fewer conclusions about health and privacy does not mean giving up on learning. AI can help you understand evidence, but it should not determine your health status or obtain a complete record of your private life just to answer a conceptual question. Maintaining these two boundaries provides a trustworthy starting point for collaborative learning.
