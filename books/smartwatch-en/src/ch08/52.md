# 9.4 Using AI to Organize Evidence: Comparing Sources, Identifying Contradictions, and Preserving Unknowns

## 9.4.1 Start by Breaking Down a Conclusion

You want to know whether your watch can record a running route on its own. The product page says it “supports satellite positioning,” the user guide mentions “connecting to a phone,” and a review says it “can record without a phone.” Give these passages to AI, and it may quickly answer yes. Yet these statements may not be answering the same question: support for positioning does not necessarily tell you which device determines the location, and connecting to a phone may be required only to sync records.

The first step in organizing evidence is not to ask AI to judge who is right or wrong, but to separate out specific claims that can be checked. For example: Does this watch have a built-in GNSS receiver? Does it need assistance data from a phone before an activity begins? Can it save location data during an activity without a phone? Can the map be displayed only on the phone? Once the questions are broken down, descriptions that initially seemed contradictory may turn out to refer to different stages: positioning, assistance, storage, and display.

AI is well suited to organizing these stages, but fluent language cannot fill gaps in the evidence. Whether a feature exists, the conditions under which it operates, and how it performs in practice should be verified separately.

## 9.4.2 Preserve the Provenance of Every Piece of Evidence

When gathering information, you can ask AI to create an evidence log. Each entry should include the source name, publication date or version, applicable model, an excerpt from the original text, the claim it supports, and any conditions left unspecified. If a webpage does not show an update date, write “not specified on the page” rather than letting AI guess. Along with saving links, keep the key passages so that you can trace the basis for your conclusions even if the page changes.

Different sources also serve different purposes. Product specifications are useful for confirming publicly claimed capabilities; user guides help identify operating conditions; and feature support pages often explain regional, system-version, and authorization restrictions. A chip datasheet explains a component’s capabilities, but cannot by itself prove that a particular watch uses that chip. Reviews can provide observations under specific conditions, but a single test cannot cover every user and environment.

You might instruct AI: “Organize only the materials I provide, without adding unverified information. After each conclusion, quote the original text supporting it and identify any evidence gaps.” If AI has internet access, also ask which pages it actually opened and which it saw only as search snippets. Finding a title, reading the full text, and checking a specific passage are not equivalent levels of evidence review.

You should also check whether sources are independent of one another. Three articles that all repeat the same press release do not amount to three independent verifications. AI can help identify shared wording and citation relationships, but you still need to return to the pages to confirm whether one source actually draws on another.

## 9.4.3 Compare Conditions Before Declaring a Contradiction

When two sources disagree, do not simply rank them by assuming that “official sources are always correct” or “newer articles are always more accurate.” First check whether they discuss the same model, region, software version, and operating mode. A health feature may have the necessary hardware but not yet be available locally; different versions within the same product series may also have different communication capabilities.

Consider water-resistance descriptions. A product page lists a water-resistance rating, while a support page lists water activities the device is not suitable for. These statements usually do not contradict each other; rather, they distinguish rated test conditions from real-world usage restrictions. If AI compresses them into “safe for unrestricted use at the rated depth,” it loses the most important limits. A proper summary should preserve the rating, the restrictions, and the explanation that sealing performance may change with use.

Genuine contradictions should be clearly flagged. For example, two official documents for the same model and version may give different answers about whether a feature is available. In that case, retain both original passages, record the date you checked them, and look for update notes or contact official support. Until further evidence is available, “the current sources are inconsistent” is more reliable than forcing them into a single answer.

## 9.4.4 Keep Facts, Inferences, and Unknowns in Separate Columns

A useful learning record should clearly distinguish three kinds of content. Facts are statements explicitly supported by a source; inferences are explanations drawn from those facts; unknowns are matters that cannot currently be confirmed from the available information. Even “facts” need to be bounded carefully: what an official page says is a verifiable documentary fact, while whether a device achieves that performance under all conditions requires separate verification.

Suppose a route recorded during an everyday walk shows some drift. Your confirmed observation may be only this: along a particular stretch, the mapped track diverged from the road you actually followed. Obstruction by buildings, reflected signals, how the watch was worn, and the positioning mode can all be listed as explanations to investigate. But a route image alone cannot establish that the receiver is damaged, much less that a particular algorithm has failed.

Asking AI to list “existing support,” “missing information,” and “safe ways to check” for each explanation is usually more valuable than asking it for a single cause. For example, checking activity-mode settings, consulting the device instructions, and comparing recordings on open stretches during normal outings are all more appropriate than taking the device apart yourself. If raw signals or internal logs are unavailable, some causes should remain in the unknown column rather than being replaced with a professional-sounding story.

## 9.4.5 Make the Evidence Table Open to Revision

Organizing evidence is not a one-off scoring contest. When information is updated, earlier conclusions may need to be narrowed or overturned by new evidence. You can label each conclusion with a status such as “supported by direct evidence,” “indirect support only,” “conflicting sources,” or “not yet confirmed,” and record the date it was last checked. These labels describe the state of the evidence, not statistical confidence probabilities.

A short exercise can help establish this habit: choose one nonmedical feature of your watch, gather the product page, user guide, and one independent observation, and ask AI to compare them point by point against the same question. Then open the original sources yourself and check whether it has omitted qualifiers, confused models, or rewritten an author’s speculation as established fact. When you find an error, retain the reason for the revision rather than merely replacing the final answer.

The final result need not be a report claiming to have “figured everything out.” Instead, it should be a learning record that identifies the evidence, conditions, and gaps. AI helps us organize the material more clearly; the evidence itself still determines how much a statement can legitimately claim.
