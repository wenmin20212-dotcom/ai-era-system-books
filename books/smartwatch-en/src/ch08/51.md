# 9.3 Using AI to Understand Documentation: Explaining Terms, Parameters, and Applicable Conditions

When you pick up a watch’s product documentation, you may recognize every word yet still not understand what “dual-frequency positioning,” “typical power consumption,” or “system-in-package” actually means. Open a chip datasheet, and the abbreviations, units, and footnotes in its tables can make it equally hard to know where to begin. Here, AI can serve as a reading assistant: unpacking unfamiliar expressions, providing essential background, and highlighting conditions that must not be overlooked. But it cannot replace the original text, nor should it turn undisclosed information into established facts.

## 9.3.1 Start by Giving AI the Original Passage You Need Explained

When asking AI to read documentation, it is best to begin with a short, accurate excerpt rather than simply asking, “How good are this watch’s sensors?” Provide the product name, document title, version date, and the passage or table you want explained, retaining units, column headings, and footnotes. If you use a screenshot, check that the text is legible, particularly to prevent misreading decimal points, plus and minus signs, and units.

A useful request is: “Use only this excerpt and explain it in language a general reader can understand. First state what it explicitly says, then explain the terminology, and finally list any conditions or context still needed to understand it. Mark information not provided as unknown.” This keeps the task focused on reading and reduces the chance that AI will fill in gaps based on common product configurations.

If AI cannot open a link, copy the relevant text instead of assuming it has visited the page. Its ability to describe a document in broad terms does not mean it has seen the version you have. For feature descriptions that are continually updated, the publication date, applicable models, and software version are themselves part of understanding the documentation.

## 9.3.2 Explain Terms by Locating Them Within the System

Explaining terminology involves more than expanding an abbreviation. For example, PPG stands for photoplethysmography, but that word alone does little to help a beginner understand it. A more effective explanation answers three questions: What does it receive, what changes does it observe, and what results can it ultimately support?

You can ask AI to explain PPG along the chain “light source—tissue—photodetector—acquisition circuitry—algorithm”: light is absorbed and scattered after entering tissue; the detector receives some of that light; the circuitry records changes in light intensity; and the algorithm uses components associated with changes in blood volume to estimate pulse information. This explanation also establishes boundaries: PPG does not capture images of blood flow or record an electrocardiogram.

When you encounter SiP, likewise ask AI to explain that it refers to packaging, not a particular fixed processor architecture. A system-in-package can integrate multiple components more closely, but that does not establish that a particular watch uses a dual-chip design. Understanding a term means more than remembering its full name: it means knowing what it does and does not tell you, and how it works with neighboring modules.

## 9.3.3 Always Read a Parameter Alongside Its Conditions

A parameter is not a promise that holds independently of its operating environment. Power consumption in a chip datasheet may correspond to a particular supply voltage, channel configuration, and operating sequence; battery life on a product page may assume specific display, positioning, and communication settings. Without those conditions, two numbers may not be directly comparable even if their units match.

Ask AI to address each item: “What does this value describe? What units does it use? In which operating mode was it obtained? Is it a typical value, a guaranteed range, or a maximum permissible value?” A typical value generally describes representative performance under specified conditions, not a result guaranteed for every individual device. An absolute maximum rating is a stress limit that must not be exceeded, not a recommended operating point.

Also watch for differences in scope. The power consumption of an optical acquisition chip is not the power consumption of the entire watch, because the light source, processor, display, and wireless communications may also consume energy. Similarly, a sampling rate in the documentation is not necessarily the frequency at which the on-screen reading updates: the algorithm may combine multiple samples before producing one result. Having AI explain these distinctions is more valuable than simply comparing which number is larger.

## 9.3.4 Use Component Documentation to Understand the Signal Chain, Not to Guess About the Whole Device

TI’s official product page for the AFE4404 can serve as an entry point for reading documentation on optical acquisition components. It can help readers understand how an LED driver, an analog front end, and a photodetector form an acquisition chain. However, it does not establish that your watch uses this particular model, nor can the component’s capabilities alone reveal the accuracy of the finished device’s health features.

When reading, ask a focused question: “Based on the official documentation provided, explain the respective roles of the LED driver and receiving circuitry. Which specifications describe the component’s capabilities, and which aspects of overall device performance still depend on optical layout, fit, and algorithms?” AI can then help distinguish their roles: the driver circuitry controls the light source according to its settings, the receiving chain processes electrical signals generated by the detector, and the final readings also depend on subsequent processing and product validation.

If you go on to compare two components, first check whether their test conditions are comparable. Power consumption measured with different channel counts, light-source currents, or acquisition timing cannot be used directly to rank them. Nor should you ask AI to decide which component is “most accurate during exercise” based solely on product summaries. Chip specifications provide only part of the picture; they cannot replace an evaluation of the entire measurement system.

## 9.3.5 Turn Applicable Conditions into Understandable Limits on Use

The final step in understanding documentation is to express its limitations in everyday language. For example, “supports dual-frequency GNSS” indicates that a device has the corresponding signal reception and processing capabilities. It does not guarantee identical positioning performance among tall buildings, in woodland, or in every multipath environment. You can ask AI to explain how these environments affect signals, but not to invent a definitive explanation for a particular deviation in a recorded route.

Water-resistance documentation benefits from the same approach. Ask AI to list the stated rating, permitted activities, prohibited situations, and maintenance requirements separately, rather than directly converting a pressure-test rating into permission to dive. Explanations of health features must likewise retain applicable models, regions, authorization status, and usage restrictions. A particular notification capability must not be expanded into a comprehensive diagnostic capability.

Try selecting a nonsensitive parameter description from your watch’s manual. First write down your own understanding, then ask AI to explain it, and check each sentence against the original. Finish with three statements: what the documentation explicitly establishes, under what conditions it holds, and what remains uncertain. In this way, AI offers more than a fluent paraphrase. It provides a reusable reading method: place terms within the system, keep numbers attached to their conditions, and limit conclusions to what the evidence supports.
