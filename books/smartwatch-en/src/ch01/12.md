# 2.6 Tracing Modules Through a Workout Record: Mapping Functional Dependencies

## 2.6.1 From Pressing “Start” to Exploring Internal Cooperation

Before heading out for a walk, you select “Outdoor Walk” on your watch and press Start. A timer appears on the screen, and you feel a vibration on your wrist. When you return and end the recording, the page shows duration, heart rate, and step count; some devices also display a route. With all these results packed into one card, it is easy to assume that they come from a single “workout sensor.” In reality, each result has its own source and depends on different modules working together.

In this section, rather than opening the watch case, we will follow an ordinary walking record to reconnect the modules introduced earlier. What we are drawing is not a map of component locations, but a functional dependency diagram: which modules supply inputs, which perform processing, and which handle storage and presentation for a given function. Understanding these dependencies helps us avoid interpreting “no route” as “the entire watch is broken.”

Base your observations on the device manual and the functions actually available. A watch without standalone positioning may obtain location data through a phone; a model that does not support a particular health feature should not be assigned the corresponding module in the diagram. Your diagram should describe your own device, not an imaginary watch that can do everything.

## 2.6.2 Breaking a Record Card into Separate Results

Start with workout duration. It mainly depends on system timekeeping, state management in the workout app, and operations such as starting, pausing, and stopping. The screen displays the time, but it is not the source of that time. Even when the screen temporarily turns off, timing usually continues as long as the workout task remains active. This lets us distinguish between “whether a result is generated” and “whether a result is visible.”

Next, consider step count. An accelerometer provides information about wrist movement, and an algorithm identifies patterns associated with walking. Some implementations also incorporate other motion information, but this does not mean every device uses its gyroscope in the same way. Arm movements and steps do not correspond one-to-one, either. Step count is therefore a record produced through interpretation, not a direct sensor count of how many times your feet touch the ground.

Heart rate follows a different branch: a light source illuminates the skin, a photodetector receives the returning light, acquisition circuitry converts the changes into a processable signal, and an algorithm estimates pulse rate. A loose fit, motion interference, or changes in local blood flow can all affect this branch. A temporary loss of heart-rate data does not necessarily mean that timing and step counting have also failed.

A route requires a source of location data. Watches with standalone GNSS can use satellite signals to determine their position; some devices or operating modes rely on phone positioning. Distance should not automatically be equated with route length, either: depending on the circumstances, it may be derived from changes in position or estimated using step count and a stride-length model. Adjacent numbers on a record card do not necessarily share the same evidential basis.

## 2.6.3 Using Arrows to Show “What Depends on What”

When drawing the diagram, place “workout record” on the right and the input and supporting modules on the left. Arrows indicate functional dependencies, not necessarily physical wiring, and certainly not the actual locations of components inside the case. The following is a conceptual illustration; you can remove or adjust branches to suit your device.

```text
Touch/buttons -> Activity app and state management <- System timing
Motion sensors -> Motion recognition ---|
Optical acquisition -> Heart-rate estimate ---|--> Save records --> Display
Watch GNSS or phone location -> Position ---|          |--> Synchronize as needed
Battery and power management --> Appropriate electrical power
Processor, memory and software --> Processing, operation and storage
```

The convergence of several branches does not mean they must all succeed at the same time. The system may save a record containing duration and step count but lacking reliable heart-rate data or a complete route. Saving a record does not mean it has already been uploaded, either: it can remain on the watch until the connection requirements for synchronization are met.

This diagram also reveals two types of dependency. The link between optical signal acquisition and heart-rate estimation is specific to one result, while power, computation, and storage provide a shared foundation for multiple functions. One processor may handle several tasks, and one package may contain several functional units. A box in the diagram therefore cannot simply be treated as a separate chip.

## 2.6.4 Checking Dependencies Through Partial Data Loss

The greatest value of a dependency diagram is that it helps us ask more precise questions. Suppose a record contains duration and step count but no route. First check whether the device supports the positioning method being used, whether you brought the required phone, whether location permissions were granted, and whether the environment was favorable for signal reception. A missing result alone does not establish that an antenna or positioning chip is damaged.

If the route is present but heart-rate data is intermittent, attention shifts to fit, the optical window, and measurement conditions. These are directions for investigation, however, not fault diagnoses. Users generally cannot see raw signals or internal operating logs, so record “observed gaps in heart-rate data” separately from “possible motion interference.”

Likewise, not feeling a vibration at the end may have to do with feedback settings or how the watch sits on your wrist; it does not prove that the record was not saved. The vibration actuator communicates status, while storage preserves the result. Both are often triggered by the same action, but they serve different purposes. Such counterexamples remind us that occurring together does not mean being mutually necessary.

## 2.6.5 Drawing a Diagram for Your Own Record

Choose a normal, safe, short walk. There is no need to aim for speed, and do not look at the screen while crossing a road. Before starting, note the device model, workout mode, whether you are carrying a phone, and the relevant positioning and health-feature settings. After finishing, review the record in a safe place, list the items actually displayed, and identify the modules needed for each result.

You can begin with four branches: duration, step count, heart rate, and route. For any missing item, simply note “not supported” or “not displayed this time.” Then add input operations, computation, storage, presentation, and power. Mark synchronization as an optional branch; there is no need to trace the exact transmission process for every piece of data in this exercise.

Finally, annotate the connections with evidence: mark dependencies explicitly stated in the manual as “checked,” explanations inferred from observations as “to be verified,” and internal implementations you cannot confirm as “unknown.” Do not guess chip models merely to make the diagram complete, and do not try to verify it by disabling safety features, engaging in dangerous exercise, or taking the device apart.

Once you have finished, ask yourself: Which branch could be removed with the loss of only one result? Which shared support function, if it failed, would affect many features? If you can answer these two questions, you have moved beyond “knowing component names” toward “understanding how modules work together.” How these modules fit tightly inside a watch is the spatial question we will address next.
