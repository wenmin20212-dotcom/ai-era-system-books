# 4.3 How Modules Form a Whole: Circuit Boards, Flexible Interconnects, and Contacts

Lay a watch flat on a table: the screen faces up, the sensor window on the back faces down, and the crown projects from the side. These components do not share a single plane, and space must also be reserved inside for the battery, speaker, and vibration actuator. How does the processor communicate with them? The answer involves more than “connecting a few wires.” Circuits with different jobs must form a stable, assembly-friendly connection network within a confined space.

## 4.3.1 Circuit Boards Are Both Foundations and Roads

A circuit board first provides mounting locations for components. The processor, memory, power components, and many tiny resistors and capacitors are secured to the board by solder joints. Copper traces inside the board and on its surfaces connect the corresponding terminals. Solder joints provide both electrical connections and some mechanical attachment, but they cannot replace all structural support.

The traces are not all crowded onto the visible surfaces. Multilayer circuit boards stack conductive layers and insulating layers, then use conductive vias to connect different layers. Think of this as a road system with overpasses: two locations that appear unconnected on the surface may be linked by internal traces. This is only an analogy to aid understanding, however. Electrical signal propagation is also affected by trace geometry, materials, and surrounding conductors.

Different roads have different requirements. Power traces need controlled resistance and voltage drop; rapidly changing digital signals need suitable transmission conditions; and weak analog signals from sensors need to stay as far as possible from interference sources. Board layout therefore involves more than routing traces wherever space is available. Power, signals, and their return paths must be arranged together. “Ground” is not a place where current magically disappears, but an important part of the circuit’s reference and return network.

## 4.3.2 Flexible Interconnects Bridge Heights and Corners

Rigid circuit boards are well suited to supporting components, but they cannot bend freely to follow internal contours. The screen sits at the top, the sensor assembly lies near the back housing, and the side buttons sit against the frame. Connecting them often requires a flexible printed circuit, commonly abbreviated FPC. It typically consists of thin copper traces supported by a flexible insulating material and covered with a protective layer, forming a circuit strip that can bend as designed.

The purpose of a flexible circuit is not to “fill up” space, but to route around the battery, brackets, and screw posts while bridging different heights. Some products use separate flex cables to connect rigid boards; others use rigid-flex boards that integrate component-bearing rigid regions with flexible connecting regions. The choice depends on layout, cost, assembly, and reliability requirements. A watch’s external appearance cannot establish that all watches use the same internal structure.

“Flexible” does not mean it can be folded arbitrarily. Too small a bend radius may place excessive strain on the copper traces, while repeated bending can cause fatigue cracks. Permissible bending depends on the materials, layer count, copper thickness, bend direction, and number of bending cycles. Bending a circuit once during assembly and securing it in place is a different design condition from bending it repeatedly every day. A seemingly sharp fold in a teardown photograph should not be treated as an instruction to copy.

## 4.3.3 Contacts Make Assembly a Controlled Process

If every component were permanently soldered together, assembly and repair would be difficult. Miniature connectors therefore serve as interfaces between modules. Board-to-board connectors allow two sets of contacts to mate, while some flex cables slide into sockets with locking latches. These provide multiple connections in a very small volume, although their exact forms and dimensions vary by product.

Reliable contact depends on more than whether something is “plugged in.” Terminals need appropriate contact pressure, and their surface finishes must balance conductivity, wear resistance, and corrosion resistance. Some connections also use retaining plates, brackets, or adhesive to limit movement caused by shock and vibration. Each structure has its own job: the connector establishes contact, while the retaining hardware reduces the risk of separation. A retaining plate should not be regarded as a universal safeguard.

Some connections do not use sockets at all. Spring contacts, for example, can press against metal contact pads to connect antennas or other structural parts, while soldering can be used where frequent separation is unnecessary. Spring contacts simplify assembly but depend on compression and positional accuracy. Soldered connections are relatively secure but may make disassembly and reassembly more difficult. No connection method is inherently superior regardless of circumstances; what matters is whether it suits the task at its location.

## 4.3.4 Electrical Connections Must Also Fit the Mechanical Space

Imagine a flex cable running from the main board to the sensor assembly at the bottom. If electrical continuity is the only thing checked, the task may appear complete. Once installed in the case, however, the cable might be pinched by an edge, push against a connector, or be pulled by a heavier component during a drop. Engineers must therefore also check the cable route, edge clearances, securing points, and assembly sequence.

Even when two watches use similar components, changing the battery shape or screen position may require redesigning the interconnects. Miniaturization does not mean eliminating every gap. It means distinguishing between spaces that can be reduced and those that must remain.

## 4.3.5 Tracing a Connection in Publicly Available Photographs

Choose publicly available teardown photographs from a reliable source that clearly identify the model, and try tracing a path from the screen to the main board without opening your own watch. First mark the visible flexible, ribbon-like structures, then look for sockets, retaining plates, or soldered connections at their ends. Represent obscured sections with dashed lines rather than filling them in from imagination.

Keep three categories of conclusions separate in your notes. “A flex cable is visible in the photograph” is an observation. “It may connect the screen to the main board” is an inference. Without supporting documentation, “which signals it carries and how many copper layers it contains” remain unknown. Color, width, and shape offer clues, but cannot independently establish function.

Once you understand the interconnects, the watch’s interior is no longer just a collection of neighboring parts: circuit boards organize the traces, flexible structures span different positions, contacts establish interfaces, and securing structures protect the connections. Together, they determine whether the modules can keep working in concert through wear, charging, and everyday bumps.
