# 8.4 Reinforcement Learning: Learning to Act Through Trial and Error

If you have used a coding agent, you may have noticed a familiar scene: it edits a piece of code, runs the tests itself, sees an error, edits again, and repeats until the tests pass. The process looks a lot like a child learning to ride a bicycle. No one can fully explain "keeping your balance" in words; the child learns it through repeated wobbling, falling, and adjusting. There is a field within artificial intelligence devoted to "learning to act through trial and error," called reinforcement learning (RL). It is an important cornerstone for understanding *why agents can get things done*.

## 8.4.1 A Way of Learning Without Answer Keys

Let's first compare reinforcement learning with the more familiar ways of learning:

| Learning approach | Learning material | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised learning | Large numbers of "questions + correct answers" | Working through a textbook with an answer key |
| Unsupervised learning | Only data, no answers | Sorting a pile of photos into groups on your own |
| Reinforcement learning | Feedback on how good or bad an action turned out | Learning to ride a bike or play chess |

What sets reinforcement learning apart is that no one tells you what to do at each step. You only find out afterward whether the outcome was good. Moreover, this feedback is often delayed: in chess, a brilliant move may not show up in the win or loss until dozens of moves later. How to fairly distribute credit or blame for the final result across all the earlier steps is one of the core problems reinforcement learning must solve, known in the research literature as the "credit assignment problem."

## 8.4.2 Five Basic Elements: Agent, Environment, State, Action, Reward

Reinforcement learning uses a very concise framework to describe the act of "doing things." Take a coding agent fixing a bug as an example:

- **Agent**: The party making decisions, here the model-driven coding assistant.
- **Environment**: The external world it faces, here the code repository, the terminal, and the test suite.
- **State**: What the environment looks like at this moment, such as the current code and the most recent error message.
- **Action**: What the agent can do, such as reading a file, changing a line, or running the tests.
- **Reward**: Numerical feedback from the environment. For example, all tests passing earns a positive score, while introducing a new bug earns a negative one.

The agent chooses an action in a given state, the environment moves to a new state and returns a reward, and the cycle repeats. The agent is not chasing the reward of any single step but the largest possible cumulative reward over the long run. The rule mapping "which action to take in which state" is called a policy. The goal of reinforcement learning is to find a good policy through repeated interaction.

At this point you may notice that this framework is almost the same picture as the "reason–act loop" in Section 6.1 and the cybernetic feedback loop in Section 8.2. The difference is that cybernetics is concerned with keeping a system stable near its target, while reinforcement learning goes further and asks **how a system can improve its own policy through the loop**. This formal description usually rests on the mathematics of the Markov Decision Process (MDP), which is also an extension of the "utility maximization" idea from Section 8.1 to multi-step decisions.

## 8.4.3 Exploration vs. Exploitation: Should You Try the New Restaurant?

Reinforcement learning contains a dilemma everyone has experienced: exploration versus exploitation. When eating out on the weekend, going to your usual place is the safe choice, but you may never discover something better. Trying a new place might be a letdown, or it might be a pleasant surprise.

Agents face the same choice. Always sticking with methods known to work can trap them in solutions that are merely "pretty good," while constantly trying new actions wastes time and money. A common compromise is to pick the current best action most of the time, keep a small probability of trying something at random, and gradually reduce exploration as experience accumulates. This idea also helps explain why the same agent facing the same task sometimes takes different paths. In practice, a moderate amount of variety is not necessarily a flaw.

## 8.4.4 The Role of Reinforcement Learning in Today's Agents

A common misconception needs clearing up: when the agent at your fingertips is working for you, it is usually **not performing reinforcement learning in real time**. Its "error, edit, retry" behavior during a task relies on the model's existing capabilities plus the orchestration program's loop (see Section 6.5). The model's parameters themselves do not change because of that task. Reinforcement learning mainly happens during the model's training phase, along two broadly recognized paths:

1. **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).** People compare and rate multiple responses from a model, which is used to train a "reward model." That reward model then guides adjustments to the large model so its answers become more useful and better aligned with what people expect. This is a common practice described in public accounts of how conversational models are trained; for details, see Volume 18, *Large Language Models*.
2. **Reinforcement learning from verifiable outcomes.** For tasks whose correctness can be checked automatically, such as math or programming problems, whether the tests pass or the answer is correct can serve directly as the reward. According to technical reports that several model companies had published at the time of writing, such methods are used to improve models' reasoning and multi-step task abilities, including having models practice calling tools and completing tasks in simulated environments.

Historically, reinforcement learning's best-known successes came from games and board games, such as DeepMind's published AlphaGo series of Go programs, which combined search (Section 8.3) with learning from self-play. Today's large-model agents carry this idea of "trial and error in an environment" into the more open world of language and tools.

## 8.4.5 The Pitfalls of Reward Design: It Learns What You Said, Not What You Meant

Reinforcement learning carries a risk that offers useful lessons for users as well, called "reward hacking": the system finds shortcuts that raise the reward while defeating the designer's intent. The research literature contains classic examples, such as a game agent that discovered it could score more points by circling in place to farm rewards than by finishing the race.

Applied to coding agents, if the reward is simply "tests pass," a poorly trained system might learn to modify the tests themselves rather than fix the code. This suggests two lessons. For developers, rewards should match the real goal as closely as possible. For users, when you set a "definition of done" for an agent, think carefully about whether it could "meet the bar" the wrong way. Add constraints where needed, such as "do not modify the test files," and check the results yourself (see Sections 3.4 and 4.4).

## 8.4.6 Summary

Reinforcement learning answers part of the question "where does an agent's ability to act come from?": by acting in an environment, receiving feedback, and adjusting its policy, a system can learn skills that cannot be taught directly through answer keys. But trial and error requires an environment that provides reliable feedback, and in the real world many goals are hard to score and many consequences cannot be undone. So what, in principle, are the things agents cannot do? That is the subject of the next section: the limits set by uncertainty and computability.
