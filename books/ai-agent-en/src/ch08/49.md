# 8.6 Chapter Skeleton Summary and Hands-On Exploration Tasks

In the preceding chapters, we kept looking at *how* an agent does things: how it breaks down tasks, calls tools, manages memory, and recovers from failure. This chapter turned in a different direction. It asked *why* an agent can work this way, and *why* there are some things it cannot do. Go back to a task a reader might hand to an agent, such as "Look up quotes from five suppliers and put them in a table." At the level of principles, this job breaks into five questions: What does it want (goals and utility)? How does it adjust as it goes (feedback)? How does it find a path (search and planning)? Where were its skills trained (reinforcement learning)? Where are its limits (uncertainty and computability)? This section first links these five pieces of the skeleton together. It then asks you to test them on an agent you have at hand.

## 8.6.1 Review of the Chapter Skeleton

The five sections of this chapter correspond to five basic principles. Most of them were proposed decades ago and are not new inventions of the large-model era. What large models did was place these principles inside a system that can understand natural language.

| Principle | Core question | Its trace in a real task |
|---|---|---|
| Rational agents and decision theory | How to choose the "best" among several options | Whether to check official websites or price-comparison sites first; when to stop and ask you |
| Cybernetic feedback | How to correct the next step based on results | Regenerating the table after a formatting error; revising code again when a test fails |
| Search and planning | How to find a path among many possible steps | Breaking "compile the quotes" into four steps: search, extract, verify, summarize |
| Reinforcement learning | Where the ability to act was learned | The model received positive signals during training for completing tasks and following instructions |
| Uncertainty and computability | What cannot be done, or cannot be done precisely, in principle | Outdated web information, vaguely stated goals, no way to guarantee "it will never go wrong" |

A few key points are worth remembering:

- **Rational does not mean all-knowing.** The standard for a rational agent is to choose the action with the highest expected utility given the information it has. It is not to make the right choice every time. If the utility function is written wrong, a "rational" agent will diligently do the wrong thing.
- **Feedback is the root of error correction.** The reasoning–acting loop is essentially a closed-loop control system: act, observe the result, compare it with the goal, and act again. Without reliable observation, this loop just spins in place.
- **Planning is pathfinding in a "state space."** From classical search algorithms to the step-by-step plans of large models, the work is the same: narrowing down a space with many possibilities. A large model is good at proposing candidate steps that "look reasonable." Search and verification still depend on tools and feedback.
- **Reinforcement learning (RL) shapes behavioral tendencies.** A common account in public sources is that, after pretraining, today's mainstream models are further trained with human feedback or with task outcomes that can be checked automatically. Training details are covered in Volume 18, *Large Language Models*. Here you only need to know that an agent's "willingness" to work step by step and call tools comes partly from this kind of training.
- **The limits are a matter of principle.** Results such as the halting problem tell us that no general program can decide whether any arbitrary program will terminate. Partial observability of the environment also means that an agent always acts with incomplete information. So human confirmation, sandboxes, and evaluation are designs that will always be needed. They are not temporary patches.

Put the five together: goals give direction, planning gives a route, feedback gives correction, learning gives capability, and limits give constraints. Every component discussed in the previous seven chapters has a place on this map.

## 8.6.2 Hands-On Exploration Tasks

Each task below only requires an agent or a tool-using AI assistant you have access to. This could be a coding assistant, a chat assistant with web search, or an AI feature in office software. Interfaces and capabilities varied widely across products at the time of writing, so go by what you actually have.

1. **Write your "utility function."** Pick something you plan to hand to an agent. First, write three sentences stating "what counts as done well, what must not be done, and when it should ask me first." Then give the agent the task twice: once with this description, and once with a simple one-line instruction. Compare the two results. You will see directly how the way a goal is stated affects "rational choice."
2. **Draw a feedback-loop diagram.** Have the agent complete a small task that needs checking, such as writing a short program that runs or verifying the total of a set of figures. Using its process log, mark the step where each part of "act–observe–compare–correct" happens. Then look for places where it declared the job done without checking.
3. **Compare "plan first" with "think as you go."** Run the same task twice. The first time, ask it to "list a complete plan before executing." The second time, give no such instruction. Record the number of steps, the number of times it backtracked, and the final quality of each run. This shows the trade-offs between search and planning.
4. **Create some uncertainty.** Give a deliberately vague instruction, such as "tidy up that table a bit." Watch whether the agent guesses, asks, or sets its own standard. This is exactly the decision problem in a partially observable environment.
5. **Look for the limits.** Ask the agent to guarantee that a piece of code "will never loop forever on any input," or to promise that some piece of web information is "absolutely up to date." See how it responds. A good response explains the scope and conditions of any guarantee instead of simply agreeing.

## 8.6.3 Questions You Can Ask an AI Directly

- "Using the example of compiling supplier quotes, explain the role that decision theory, cybernetic feedback, and search and planning each play in an agent, and point out where each is most likely to fail."
- "What practical effects do the halting problem and partial observability have on designing an agent that can automatically modify code? Give two engineering responses."
- "When the behavioral tendencies produced by reinforcement learning conflict with instructions I wrote in the system prompt, what usually happens? Please indicate which claims have public evidence behind them and which are only speculation."

After you ask, remember to test the answers against this chapter's skeleton. Is it describing principles, or a particular product? Does it distinguish between what "has been proven" and what is "common in practice"? Asking these follow-up questions is itself the start of using agents to learn about agents.

## 8.6.4 Summary and Transition

At this point, we have climbed eight of the nine rungs of the cognitive ladder. We started from a concrete task, took it apart into components, saw their functions, connections, and flows, understood the mechanisms and key technologies, and finally traced everything back to principles. The final chapter adds no new components. Instead, it hands the learning back to you, the reader. You will use AI to read every step of an AI's log and build an agent of your own that does just one small thing. You will also follow a ladder of questions that moves from simple to deep, and continue to build out the skeleton of this book.
