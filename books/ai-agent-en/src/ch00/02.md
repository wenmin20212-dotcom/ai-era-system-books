# 0.2 A Cognitive Skeleton Map of AI Agents

Suppose you say this to an AI assistant right now: "Take the three months of expense reports in this folder, organize them into a single spreadsheet, total them by department, and write a short explanation." A few minutes later, a spreadsheet and a paragraph of text appear in front of you. What happened in between? Which files did it read first? Where did it make a mistake, and how did it notice and fix the error? Most people see only the beginning and the end. This book unfolds the invisible process in the middle so we can examine it. Before we unfold it, though, we need a map.

## 0.2.1 Why We Need a "Skeleton Map"

The first time you walk into a large hospital, the most useful thing isn't a detailed description of any one department. It's the floor directory by the entrance. It tells you how many floors the building has, what each floor handles, and where the stairs and elevators are. With it, you always know where you are, wherever you go.

Learning about agents works the same way. The field is full of terms: prompts, function calling, retrieval augmentation, context windows, sandboxes, multi-agent systems, tool protocols... If you try to memorize them one by one, they soon blur together. A more reliable approach is to build a skeleton first and learn where each term hangs on it. Then you can add the flesh. This book's skeleton has nine levels, which we call the "Nine-Level Cognitive Ladder."

## 0.2.2 The Nine-Level Ladder: From "What Is It?" to "Why Can It Do That?"

These nine levels are not arranged at random. They answer nine questions in sequence, and each question builds on the one before it. Let's stay with the expense-report task as our example:

| Level | Question Answered | In the Expense-Report Task | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Object | What is this thing? | An assistant that can read files, do the accounting, and write explanations on its own, not just chat with you | Chapter 1 |
| 2 Structure | What parts is it made of? | The model (the brain), instructions, tools (for reading files and working with spreadsheets), memory, the runtime environment, and the orchestration program | Chapter 2 |
| 3 Function | What does each part do? | Understand the receipts, break the work into steps, do the calculations, check the results, and ask you when something is unclear | Chapter 3 |
| 4 Connection | How does it interact with the outside world? | How it calls the spreadsheet tool, how it looks up the company's reimbursement policy, how it waits for you to click "Confirm" | Chapter 4 |
| 5 Flow | What flows through the system? | Tasks, messages, context, permissions and data, time and cost | Chapter 5 |
| 6 Mechanism | How exactly does it operate? | The loop of thinking a step, acting a step, and observing a step; how it recovers from errors | Chapter 6 |
| 7 Key Technologies | What makes it work in engineering terms? | Development frameworks, tool protocols, sandbox isolation, evaluation methods, safety governance | Chapter 7 |
| 8 Principles | What is the underlying logic? | Decision-making and utility, feedback control, search and planning, reinforcement learning, capability limits | Chapter 8 |
| 9 AI Collaboration | How can AI help you keep learning? | Have an agent explain its own execution logs, and build a small agent yourself | Chapter 9 |

The first three levels look at the agent at rest. They identify the object, take it apart, and explain what each part is for. The middle three levels look at the agent in motion: how the parts connect, how information flows, and how the loop keeps turning, cycle after cycle. The last three levels go deeper and further. They cover the technologies used to build it, the mathematical and theoretical reasons it works, and finally how to hand the initiative for learning back to the reader and to AI.

## 0.2.3 Three Threads Running Through the Book

The nine levels are vertical steps. Three horizontal threads also run through every level, appearing again and again. You can use them as three yardsticks to check whether you have really understood the material.

1. **The perceive–think–act–correct loop.** The biggest difference between an agent and an ordinary chatbot is that an agent takes action and then looks at the results of that action. Suppose it notices that it misread the amount on one receipt and reads the receipt again. That small act of going back is the loop at work. Chapter 3 covers its function, Chapter 6 covers its mechanism, and Chapter 8 explains its principles through the cybernetic concept of feedback.

2. **The balance between capability and limits.** The more an agent can do, the more pressing the question becomes: what is it allowed to do? Reading expense reports can be left to the agent. Deleting the original files or sending data outside should require asking you first. Permissions, human confirmation, sandboxes (a technique that confines a program to an isolated environment while it runs), and governance all belong to this thread.

3. **Cost awareness.** Every step of reasoning and every call takes time and consumes tokens. A token is the basic unit of measurement for the text a model processes; see Volume 18, *Large Language Models*, for details. The discussion of "cost flow" in Chapter 5 takes up a specific question: why an agent sometimes does better when it thinks fewer steps.

## 0.2.4 How to Use This Map

There are three ways to use this map.

**Read it in order.** This works best for readers encountering agents systematically for the first time, especially middle school students, high school students, university students, and newcomers to the field. Each level lays the foundation for the next. By the time you reach decision theory in Chapter 8, you will find that all the earlier examples fit into it.

**Look things up by question.** This works best for professionals and managers who already use AI assistants or coding agents at work. If you are worried about whether an agent might delete files by mistake, go straight to Section 4 of Chapter 4 and Sections 3 and 5 of Chapter 7. If you want to know why an agent keeps getting slower and more expensive, start with Chapter 5.

**Use it alongside a real product.** The last section of each chapter includes a hands-on exploration task. You can compare the skeleton against any agent product you are currently using. Where is its list of tools? Can you expand its execution logs? When does it stop and ask for your confirmation? The skeleton in this book is stable, while the products in your hands keep changing from one generation to the next. Using an unchanging skeleton to understand changing products is exactly the "systems X-ray vision" this book aims to give you.

This map deliberately leaves out two topics: how large models are trained and how they compute internally, and the data centers that keep them running. This book touches on them only briefly when needed. For full details, see Volume 18, *Large Language Models*, and Volume 17, *Artificial Intelligence Systems*. This volume keeps its focus on a single question: with a model at its core, how does a system complete multi-step tasks on its own?

With the map in hand, a new question follows. How can this skeleton on paper be combined with an AI that can talk and take action, so that it helps you learn faster and more deeply? That is the subject of the next section.
