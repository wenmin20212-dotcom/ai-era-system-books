# 0.1 Why This Book Differs from Every Other Agent Book You Have Read

Picture an afternoon like this: you open an AI assistant on your computer, and instead of asking it "What is inflation?" as you usually would, you hand it a job: "Take the thirty expense reports in this folder and organize them into a single table, totaled by department, and flag any whose amounts don't add up." A few minutes later, lines of activity scroll across the screen: it opens the files, reads their contents, writes a short program, hits an error, fixes one spot, runs it again, and finally hands you a spreadsheet, along with a note: "Two of the receipts have unclear dates. Please confirm them."

You sense that something is different. It isn't just "talking"; it is "doing." This kind of thing has a name: an AI agent. As this book is being written, such systems have already appeared in programming tools, office software, browser extensions, and enterprise automation workflows, and their names and capabilities change almost every quarter. Precisely because things change so fast, we need a way of seeing them that won't quickly go out of date. This section first explains how this book intends to look at them.

## 0.1.1 Three Common Approaches and Their Blind Spots

Material on agents falls roughly into three categories. Each has its value, and each has things it cannot see.

| Approach | Typical Content | Strengths | What It Tends to Miss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product tutorial | How to install and use a particular tool, plus tips and tricks | Quick to pick up, immediately usable | Goes stale as soon as the product updates; doesn't explain why it fails |
| Frontier commentary | Which industries agents will transform, which jobs they will replace | Broad perspective | Stays at the level of imagination and judgment; can't see how things work inside |
| Technical development | A framework's APIs, code samples, deployment methods | Precise and actionable | Requires programming background; easy to see the parts but miss the whole |

This book belongs to none of these categories. It won't show you where the buttons are in a particular product, it won't predict which professions will disappear, and it doesn't require you to know how to code. What it sets out to do is treat the agent as a **system**: to see clearly which parts it is made of, how those parts connect, how information and actions flow through it, and which relatively stable principles lie behind it.

## 0.1.2 Anchored in a Real Task

The greatest danger in explaining a system is leaving it floating in the abstract. So from beginning to end, this book keeps returning to one concrete anchor: **a task you yourself hand to an agent**. It might be researching a topic and writing a summary, organizing a spreadsheet, or modifying a piece of code.

Viewed through the lens of "seeing through the system," the expense-report example above breaks down into at least the following questions:

- How does it "see" the contents of the files? This is perception.
- Why does it read the files first, then write a program, and only then total things up? This is planning.
- When it runs the program, where does the program actually run? Could it touch anything else on your computer? This is the runtime environment and the sandbox.
- After an error, how does it know which spot to fix? This is feedback and error correction.
- Why does it stop to ask you about the dates? This is permissions and human confirmation.
- What did it "spend" during those few minutes? This is cost.

You will find that a whole structure lies hidden behind this one small task. Each chapter of this book revisits this same task from a different angle. With this anchor in place, abstract concepts have somewhere to land: when you read about the "context window," you can picture why, by the twentieth expense report, it might forget the format of the first; when you read about "prompt injection," you can picture what would happen if one of the receipts contained the hidden line "Ignore all previous instructions."

## 0.1.3 The Nine-Level Cognitive Ladder: From "What Is This?" to "Why Can It Work This Way?"

To give understanding an order, this book unfolds along a nine-level ladder:

1. **Object**: what kind of thing this is, and how it differs from chatbots and automation scripts;
2. **Structure**: it is composed of a model, instructions, tools, memory, a runtime environment, and an orchestrator;
3. **Function**: what each part is responsible for—perception, planning, execution, reflection, and collaboration with humans;
4. **Connection**: how it hooks up to tools, knowledge bases, other agents, and people;
5. **Flow**: how tasks, messages, context, permissions, data, and costs move through it;
6. **Mechanism**: the reason–act loop, task decomposition, tool calling, memory management, and failure recovery;
7. **Key technologies**: frameworks, protocols, sandboxes, evaluation, and safety governance;
8. **Principles**: decision theory, cybernetics, search and planning, reinforcement learning, and computability;
9. **AI collaboration**: using agents to keep learning about agents.

The first few levels answer "what is it, and what is it made of"; the middle levels answer "how does it run"; and the last few answer "why can it work this way, and what can't it do." For example, Chapter 8 will show that an agent's habit of "try something, look at the result, then adjust" descends directly from the feedback loops of cybernetics developed decades ago; and why it cannot guarantee getting things right every time can likewise be traced to the theories of uncertainty and computability. Principles don't change when products are replaced by new generations, and they are what this book most hopes to leave you with.

## 0.1.4 Division of Labor with the Two Preceding Volumes

This book is the nineteenth volume in the series *Foundational Readings on Systems Knowledge for the AI Era*. Volume 17, *Artificial Intelligence Systems*, covers the overall landscape of AI as a system, and Volume 18, *Large Language Models*, covers how the models themselves are trained and run inference. This volume uses the large language model as the agent's "brain" and focuses on the system built around that brain—one that can autonomously complete multi-step tasks. When we touch on the internal workings of models or on data centers, we will cover them only briefly and point you to the two preceding volumes.

This division of labor is also a reminder: an agent's performance doesn't depend solely on how powerful its model is. The same model, paired with different instructions, tools, permissions, and checking mechanisms, can produce very different results. Many problems that look like "the model isn't smart enough" actually originate in other parts of the system.

## 0.1.5 Book, Real-World Tool, and AI: Reading with All Three Together

This book adopts a "book + real-world tool + AI" approach to learning. The book provides a stable skeleton of knowledge; the agent tool you are using right now serves as a real-world anchor that you can open at any moment to verify what you read; and AI takes on the job of elaborating on details the book doesn't develop. Every chapter ends with hands-on exploration tasks and questions you can take straight to an AI.

In other words, as you read this book, you can also have an agent explain agents to you—and then use the book's skeleton to check whether what it says is right.
