# 0.3 The Paper Skeleton in Dynamic Symbiosis with AI: Using Agents to Learn About Agents

Suppose you have just asked a coding assistant to fix a bug in a spreadsheet script. A long stream of records scrolls across the screen. First it read the file, then it ran the program once. After seeing an error, it changed two lines of code, ran the program again, and finally told you "Fixed." You probably understand the outcome. You may not be able to say why the intermediate steps were arranged that way, or on what basis it judged the bug "fixed."

At this moment you actually have three things at hand: a book about how agents are structured, a real agent that has just finished a job, and an AI you can question at any time. This section is about how to use these three together.

## 0.3.1 Three Things, Each with Its Own Job

This series adopts a "book + real object + AI" learning paradigm. The three play different roles, and none can replace the others.

| Role | What it corresponds to in this book | What it is good at | What it is not good at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Book (paper skeleton) | The nine-level cognitive ladder, plus each chapter's structural diagrams and comparison tables | Stable and systematic; tells you what to ask | Cannot keep up with the latest changes in every product |
| Real object (anchor) | A task you personally hand to an agent, along with its run log | Real and concrete; can verify whether a claim holds | Only shows phenomena; does not explain principles on its own |
| AI (expander) | A chat assistant, or the agent itself | Answers on demand; can expand on details at your level | Can be wrong; needs the skeleton and the real object to check it |

The book is the map, the real object is the road beneath your feet, and the AI is a guide you can ask for directions at any time. With only a map, you get armchair theorizing. With only the road, you easily get lost. And if you only listen to the guide, you may be led astray. When the three check one another, learning has solid ground to stand on.

## 0.3.2 Why "Using Agents to Learn About Agents"

When you study other systems, the object of study and the learning tool are usually separate. You cannot ask an engine to explain itself while studying engines, but you can do this when studying agents. There are two reasons.

First, most of an agent's work leaves a written record. Many agent products display each step's "thought–action–result" in the interface or in logs. How much they show varies from product to product, and practices were still changing at the time of writing. These records are ready-made observational material, and Section 9.1 of Chapter 9 will teach you specifically how to read them.

Second, the core of an agent is a Large Language Model (LLM), which is itself good at explaining text. You can hand a run log to an AI and have it explain step by step, for example "why this tool was called at this step" or "what this error means." Here the object of study and the explainer coincide.

This convenience comes with a trap. When an AI explains its own behavior, what it says is not necessarily what actually happened internally. It may instead be a plausible-sounding after-the-fact account. That is why the book's skeleton must be in place first. You need to know that an agent is made up of a model, instructions, tools, memory, a runtime environment, and an orchestration program (Chapter 2). Then you can judge whether the AI's explanation has left out a layer, or attributed work done by the orchestration program to the model.

## 0.3.3 How the Skeleton Guides Your Questions

Without a skeleton, people tend to ask broad questions like "How is it so smart?" or "Will it make mistakes?", and the answers they get are usually just as vague. With the nine-level ladder, questions can land on specific levels. Take the bug-fixing task from the opening as an example:

- **Structural level**: In this task, which parts were done by the model, and which by external tools?
- **Flow level**: In the run log, what information entered the model's context? How did the error message get back to the model?
- **Mechanism level**: When it ran the program a second time, which step of the reasoning–action loop was that?
- **Principle level**: How does "run once, look at the result, then revise" relate to the feedback loop in cybernetics?

Each question points to a chapter in the book, and each answer can be taken back to your real object and tested. This is what we mean by "the skeleton provides the frame, the AI fills in the flesh, and the real object checks the goods."

## 0.3.4 A Small Loop You Can Follow

Turned into actions, the ideas above come down to roughly four steps:

1. **Do**: Give the agent a small, real task, such as tidying up a contact-list spreadsheet, and keep its run log.
2. **Match**: Turn to the relevant structural diagram in the book and find where each component appears in the log.
3. **Ask**: Pick a spot that doesn't match up or that you don't understand. Question the AI about it level by level according to the skeleton, and ask it to point to its evidence.
4. **Verify**: Take the AI's explanation back to the real object and test it. For instance, tweak the task description, run it again, and see whether the behavior changes the way it said it would.

These four steps are themselves a "perception–action–feedback" loop, the same structure as the way an agent works. While you learn about the agent, you are also learning by its method.

## 0.3.5 A Few Ground Rules

- **Check the facts**: For any product features, versions, or numbers the AI gives you, treat the official documentation as authoritative.
- **Isolate before you experiment**: Use unimportant files and accounts for experiments, and don't let the agent touch important data. Chapter 7 explains why in detail when it covers sandboxes and permissions.
- **Drill down one level at a time**: Pursue only one level per round of questioning, and get it clear before moving on, so the answers don't scatter the further you go.
- **Record your own questions**: The exploration tasks and question lists at the end of each chapter can serve as a starting point, but your own questions make a better destination.
