# 9.4 Chapter Skeleton Summary and Hands-On Exploration Tasks

By now, the task you have handed to an agent might be asking it to look up some information, tidy up a spreadsheet, or modify a piece of code. Since the prologue, we have kept circling around that task: first recognizing what an agent is, then taking it apart into its components to see what each part does, how the parts connect, what flows through them, what mechanisms keep them running, which key technologies they rely on, and what principles lie behind them. Chapter 9 changes direction: instead of relying on the book alone to explain, it lets readers carry this skeleton with them and ask AI to keep expanding on it. This section first reviews the chapter's key points, then distills the whole book into a method you can use again and again.

## 9.4.1 Chapter Skeleton Review

Chapter 9 covered three actions for "using agents to learn about agents":

- **Reading the logs** (9.1): Each step an agent takes usually leaves a run log, including the instructions it received, its reasoning notes, the tool calls it made, the results the tools returned, and its final answer. Annotating a log line by line as "goal—plan—action—observation—correction" lets you see the reason–act loop described in Chapter 6. Repeated calls, truncated context, and pauses waiting for human confirmation in the log correspond, respectively, to failure modes, context flow, and permission flow.
- **Building a small agent** (9.2): Have it do just one small thing, such as "read a bookkeeping spreadsheet and use a calculator to compute the total," and give it read-only permissions only. A minimal agent needs just four things: an instruction describing the task, one or two clearly defined tools, a loop program, and a stopping condition. Once you have built one yourself, you will understand that a so-called "framework" mainly helps you manage this loop, tool registration, and context.
- **Three levels of questioning** (9.3): The same question can be asked at three depths. The first level asks "what is it and what does it do," which suits beginners building intuition; the second asks "how does it do it and why is it designed this way," which corresponds to structure and mechanisms; the third asks "where are the boundaries, how can it be verified, and who is responsible when something goes wrong," which corresponds to engineering implementation, evaluation, and governance. The higher you climb, the more you need to verify AI's answers yourself.

These three actions share a common premise: **AI's explanations can be wrong too**. When reading logs, rely on what the tools actually returned rather than on the model's own account; when building experiments, use a small task you can check independently; when asking questions, press for the basis of the answer and verify it against official documentation or runnable results.

## 9.4.2 A Final Review of the Whole Book's Skeleton

Compressing the nine-level cognitive ladder into one table gives you something to check any agent product against at any time:

| Level | Core question to ask | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Is it an agent? How does it differ from a chatbot or a script? | Chapter 1 |
| Structure | What are the model, instructions, tools, memory, runtime environment, and orchestration program? | Chapter 2 |
| Function | Who handles perception, planning, execution, reflection, and collaboration with humans? | Chapter 3 |
| Connection | How does it connect to tools, knowledge, other agents, and people? | Chapter 4 |
| Flow | How do tasks, messages, context, permissions and data, and costs flow? | Chapter 5 |
| Mechanism | How does the loop run? How does it recover from errors? | Chapter 6 |
| Key technologies | Which frameworks, protocols, sandboxes, and evaluations does it use? | Chapter 7 |
| Principles | What decision, feedback, search, or learning ideas lie behind it? What can it not do? | Chapter 8 |
| AI collaboration | How can AI help you keep expanding each of the levels above? | Chapter 9 |

Products get updated and names change; the frameworks and protocols popular at the time of writing may be replaced within a few years, but the questions in this table are unlikely to go out of date. Faced with a new product, ask the questions row by row, and you can usually see its rough construction and where its risks lie in a fairly short time.

## 9.4.3 Hands-On Exploration Tasks

1. **Annotate a real log**: Pick an agent tool you use often that can display its execution process—for example, at the time of writing some coding assistants show each tool call step by step—and give it a small task. Copy down the process, mark goals, plans, actions, observations, and corrections in five different colors, count how many times the loop ran, and identify which round was correcting an error.
2. **Draw a permission map**: List which files this tool can read, whether it can access the internet, whether it can run commands, and which operations trigger a confirmation prompt. Compare against the permission flow in Chapter 5 to judge whether there is anything "it can do but you would rather it didn't," and tighten one setting.
3. **Build and modify a minimal agent**: Complete the small bookkeeping-assistant experiment following the approach in 9.2, then change just one variable—for example, remove the "at most 10 steps" limit, or deliberately make the tool return an error message—and observe whether it repeats endlessly, drifts off course, or manages to recover. Record what happens, then compare it with the analysis in Section 6.5.
4. **Keep a cost ledger**: For the same task, give instructions in two ways—"detailed instructions all at once" and "just one vague sentence stating the goal"—and compare the time taken, the number of conversation turns, and the usage shown in the interface. Experience for yourself what Chapter 5 means by "clear instructions save costs."
5. **One-page retelling**: Without looking at the book, use one page to explain to a friend unfamiliar with agents "how it gets things done." Wherever you can't explain clearly is where you should go back to the book or ask AI next.

## 9.4.4 Questions You Can Ask AI Directly

- "Here is an agent's run log (log attached). Please annotate it step by step as 'goal—plan—action—observation—correction,' and point out which steps base their judgments on tool returns and which are merely the model's guesses."
- "I want an agent to automatically process attachments in my work emails. From the four angles of permissions, data flow, prompt injection, and human confirmation, list the risks most in need of guarding against and the simplest protective measure for each, and indicate which suggestions I need to verify in official documentation."
- "Using the three perspectives of decision theory, cybernetic feedback, and search-based planning, explain why a coding agent automatically runs tests after modifying code, and then explain what each of these three perspectives fails to explain."

## 9.4.5 Closing: The Skeleton Is in the Book, the Unfolding Is in Your Hands

What this book offers is a skeleton: nine levels, a set of recurring questions, and an attitude that neither blindly trusts nor fears. An agent is not a mysterious "digital employee," nor an all-purpose assistant you can leave unattended. It is a machine made of a model, instructions, tools, memory, and programs, acting in a loop within an environment—its capabilities are real, and so are its limits. Next, hand it your next task, and open your "system X-ray vision" as you do: watch it think, watch it act, watch it err, watch it correct itself. Every step you come to understand will become knowledge of your own.
