# 1.4 From Expert Systems to LLM Agents: The Evolution of Agents

In the previous section we drew the boundaries of an agent: the model, the program, the environment, and the human. The agent at your fingertips might be an assistant that looks things up and tidies spreadsheets for you, or a coding agent that can edit files and run tests on its own inside a code repository. These look like something new that has appeared only in the past few years. Yet the idea of "letting machines judge and act for themselves" has been around for decades. Understanding the road it has traveled shows where today's agents are strong and where they are weak, and reveals which structures have carried through all along.

## 1.4.1 First Stop: Expert Systems—Writing Expert Experience as Rules

In the 1970s and 1980s, one of the most prominent applications in artificial intelligence research was the expert system. The idea was simple: ask domain experts to articulate their judgment and experience, then have engineers encode that experience as a large number of "if… then…" rules stored in a knowledge base. A separate inference engine applied these rules step by step to derive conclusions.

A classic textbook example is MYCIN, developed at Stanford University in the 1970s. Based on a patient's symptoms and lab results, it inferred likely bacterial infections and recommended treatments. Another frequently cited example is XCON, a system used by DEC to configure computer orders.

Expert systems had two strengths. First, their reasoning was transparent: every conclusion could be traced back to specific rules. Second, they performed reliably within the scope their rules covered. Their problems were just as clear:

- **Knowledge acquisition is hard**: Much expert experience is intuition that is "hard to put into words," and turning it into rules is slow and laborious. This came to be known as the "knowledge acquisition bottleneck."
- **Brittleness**: When a situation arose that the rules did not cover, the system often simply failed rather than adapting.
- **Maintenance is hard**: As the number of rules grew, conflicts and gaps among them became difficult to track down.

Seen from today, expert systems already had the paired structure of "knowledge base + reasoning," but they could neither fetch new information on their own nor take action themselves.

## 1.4.2 Second Stop: Rational Agents—A Clear Definition of "Agent"

By the 1990s, the "agent" had become a central concept in AI textbooks. The textbook *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach* by Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig framed the subject of AI as the "rational agent": one that perceives its environment through sensors, acts upon it through actuators, and chooses the actions expected to maximize its performance.

The value of this definition is that it is independent of any particular technology. Robot vacuums, chess programs, and web crawlers can all be analyzed within the same framework. In the same period, researchers developed directions such as multi-agent systems and software agents, studying how multiple programs communicate, negotiate, and divide labor. The decision theory and control theory covered in Chapter 8 of this book form the theoretical foundation of this framework.

Agents of this era were usually "specialists." A chess program could only play chess; a navigation program could only plan routes. Their ways of perceiving and their available actions were designed in advance by engineers, and switching to a new task meant developing a new system.

## 1.4.3 Third Stop: Learning Agents—Gaining Skills Through Trial and Error

In the 21st century, machine learning—deep learning in particular—advanced rapidly, and agents increasingly acquired their abilities from data and training. Reinforcement learning (RL) lets an agent learn to make decisions through repeated trial and error: "act—receive a reward or penalty—adjust the strategy." In 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated a top professional Go player, the best-known achievement of this line of work.

Learning agents no longer relied entirely on hand-written rules, and their performance in complex environments improved dramatically. But most remained confined to a single domain and required extensive training. A system that plays Go cannot simply be repurposed to help you fill out an expense report.

## 1.4.4 Fourth Stop: LLM Agents—Language as a Universal Interface

In recent years, the arrival of large language models (LLMs) brought a key shift: models can understand and generate natural language, possess a degree of general reasoning ability, and can produce structured text on request. Researchers and engineers soon found that if the model outputs not just an answer but "which tool to call and with what arguments," and an external program actually executes the call and sends the result back to the model, the model can complete multi-step tasks one step at a time.

Work such as ReAct, published around 2022, systematized the practice of interleaving "reasoning" and "acting." Afterward, companies began offering function calling in their products, and the open-source community produced a variety of agent frameworks. As of this writing, forms such as coding agents, browser-operating agents, and office-assistant agents have all entered everyday use. Products in this space change quickly, so specific features should be checked against each vendor's documentation at the time. For how models work internally, see Volume 18 of this series, *Large Language Models*.

## 1.4.5 The Four Stages Compared

| Stage | Source of capability | How new knowledge is acquired | How it acts | Main limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expert systems | Hand-written rules | Manually adding rules | Mostly just outputs conclusions | Brittle, hard to maintain |
| Rational agent framework | Perception and decision programs designed by engineers | Collected as designed | Predefined actions | Each task developed separately |
| Learning agents | Training on data | Retraining | Acts within a specific environment | Narrow domain, high training cost |
| LLM agents | Large-scale pretraining plus instructions | Retrieval, reading files, calling tools | Acts on real software environments through tools | Makes mistakes; needs permission controls and human oversight |

## 1.4.6 What Has Changed, and What Hasn't

The table shows that the biggest change is in "generality." Today's agents take tasks in natural language, and the same model, equipped with different tools, can look up information or modify code.

Some things have not changed. The loop of "perceive—decide—act—get feedback" has never changed. The principle that "knowledge must work together with reasoning" was established in the expert-system era and lives on today in the form of retrieval and knowledge bases. The same goes for "actions must have boundaries": in the past these were hard-coded into programs; today they rely on sandboxes, permissions, and human confirmation. The brittleness of expert systems has not disappeared either; it has only changed form. LLM agents also make mistakes in unfamiliar situations—and often appear quite confident when they do.

So LLM agents can be understood as a combined upgrade of decades of agent research: a large model serves as a general-purpose "brain," connected to the long-mature mechanisms of tool calling, retrieval, and control.
