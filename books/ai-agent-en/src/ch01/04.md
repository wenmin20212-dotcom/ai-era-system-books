# 1.1 Starting from a Real Task: How an Agent Gets Things Done

It's Monday morning, and you type one sentence to the AI assistant on your computer: "Merge last month's store sales spreadsheets from our three cities, summarize them by city, calculate the month-over-month growth, and write a 200-word summary." In the past, you would have opened the three files yourself, copied and pasted, written formulas, checked the numbers, and then written the summary. All told, that could take half an hour. Now you watch the screen. The assistant first says, "Let me look at the structure of these three files." Then it opens them and finds that the "Date" column in one spreadsheet is formatted differently from the other two. So it standardizes the format first, then merges, summarizes, and calculates. Finally, it hands you a table and a paragraph of explanation, adding one note: "Two rows of data for City B have blank amounts. I didn't fill them in. I've highlighted them in yellow in the table. Please confirm how you'd like to handle them."

This scene is the anchor of this book. Suppose instead you ask "How do you calculate month-over-month growth?" and get an explanation back. That is a different kind of thing altogether. The second case is answering a question. The first is getting a job done. This book is about AI systems that can break a task into steps on their own, take action, check the results, and stop to ask you when necessary. Such a system is called an **AI Agent**.

## 1.1.1 Slowing Down the Process: The Five Stages of a Task

If we replay those few minutes in slow motion, the process falls roughly into five stages:

| Stage | What the agent is doing | How it shows up in this example |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding the goal | Working out what result you want | A summary table plus a short write-up, with month-over-month growth as the key metric |
| Drafting the steps | Breaking the goal into small, executable steps | Read files → standardize format → merge → summarize → calculate → write the summary |
| Taking action | Calling tools to actually carry out the work | Opening files, running a calculation program, generating a new spreadsheet |
| Observing and checking | Seeing whether the results are correct | Discovering the inconsistent date format and the blank values |
| Delivering and confirming | Handing the results to a person and pointing out uncertainties | Highlighting the blank values and asking you how to handle them |

These five stages are not completed strictly in a single pass. In practice, an agent usually works by "taking a step, looking at the result, and then deciding the next step." The original plan contained no "standardize format" step. The agent added it on the fly after it observed the date problem during execution. This ability to adjust its actions based on results is what most clearly separates an agent from a program that generates an answer in one shot. Chapter 6 covers this "reasoning–action loop" in detail.

## 1.1.2 It Is Not One Thing, but a Set of Parts Working Together

All you see is a chat window, but behind it at least several components are working together:

- **Large Language Model (LLM)**: Understands what you say, figures out what to do next, and writes the explanatory text. It is the "brain," but on its own it can only output text. It cannot open a file directly. For how models work internally, see Volume 18 of this series, *Large Language Models*.
- **Instructions**: Guidance the system writes for the model in advance. For example: "You are a data assistant. These are the tools you can use. When you encounter uncertain data, ask the user first."
- **Tools**: Specific capabilities such as reading files, running code, and searching the web. The model says, "I want to call the file-reading tool on the file named such-and-such," and an external program actually carries it out.
- **Memory**: It remembers what the three spreadsheets it already looked at contain and how far it has progressed.
- **Runtime environment and orchestration program**: A program that "relays messages and runs errands." It turns the model's decisions into real operations and sends the results back to the model. The operations usually take place in a restricted environment, so the agent cannot accidentally modify your other files.

An analogy may help. The model is like a capable consultant who has no hands. The tools are a row of assistants he can direct. The orchestration program is the person passing notes back and forth between the consultant and the assistants. The runtime environment is the office where they all work. Take away any one of them, and the job doesn't get done. Chapter 2 takes these components apart one by one.

## 1.1.3 Why It Stops to Ask You

Perhaps the most noteworthy part of the example is that final line: "Please confirm." A blank value could be filled with zero or with an average, or the whole row could be deleted. Each choice changes the summary results. Which one is right depends on the realities of the business, and only you know those. A well-designed agent should stop at points like this, where making a decision on your behalf has consequences. It should not quietly pick one option.

This points to a thread that runs through the whole book: an agent can act on its own, but its actions need boundaries. You can safely let it read files freely. Operations that are hard to undo, such as deleting files, sending emails, or making payments, usually need your approval. At the time of writing, most mainstream coding agents and office assistants offer some form of "confirm before acting" setting. The details vary from product to product, but the idea is the same: build permissions and confirmation into the system rather than hoping the model never makes a mistake. Chapters 4 and 7 discuss this in detail.

## 1.1.4 It Makes Mistakes, and Its Mistakes Follow Patterns

The same agent can also botch the same task. It might calculate year-over-year growth instead of month-over-month. It might misread a header, or get stuck retrying a step over and over. It might also treat a blank value as zero and fold it into the calculation without telling you. These errors are not mysterious. Most fall into a few categories: misunderstanding, skipped steps, misused tools, and insufficient checking. Once you know the mistakes follow patterns, you know where to check its work. You might spot-check a few numbers or look over its operation log. Many agent products display which tool was called at each step and what result came back. That record is your best material for understanding and supervising the agent, and Chapter 9 teaches you how to read it.

## 1.1.5 Summary: Keep This Task in Mind as You Read On

Let's return to that spreadsheet from Monday morning. Getting it done took three things. The first was a repeated cycle of "understand, plan, execute, check, confirm." The second was the cooperation of the model, instructions, tools, memory, environment, and orchestration program. The third was human oversight at the critical points. Every following chapter of this book comes back to tasks like this one and digs a layer deeper each time. What is it made of? What does each part do? How does it connect to the outside world? What flows through it? What mechanisms make it run? What key technologies does it use? What principles lie behind it?

Try this now: pick a small task you actually need to do, such as organizing a list of materials or revising a piece of code. Hand it to the AI assistant you have on hand and watch what it does at each step.
