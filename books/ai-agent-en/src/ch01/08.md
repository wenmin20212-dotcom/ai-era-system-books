# 1.5 Chapter Skeleton Summary and Hands-On Exploration Tasks

Suppose you have just handed a small task to an AI agent, something like "turn the dozen or so meeting notes in this folder into a single to-do table." A few minutes later, the table appears. The four sections of this chapter set out to answer what happened during those few minutes, and what this "assistant that takes action on its own" really is. Below, we gather the chapter's conceptual skeleton into a few key points, followed by several exploration tasks you can try on your own computer or phone, and some questions you can put directly to an AI.

## 1.5.1 Chapter Skeleton Review

This chapter is the first rung of the nine-level cognitive ladder: "getting to know the object." It does not take anything apart. It does just one thing: it gives a clear view of the object's overall outline, how it differs from similar things, and where it came from.

## 1.5.2 How an Agent Gets Things Done (1.1)

- What an agent receives is a **goal**, not a request for a ready-made answer.
- It works in a loop: understand the task, decide the next step, call a tool to carry it out, observe the result, then decide the next step again. It repeats this until it judges the task complete or decides it needs help.
- What it ultimately delivers is a **changed state of the world**, such as a generated table, a revised piece of code, or a drafted email, not merely a passage of text.

### 1.5.2.1 How It Differs from Chatbots and Automation Scripts (1.2)

| Dimension | Chatbot | Automation Script | AI Agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input | A question | Data in a fixed format | A goal |
| Who decides the steps | Essentially one question, one answer | Hard-coded in advance by a programmer | Decided by the model at runtime |
| Can it take action? | Usually outputs only text | Yes, but only predefined actions | Yes, through tool calls |
| When the unexpected happens | The user asks follow-up questions | Usually throws an error and stops | Can try to adjust, but may also go off track |

The point to remember is that these three are not ranked from lower to higher. They represent **a trade-off between flexibility and predictability**. Well-defined, repetitive work is handled more reliably by a script. Only open-ended, changeable work calls for an agent.

**3. The Boundaries of an Agent (1.3)** An agent system has at least four parties. The **model** understands and decides. The **program** orchestrates and executes. The **environment** is what gets read and changed (files, web pages, software). The **human** sets goals, grants permissions, and confirms critical operations. Much of what looks like "agent capability" actually comes from the program and its tools, not from the model alone. Likewise, many "agent mistakes" arise at the boundaries: permissions granted too broadly, incomplete information about the environment, or a human failing to check things at key points.

## 1.5.3 From Expert Systems to LLM-Based Agents (1.4)

- Early expert systems reasoned using rules written by hand. They were reliable but brittle, and they struggled with situations outside their rules.
- Classical artificial intelligence defined an "agent" as a system that perceives its environment and takes actions to achieve goals. This definition remains the theoretical foundation today, and Chapter 8 will return to it.
- Large language models (LLMs) let systems understand goals stated in natural language and generate plans of action. As a result, "a general-purpose decision core + interchangeable tools" had become the mainstream way of building agents at the time of writing.
- What changed is the decision core. What stayed the same is the skeleton of "perceive, decide, act, get feedback."

## 1.5.4 The Skeleton in One Sentence

> **An agent = a system that uses a model as its decision core, is driven by a program, acts on its environment through tools, and pursues goals in a loop under human authorization and oversight.**

The remaining eight chapters all unpack this sentence. Chapter 2 takes apart its components, Chapter 3 explains what each component does, Chapters 4 through 6 cover connections, flows, and mechanisms, and Chapters 7 and 8 cover technology and underlying principles.

## 1.5.5 Hands-On Exploration Tasks

The following tasks require no programming. Any AI assistant or agent product you have access to will do. At the time of writing, many mainstream AI assistants offer features such as web search, file analysis, or code execution. Check your product's documentation for the exact names and availability of these features.

1. **Tell "answering" apart from "acting"**: Ask the same AI assistant "What will the weather be like in Beijing tomorrow?" Then ask again after turning on web access or tool features. Compare the two responses, and watch for prompts such as "Searching…" or "Running code…" in the interface. These prompts are traces of tool calls.
2. **Record a multi-step task**: Give an agent a small task that takes at least three steps, for example "Read this spreadsheet, calculate the total for each month, then draw a bar chart." If the interface lets you expand the intermediate steps, note them one by one. What did it do first, what did it do next, and did it change course partway through?
3. **Identify the four-party boundaries**: For Task 2, draw a four-cell table and write down which parts belong to the model, the program, the environment, and the human. In particular, find the steps where you granted permissions or clicked "Confirm."
4. **Script or agent?**: List three small repetitive tasks from your work or studies. For each one, decide whether it is better suited to a fixed script, a spreadsheet formula, or an agent, and write down your reasons.
5. **Introduce a little surprise**: Deliberately provide a messily formatted file with missing values. Watch whether the agent handles it automatically, asks you questions, or produces wrong results without noticing. This previews "reflection and error correction" in Chapter 3.

## 1.5.6 Questions You Can Ask an AI Directly

- "Using the example of organizing meeting notes, explain step by step how an AI agent and an ordinary chatbot differ in how they handle the task, and indicate whether each step is performed by the model or by the program."
- "How do classical artificial intelligence textbooks define an 'agent'? In what ways is that definition the same as, and different from, today's agents built on large language models?"
- "For the task I just asked you to complete, which tools did you call, and what result did you see at each step? Please list them in order."

When you ask, it is worth following up with "Is there a reliable source for that?" to build a habit of verification. At this point, we have come to know the overall shape of the object. The next chapter opens up the casing to see what its "brain," "hands," and "memory" are.
