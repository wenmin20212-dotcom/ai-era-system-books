# 3.5 Collaborating with People: Asking, Confirming, and Handing Off

You ask an agent to tidy up a customer list and send a holiday greeting email to everyone on it. After organizing the spreadsheet, it pauses and displays a line of text: "3 email addresses on the list have an invalid format, and 2 customers have 'Do not contact for now' in their notes. Skip these 5 people and send to the rest? Please confirm." You glance at it and click "Confirm." A few minutes later, it reports: "Sent 128 emails, skipped 5. See the attachment for the full list."

The pause is brief, but it shows something people often overlook. A useful agent is not one that never interrupts you. It is one that knows when to ask, when to stop, and how to hand the results back to you once the work is done. The previous sections covered perception, planning, execution, and reflection, all of which happen inside the agent. This section turns to a function that faces outward: collaborating with humans.

## 3.5.1 Why Ask: Task Instructions Always Have Gaps

When people assign a task, they almost never spell out every condition. "Book me a high-speed train to Shanghai next week" says nothing about which day, what time, what seat class, or what the budget is. A human assistant would follow up with a question, and an agent should do the same.

An agent needs to ask questions for three main reasons:

- **Missing information**: A parameter required to complete the task has not been given, such as a date, a file location, or the set of recipients.
- **Ambiguity**: A sentence has more than one reasonable reading. "Sort the spreadsheet by time" could mean ascending or descending order, and "time" could mean either the creation time or the deadline.
- **Unclear preferences**: Several approaches are all correct, and the choice comes down to your taste, such as whether a report should be written as bullet points or as paragraphs.

Asking has its costs. Ask too much, and the user feels they might as well have done the job themselves. Ask too little, and the agent has to guess, and wrong guesses mean redoing the work. A sensible rule of thumb is this: if something can be reasonably inferred from context and a wrong guess would cost little, act on the inference and state in the results, "I assumed that…" If a wrong guess would cause real damage, ask first. Good questions usually come with options, such as "There's a suitable train next Tuesday morning and another next Wednesday afternoon. Which would you like?" That takes far less effort from the user than a bare "Which day do you want to leave?"

## 3.5.2 When to Stop: The Threshold for Human Confirmation

Asking questions makes the task clear. Confirmation exists to catch risk. In engineering, this kind of design is often called "Human-in-the-Loop" (HITL), meaning that a step requiring a human decision is built into key points in the workflow.

Two questions help determine which actions need confirmation: Can this step be undone? How wide is its impact?

| Action type | Examples | Reversible? | Common handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Read-only queries | Searching the web, reading files | No side effects | Usually executed directly |
| Local reversible changes | Editing text in a draft, creating temporary files | Easy to undo | Executed directly, reported afterward |
| Outbound actions | Sending emails or messages, submitting forms | Hard to undo | Confirmed before execution |
| Irreversible or high-value | Deleting data, making payments, changing production system settings | Very hard or impossible to undo | Confirmed every time, sometimes requiring approval from several people |

At the time of writing, many coding agents and office agents offer similar tiered settings. For example, before running a terminal command or modifying a file, the system asks for the user's permission, and the user can set some low-risk operations to "always allow." The exact options differ from one product to the next and change quickly, but the underlying principle is much the same: the harder an action is to undo and the wider its impact, the more the decision should rest with a human.

The confirmation request itself also needs to be clear. "Continue?" carries almost no information, so the user can only click blindly. A good confirmation request states three things: what is about to be done, which objects it affects, and what consequences it might have. The example at the start of this section works well because it lists how many entries were flagged and why, so you can decide within seconds.

## 3.5.3 How to Hand Off: Handoff Is the Last Step of the Task

Many people assume that a task ends once the agent completes its final action. In fact, one step remains: the handoff, which means returning the results, the process, and any open issues to the human. If the handoff is done poorly, you have to dig through files and logs yourself, and the time you saved earlier is spent all over again.

A proper handoff usually includes:

1. **Summary of results**: What was completed and where the outputs are.
2. **Key decisions and assumptions**: What judgments were made on your behalf along the way, and on what basis.
3. **Unfinished items and exceptions**: Which steps failed or were skipped, and why.
4. **Suggested next steps**: What you should review, or what could be done next.

The idea is the same as a nurse's shift handover at a hospital or an engineer's change notes: the person taking over was not there and has to rely on the handoff to understand what happened. A handoff can also happen in the middle of a task. When the agent gets stuck, for example because it needs a login verification code, lacks permissions, or has failed several attempts in a row, it should stop and pass the current situation and what it has already tried to a human. It should not keep spinning its wheels or look for a way around the restriction. Handing a problem over on its own initiative is a form of reliability in itself.

## 3.5.4 The Essence of Collaboration: Responsibility Stays with the Human

On the surface, asking, confirming, and handing off are matters of interaction design. Underneath, they are about dividing up responsibility and information. An agent's judgment can be wrong, the information it sees may be incomplete, and it may even be misled by malicious instructions hidden in a web page, a topic discussed in Chapter 7. For these reasons, the system needs to return decision-making authority to the human at key points.

Your relationship with an agent is therefore more like that of a client and an assistant than that of a person and a machine you can forget about once you press a button. The clearer the delegation, the fewer questions are needed. The more carefully you confirm, the smaller the risk. The more complete the handoff, the easier it is to check the results.

All five functions of an agent have now been introduced: perception lets it understand the world, planning lets it think through the steps, execution lets it actually act, reflection lets it catch its mistakes, and collaboration with humans lets it stop at the critical moments and defer to you.
