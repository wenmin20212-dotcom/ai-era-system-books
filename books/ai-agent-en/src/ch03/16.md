# 3.2 Planning: Breaking a Big Goal into Small Steps

Suppose you say to an agent: "Put together a table comparing the opening hours of five libraries in our city, and mark the one that opens earliest on weekends." That sentence contains a single goal, yet to accomplish it the agent must at least find a list of five libraries, look up each one's official opening hours, standardize the time format, fill in the table, compare the weekend hours, and finally state a conclusion. Once the agent receives the request, its first piece of substantive work is planning: turning a broad, vague goal into a sequence of small steps that can be carried out one at a time and checked for correctness.

The previous section, on "perception," dealt with "what has been understood." This section answers the question "how do I intend to do it?"

## 3.2.1 From One Sentence to a Checklist: What Planning Does

People rely on planning too. Before moving house, few people start by hauling the wardrobe; they usually make a list first: book a truck, pack, disassemble the furniture, move everything, return the keys to the old place. An agent's planning works in a similar way and mainly involves three things:

1. **Decomposition**: breaking the goal into subtasks, such as "find the list," "look up the hours," "build the table," and "draw a conclusion."
2. **Ordering**: determining sequence and dependencies. Without knowing which five libraries are involved, you can't look up their hours; until the table is filled in, you can't make the comparison.
3. **Choosing means**: matching each step with a suitable method, such as using a search tool to find the list, a web-reading tool to check official websites, and a spreadsheet tool to compile the results.

Once these three things are done, the agent usually ends up with a checklist. Some systems display the checklist directly to the user. At the time of writing, many coding agents and research assistants show a "to-do list" in their interface, checking off each item as it is completed. Other systems keep the plan internal, where the user cannot see it.

## 3.2.2 Two Basic Styles: Plan First, or Think as You Go

Agent planning broadly falls into two styles, which can be compared to two ways of traveling:

| Style | Analogy | Approach | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plan first, then execute | A package tour with the itinerary printed in advance | Lay out all the steps at the start, then carry them out one by one | Clear reasoning, easy for people to review, steps are less likely to be missed | When reality changes, the original plan may become entirely obsolete |
| Think as you go | Independent travel, deciding the next stop on arrival | Decide only the next step each time, then think about the one after once results come in | Flexible, able to absorb new information at any point | Prone to detours; in long tasks, may lose sight of the overall goal |

Most real systems combine the two: they draft a rough plan first, then revise it based on results during execution. For example, if the agent discovers on reaching the third library that it is closed on weekends, it needs to adjust how the "compare weekend hours" step is handled rather than mechanically filling in the table according to the original plan. This back-and-forth of "plan, execute, revise" is examined in detail at the level of mechanism in Section 6.2 of Chapter 6. For now, keep one point in mind: **planning is not a one-time action; it is continually updated as execution proceeds.**

## 3.2.3 How Fine Should the Breakdown Be?

The granularity of decomposition is where planning most tests one's judgment. If the steps are too coarse, say, just "research" and "make the table," each step is still too large, making errors likely during execution and hard to pinpoint when they occur. If the steps are too fine, listing "open the web page," "scroll down," and "copy a line of text" as separate steps, the plan becomes bloated, and having the model reread this list over and over consumes a great deal of context and many calls, a point we will return to in Chapter 5's discussion of cost flow.

A commonly used test is this: **each small step should be verifiable by a clear result.** "Find the Saturday opening time of such-and-such library" is a good step, because whether it was found, and whether it is correct, is obvious at a glance. "Learn about the libraries" is not a good step, because it has no clear criterion for completion. This test is also the precondition for the next section on "execution" and for Section 3.4 on "reflection and error correction": only when steps can be checked can errors be caught in time.

## 3.2.4 Where Planning Ability Comes From

An agent has no dedicated, standalone organ responsible for "drawing up plans." In today's mainstream agents built on large models, planning comes mainly from two sources:

- **The model's reasoning ability.** Large language models have learned, from vast amounts of human text, the common sense of "what steps something usually involves." Researchers have found that prompting a model to "think step by step" noticeably improves its performance on multi-step problems. In the published literature, this kind of approach is often called Chain of Thought (CoT). Why models are able to do this belongs to the internal workings of models; see Volume 18, *Large Language Models*.
- **Constraints from the orchestrator and instructions.** Developers can specify in the system prompt that the agent should "write out a plan before acting," or have the orchestrator split planning and execution into two phases, even assigning them to two separate model calls. The "instructions" and "orchestrator" discussed in Chapter 2 directly shape how an agent plans here.

It is important to stay clear-eyed: a plan produced by a model is not necessarily reliable. It may omit a key step, get the order wrong, or produce a plan that looks complete but cannot actually be carried out, for instance one that calls for a tool the agent simply does not have. That is why letting the user glance over the plan before execution is itself an important safety and quality design choice, a topic Section 3.5 will take up further.

## 3.2.5 Summary: Planning Is the Bridge Between Goals and Actions

Return to the library example. Without planning, the agent can only act blindly on a single sentence and may well draw a conclusion after checking just two libraries. With planning, it holds a roadmap: it knows what is still missing, what to do next, and when the job is done. Planning translates "what is wanted" into "what to do, in order," serving as the bridge that connects goals to actions.

Still, however well a plan is written, it exists only on paper. What actually makes things happen is the next stage: how the agent calls tools, writes code, and operates software to turn each item on the checklist into a real result. That is the subject of Section 3.3, "Execution."
