# 5.6 Chapter Skeleton Summary and Hands-On Exploration Tasks

Remember the task we handed the agent at the very start of this chapter? "Look up the public prices of a few competitors over the past three months and put them in a table." From the moment you press Enter to the moment a table appears on screen, the system never stops moving for an instant: the task gets broken apart, messages pass back and forth, the context swells and shrinks, permissions are granted one after another, and the numbers on the bill tick steadily upward. This chapter took a different vantage point. Instead of asking "What is it made of?", it asked "What flows through the system?" Below, we first gather this thread into a skeleton, and then invite you to trace these flows with your own hands.

## 5.6.1 Chapter Skeleton Review: Five Flow Lines

Picture a running agent as a small factory. Chapter 2 looked at what machines stand on the factory floor; this chapter looks at what runs through the pipes. The five flow lines each have their own role, yet they are all interconnected:

| Flow Line | What Flows | Key Question | Typical Symptoms When Things Go Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task flow (5.1) | Goal → subtasks → concrete actions | How does a single sentence become a sequence of executable steps? | Missed steps, repeated work, drifting from the original goal |
| Message flow (5.2) | Individual messages among the user, the model, and tools | Who said what to whom, and when? | Tool results not passed back, garbled formatting, stalled conversations |
| Context flow (5.3) | All the text that enters the model's "field of view" | What gets brought in, kept, and pushed out? | Forgetting earlier instructions, getting distracted by irrelevant content |
| Permission flow and data flow (5.4) | Read/write authorizations and sensitive data | Who can see what, who can change what, and where does the data go? | Unauthorized operations, data leaks, accidentally deleted files |
| Cost flow (5.5) | Time, tokens, number of calls | How much did each step cost, and was it worth it? | Slow runs, runaway costs, infinite loops |

A few key points are worth remembering:

- **Task flow is the main line; the other four are supports.** Every step the task advances generates messages, occupies context, invokes permissions, and consumes cost.
- **Message flow is the "visible flow."** Most of an agent's behavior ultimately leaves a record in the form of structured messages, which forms the basis for Chapter 9's "Reading Every Step of the Record."
- **The context window is a limited "workbench."** Information entering, staying, and being pushed out directly determines what the agent "remembers"; Chapter 6 will explain how summarization and compression come into play here.
- **Permission flow and data flow run in opposite directions, yet must be examined together.** Permissions are authorizations flowing from people to the agent, while data may flow from the system to the outside world; human confirmation is the gate placed where these two flows meet.
- **Cost flow converts all movement into a price.** Every extra step means extra time and tokens, so "can do" does not mean "worth doing."

To sum it up in one sentence: **The process by which an agent gets work done is the process of a task advancing through message pipelines, being handled within a limited context, executing within permission boundaries, and continuously consuming cost.** Chapter 6 will dig into the mechanisms behind these flows, answering "Why do they flow this way?"

## 5.6.2 Hands-On Exploration Tasks: Trace the Five Flows Yourself

All of the following tasks can be done with whatever AI assistant or coding agent you have on hand. At the time of writing, many mainstream products display tool-call processes or run logs in their interface, though the exact location and naming vary by product, so go by the version you are using. Do not experiment with real sensitive accounts or important files.

1. **Draw a task flow diagram.** Give the agent a multi-step task, such as "Check tomorrow's weather in three cities, compare them, and recommend one suitable for outdoor activities." Watch how it carries out the task, then use pen and paper to draw the decomposition tree from goal to each action, marking which steps it came up with on its own and which ones you proposed.

2. **Count the message round trips.** Repeat the previous task in an interface that shows tool calls, and record each message one by one: how many user messages, model replies, tool-call requests, and tool results there are. Arrange them chronologically into a timeline and see which step involved the longest wait.

3. **Run a "forgetting test."** At the start of a conversation, give a clear instruction (for example, "Round all numbers to one decimal place"), then have it process several fairly long documents in a row, and observe after how many turns this instruction starts being ignored. Consider: was it pushed out of the context, or diluted by new content?

4. **Take inventory of permissions.** Open the settings page of the AI tool you use most, and list what it can currently access, such as files, calendar, email, web pages, or the terminal. Label each item as "read-only," "writable," or "requires confirmation," then think about which of these you don't actually need to grant it.

5. **Estimate a cost.** Pick a task you often have an agent do, and record its total time and number of tool calls; if the platform shows token usage, note that down too. Then rewrite the instructions so the task description is clearer and its scope smaller, run it again, and compare the difference in cost between the two runs.

## 5.6.3 Questions You Can Ask AI Directly

- "I just had you complete a task. Please list every step you took, in chronological order: what information you received, which tool you called, what result you got, and why you decided to take the next step the way you did."
- "If our conversation keeps getting longer and exceeds what you can handle at once, how do you deal with the earlier content? Please give a concrete example of something that could go wrong as a result."
- "Suppose an agent can read and write files on my computer and access the internet. From the perspectives of permission flow and data flow respectively, list the three operations that most need human confirmation, and explain why."

Once you've asked, don't forget to check its answers against this chapter's skeleton: which statements match the actual flows you observed, and which are just vague generalities? If you can tell the difference, you have already begun to see through agents with a "flow" lens.
